**Direct answer:** A specification sheet and a certificate of analysis (COA) are two different documents that answer two different questions. The spec sheet says *what the supplier promises to deliver* — materials, botanical identity, form, particle size, moisture, burn performance, packaging. The COA says *what a test actually measured* — and only if it names the batch, the test method, and the issuing body. A buyer who checks the spec but not the COA is accepting a promise; a buyer who checks neither is buying blind.

**Key facts:** Botanical incense has no universal regulatory standard, so "COA" means whatever the issuer measured. The document is only as strong as three things: the batch link (does the COA cover *this* shipment?), the method (what was tested, and how?), and the issuer (can the result be verified against the issuing body?).

## Spec sheet: what it should contain

A usable spec sheet for botanical incense materials should pin down:

- **Botanical identity** — the Latin binomial (*Santalum album*, *Aquilaria* spp.), not just the trade name. "Sandalwood" is a family of species; "agarwood" is a condition, not a species.
- **Part and form** — wood, bark, resin, flower, or root; chips, powder, or whole pieces.
- **Particle size** — powder mesh or particle range, which drives burn behavior and blending.
- **Moisture content** — botanical material absorbs and loses moisture; the spec should state the target and tolerance.
- **Foreign matter / purity** — the acceptable limit on non-product material.
- **Origin** — region and, for high-value or regulated materials, the supply chain statement.
- **Burn performance** (for finished incense) — burn time per unit, ash behavior, smoke character.

**What a spec sheet is not:** it is not proof. It is the supplier's own written commitment — useful exactly to the extent that a later test can check it.

## COA: the three questions that decide whether it means anything

1. **Does it cover this batch?** The COA must carry a batch or lot number that links to the shipment. A generic COA with no batch link describes a *capability*, not *your goods*.
2. **What was tested, and how?** Each line should state the parameter (identity, purity, moisture, microbial load, residue), the result, the limit, and the method (e.g., TLC, GC-MS for identity/adulteration screening, gravimetric for moisture). "Passed" without a method is a statement of faith.
3. **Who issued it?** Third-party COAs are stronger than in-house ones — but only if the issuer exists, is accredited for the relevant scope, and the certificate can be verified. A real certificate has a number you can check.

## What a buyer can and cannot verify from documents alone

**Documents can verify:** species claims (against a credible identity test), moisture and purity numbers (if the method is stated), and — for regulated species like *Pterocarpus santalinus* or CITES-listed *Aquilaria* — the species-level trade documentation.

**Documents cannot verify:** aroma quality, grade, or aging. No standard COA measures "creamy," "complex," or "high grade." These are sensory and market judgments, which is why the sample remains the final arbiter — see the [samples program](/blog/samples-program/) and the [agarwood buying guide](/blog/how-to-buy-agarwood-for-incense-manufacturing/).

**The honest rule:** request the spec sheet before ordering, the batch-linked COA with the shipment, and check the certificate number against the issuer for high-value botanicals. If a supplier cannot produce a batch-linked document for a CITES-relevant or high-value material, treat the claim as unverified — not necessarily false, but unverified.

## Common gaps that matter in practice

- **"Natural" is not a tested parameter.** No COA proves a material is "natural" in the consumer sense; identity and purity tests screen for known adulterants, and the scope must be stated.
- **Species ambiguity.** A COA saying "*Santalum*" without the species is weaker than one saying "*Santalum album*". Ask for the binomial.
- **Re-test on arrival.** For volume orders, sampling your own re-test on arrival closes the gap between the paper and the goods — especially when the material crosses multiple hands.

## FAQ

**Is a COA required for incense materials?**
Not universally. Finished consumer goods face market-specific requirements (combustion safety, labeling, restricted substances); raw botanicals for B2B trade are usually a contractual matter. Regulated species carry mandatory species documentation regardless of contract. Confirm destination requirements before shipping — see [export considerations](/blog/incense-export-considerations/).

**How do I check a COA is real?**
Check the issuer's name and accreditation against its own registry, verify the certificate number, and confirm the batch number on the certificate matches the shipment. A document that cannot be traced back to an issuing body is a piece of paper.

**What should I do if a supplier won't provide a batch-linked COA?**
For commodity materials this may be a paperwork limitation; for high-value or regulated botanicals it is a serious red flag. At minimum, arrange your own arrival testing — or walk away. The cost of a failed shipment exceeds the cost of testing.
