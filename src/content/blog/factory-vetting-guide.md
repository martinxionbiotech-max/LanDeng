**Direct answer:** Vetting an incense manufacturer means verifying four layers before you commit: **legal identity**, **production capability**, **quality-control process**, and **documentation** (certifications and test reports). You verify these through questions, documents, references, and — where the order size justifies it — an on-site or third-party audit. The goal is not to eliminate risk but to know *which* risks you are accepting.

**Key facts:** This guide gives you the audit points, the QC program you should expect, and the certification and test documentation to request. It does not tell you that any specific factory holds a specific certification — that is precisely what you must verify per supplier. No certifications are implied for LanDeng or anyone else.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Vet in four layers: **identity → capability → QC → documentation.**
- A **QC program** is more important than any single certificate — ask how quality is checked, not just which badges hang on the wall.
- Request **test reports and certificates per order**, not a generic statement.
- **Certifications are claims, not guarantees** — verify them against the issuing body.
- Match the depth of audit to the size and risk of the order.

---

## The Four Layers

| Layer | What to verify | How |
|---|---|---|
| Legal identity | Registered name, business license, entity type | Ask for registration; cross-check with references |
| Production capability | What they make in-house vs source, formats, volume | Site visit, trial order, capability questionnaire |
| Quality control | Incoming, in-process, and final inspection | Ask for the QC procedure, not just a certificate |
| Documentation | Certificates, test reports, compliance | Request per-order documents, verify with issuers |

---

## The QC Program: What to Ask For

A real quality-control program has three stages. Ask the manufacturer to describe each one.

1. **Incoming inspection** — how raw materials are checked when they arrive (species, grade, adulteration). See [authentication](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).
2. **In-process control** — how the blend, moisture, and forming are held consistent during production.
3. **Final inspection** — how the finished product is checked against the agreed specification (format, burn time, aroma, packaging).

A manufacturer who can describe all three in concrete terms is signaling a real process. One who can only point at a wall of certificates is not.

---

## Certifications and Test Documents: What to Request

Certification requirements depend on your product and destination market, so there is no universal list. The useful move is to ask what *they* can provide and then verify it.

| Document type | What it addresses | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Quality-management certification (e.g., ISO 9001) | Process consistency | General supply qualification |
| Material/ingredient test reports | Species, grade, adulteration | High-value botanicals (sandalwood, agarwood) |
| Safety / burn-behavior test reports | Smoke, burn time, flash point | Products sold where combustion safety is regulated |
| Compliance / regulatory documents | Market-specific requirements | Depends on your destination country |
| Country-of-origin / export documents | Customs and traceability | Every export shipment |

**Request these per order, and verify them against the issuing body.** A certificate is a claim made by someone; your job is to check the claim.

---

## When to Use a Third-Party Audit

An on-site or third-party audit costs money and time, so match it to the stakes:

- **Small trial order** → references, a sample, and clear terms may be enough.
- **Large or recurring order** → a third-party inspection or audit is the standard safeguard.
- **High-value botanical material** → verification of material claims (species, grade) becomes the priority, and laboratory testing may be warranted.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I verify an incense supplier is legitimate?
Verify identity first: ask for the registered legal name and business registration, then cross-check it independently — through references, public records, or a third-party verification where the order justifies it. Then verify capability: confirm what they manufacture in-house versus source, and match that to your product. Finally, run a trial order with agreed inspection checkpoints. Legitimacy is established by evidence that survives checking, not by a professional-looking catalog. See [the wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/).

### Q: What certifications should an incense factory have?
There is no universal list, because the right certifications depend on your product and your destination market. The general categories are a quality-management certification (such as ISO 9001) for process consistency, material test reports for species and grade, safety or burn-behavior test reports where combustion safety is regulated, and any destination-specific compliance documents. The key discipline is not which certificates to demand in the abstract, but to request documents per order and verify them against the issuing body.

### Q: What is a third-party factory audit?
A third-party factory audit is an inspection of the manufacturer's facility, processes, and documentation by an independent party rather than the supplier's own word. It typically checks the production line, quality-control procedures, and records against the claims the supplier has made. It costs money and time, so it is matched to the stakes — a large or recurring order, or high-value material, justifies it, while a small trial order may not. An audit reduces risk by giving you independent evidence, not by removing risk entirely.

### Q: How do I check the quality of incense before buying in bulk?
Check it in three layers. First, the product itself: pull your own sample and verify format, material, and aroma on gentle heat against a reference — the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) gives practical field signals. Second, the process: ask how the manufacturer inspects incoming material, holds the process steady, and releases the final batch. Third, the documentation: request per-order test reports and verify them. Product, process, and documents together — not any one of them alone — are how you check quality.

### Q: What should I look for in a manufacturer's QC process?
Look for a three-stage process, not a slogan. Incoming inspection — how raw materials are checked for species and grade when they arrive. In-process control — how the blend, moisture, and forming are held consistent during production. Final inspection — how the finished product is checked against the agreed specification before release. A manufacturer who can describe all three in concrete, testable terms is demonstrating a real quality system; one who points only at certificates is not.
