**Direct answer:** Sandalwood (檀香, tánxiāng) and red sandalwood (紫檀, zǐtán) are **unrelated plants that share one Chinese character**. Sandalwood is the fragrant heartwood of *Santalum album* (Santalaceae), the creamy, milky reference incense wood. Red sandalwood is the dense crimson heartwood of *Pterocarpus santalinus* (Fabaceae), a leguminous dye-and-carving wood of southern India whose finely-ground wood is also used in incense as a sweet, softly spicy supporting material. The names collide because 檀 in 紫檀 is honorific — "wood of the aromatic register" — not botanical. The scientific name is the only reliable anchor.

**Key facts:** 檀香 is *Santalum album*, Santalaceae, an evergreen hemiparasitic tree, valued for scented heartwood. 紫檀 is *Pterocarpus santalinus*, Fabaceae, a legume of the Eastern Ghats, valued for crimson dye (santalin) and dense timber, CITES trade-restricted. Both appear in Chinese incense, but as different materials with different roles.

## What is the difference?

| | 檀香 — Sandalwood | 紫檀 — Red sandalwood |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific name | *Santalum album* | *Pterocarpus santalinus* |
| Family | Santalaceae (sandalwood family) | Fabaceae (legume family) |
| Plant type | Evergreen hemiparasitic tree | Leguminous tree |
| Origin | India, Indonesia, and other regions (now cultivated widely) | Dry deciduous forests of southern India (Eastern Ghats) |
| Part used | Scented heartwood | Crimson heartwood (sapwood is nearly valueless) |
| Signature trait | Creamy, milky, sweet-woody aroma | Deep red pigment (santalin) used as dye |
| Incense role | Reference wood; backbone or standalone | Subtle supporting wood; sweet, softly spicy |
| Trade status | *Santalum album* trade-managed (sustainability concerns, cultivation response) | CITES-listed; scarce and trade-restricted |

## Naming: where the confusion comes from

In Chinese, 檀 (tán) is the character for the aromatic-wood register. 檀香 — literally "sandalwood fragrance" — names the true incense wood. 紫檀 — "purple/violet sandalwood" — applies the honorific 檀 to a dense dark wood that is not an aromatic *Santalum* at all. The classical pharmacopoeias already recorded the variety, which is what makes the confusion documented rather than new: 香乘 quotes 苏颂 — 「檀香有数种黄白紫之异」 — *sandalwood has several kinds, differing as yellow, white and purple.* In that classical scheme 紫 names a *color-and-density profile*, not a botanical identity.

The English naming layer compounds the problem: "red sandalwood" sounds like a sandalwood variant. It is not. When a specification sheet says "sandalwood," only the Latin binomial tells you which material is actually on the table.

## Aroma and use in incense

**Sandalwood** is the reference wood for a reason: its aroma is warm, creamy, milky, sweet and soft-woody, developing slowly on gentle heat and aging well in blends. It functions as a backbone (君-material in many 合香 compounds) and stands alone as a single-material incense.

**Red sandalwood** is a different character: woody and sweet rather than resinous or sharp — soft, slightly rosy, gently spicy, developing on gentle heating. It is a subtle supporting wood, not a loud top note, and its defining trait is the vivid red heartwood that doubles as dye. In incense it appears as finely-ground powder, often to add warmth and depth rather than a signature scent.

## What a buyer should check

- **The Latin name on the spec.** *Santalum album* vs *Pterocarpus santalinus* is the entire difference. Ask for the binomial, not the English or Chinese trade name.
- **CITES documentation for red sandalwood.** *Pterocarpus santalinus* is trade-restricted; legitimate shipments carry species-level documentation. "Red sandalwood powder" without an origin and species statement is a red flag.
- **Sandalwood species ambiguity.** Even within true sandalwood, *Santalum* has several species (album, spicatum, austrocaledonicum). Indian sandalwood (*S. album*) is the classical reference, but Indonesian, Australian and other origins circulate — each with different aroma profiles and price points. Pin down the species and origin separately.
- **Powder vs chips.** For both woods, powder is the incense-ready form; chips suit gentle heating. Powder of either is easy to adulterate — check the burn/smell character before committing to volume.

## Which should you use?

Neither is a substitute for the other. Choose **sandalwood** for the creamy, milky reference aroma — as a standalone or the backbone of a compound blend. Choose **red sandalwood** when a blend needs a subtle, sweet-spicy depth and warmth — or when the red color itself matters (historical dye-and-powder uses). Mixing them up is not a small error: the aroma profiles, price structures, and documentation requirements are entirely different.

## FAQ

**Are sandalwood and red sandalwood the same plant?**
No. Sandalwood is *Santalum album* (Santalaceae); red sandalwood is *Pterocarpus santalinus* (Fabaceae). They are unrelated plants that share the Chinese character 檀. The shared character is honorific in 紫檀, not botanical.

**Why is red sandalwood called "sandalwood" if it isn't?**
Because the English name was coined from the Chinese 紫檀 (purple 檀), where 檀 marks the aromatic-wood register rather than the *Santalum* genus. The classical sources already listed 檀香 varieties as 黄白紫 — yellow, white, purple — so the color-based naming predates modern taxonomy.

**Is red sandalwood a good substitute for sandalwood in incense?**
Not as a substitute. Its aroma is woody-sweet and softly spicy — a supporting character — versus the creamy, milky reference aroma of true sandalwood. It works alongside sandalwood as a warm supporting note, but it does not replace it.

**Which one is more expensive?**
Pricing depends on grade and origin, but genuine *Pterocarpus santalinus* is scarce and CITES-restricted, so quality red sandalwood commands serious prices; high-grade Indian *Santalum album* is also expensive due to slow growth and historic overharvesting, though cultivation has widened supply. Both woods have active adulteration markets — neither price is a guarantee of authenticity.

**How do I tell them apart?**
Color is the quickest visual cue: red sandalwood heartwood is deep crimson and visibly dense; sandalwood heartwood is pale yellowish-brown. Aroma confirms it: sandalwood is creamy-milky; red sandalwood is softly spicy-sweet. For commercial quantities, the species statement on the documentation is the only reliable test.
