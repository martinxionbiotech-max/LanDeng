**Direct answer:** Buying agarwood for incense manufacturing is a specification problem, not a shopping problem. The buyer's job is to pin down, in writing and per lot: **species, origin, cultivated vs wild, resin content, grade, moisture, and processing state** — then verify those claims on a sample before any volume commitment. Price and MOQ are the *last* variables to discuss, not the first, and both are confirmed per inquiry, never from a list.

**Key facts:** This page is a sourcing checklist for buyers who will put agarwood into a product — sticks, cones, blends, or loose chips — rather than buy a single collectible piece. It walks through the specification dimensions in the order a real procurement decision follows them, and it publishes no MOQs, prices, or capacities, because those vary by supplier and must be verified directly.

## Key Takeaways

- Agarwood is **not a commodity** — the same "agarwood" label covers a wide range of species, grades, and price points, so the specification is the purchase.
- Pin down **species, origin, cultivated vs wild, resin content, grade, moisture, and processing** before discussing price.
- **Price and MOQ are confirmed per inquiry** — there is no honest universal number, and any supplier who quotes one without asking your specification is guessing.
- **Verify on a sample you pull yourself** — a sample confirms the product, not the supplier's ability to reproduce it at volume.
- The deepest risk is **adulteration**, which is why authentication checks come before, not after, the commercial negotiation.

## Species and Origin: The Two Questions That Anchor Everything

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is not one tree — it is resin-saturated heartwood from several *Aquilaria* species, and origin changes both aroma character and legal status. A buyer should ask "which species, which region" and expect a specific answer, because the name "agarwood" alone identifies nothing.

Agarwood (沉香, chénxiāng) is a condition of the wood, not a species. The two names a manufacturing buyer encounters most are *Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香, traditionally harvested in southern China) and *Aquilaria malaccensis* (the main Southeast Asian commercial species, CITES-listed). Because the material sold as "agarwood" can come from different species, the first specification question is always: **which *Aquilaria* species, and which region?**

Origin matters for two practical reasons beyond aroma. First, regional material has different market reputation and price — Hainan and Vietnamese material has long been the aromatic benchmark in Chinese trade. Second, origin interacts with legal status: wild-harvested material from CITES-listed species may require permits, while cultivated plantation material is the renewable mainstream. The botanical and legal specifics are in the [agarwood ingredient page](/ingredients/agarwood/).

## Cultivated vs Wild: A Documentation Question, Not Just a Quality Question

> **Direct answer:** Wild agarwood is scarce, slow to form, and legally sensitive; cultivated (plantation) agarwood is produced by inoculating farmed trees and is the renewable, lower-cost mainstream. The two differ in age, resin character, price, and legal status, so the buyer must ask which is being offered.

Most commercial agarwood today is **cultivated** — produced by deliberately inoculating farmed *Aquilaria* trees to induce resin formation. Wild old-growth agarwood is depleted and subject to CITES controls. The distinction carries three consequences for a manufacturer:

- **Cost** — wild and old-growth material commands far higher prices than plantation material.
- **Consistency** — plantation material can be produced more repeatably, which matters for a product that must be reorderable.
- **Documentation** — wild material, or material from CITES-listed species, may require permits that a buyer must see before the goods move.

The practical rule is simple: a credible supplier can state whether the material is wild or cultivated, name the species and origin, and provide the documents that support the claim. A supplier who offers only an unqualified "agarwood" label has not answered the specification.

## Resin Content and Grade: The Quality Axis

> **Direct answer:** Resin content is the core driver of agarwood quality — it sets density, aroma intensity, and longevity. Chinese practice grades it by density (the sinking test), and the top grade 奇楠 (qínán) is structurally distinct. For manufacturing, the relevant question is "what grade can you supply repeatably," not "what is your best piece."

Chinese practice grades agarwood primarily by resin density, using the water test: 沉水 (sinks) is the most resin-saturated and valuable, 半沉半浮 (half-sinks) is medium, and floating material is lower. 奇楠 (qínán, "kyara-grade") is the structurally distinct top grade — "oil wraps wood" rather than "wood wraps oil" — and is far rarer and more expensive than ordinary agarwood.

For a manufacturer, grade has a specific meaning that differs from a collector's: the question is not whether a single superb piece exists, but whether the supplier can deliver a **consistent grade, batch after batch**, at a workable cost. A steady mid-grade material with a known species and origin is usually more useful in a product than an irreproducible top grade. The full grading vocabulary — including the 奇楠 distinction and the five-color hierarchy — is in the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/), and the special caution that applies to buying 奇楠 specifically is in the [qínán buying guide](/blog/qinan-buying-authentication/).

## Moisture and Processing State: The Manufacturing Variables

> **Direct answer:** Moisture and processing state — whether the material is raw wood, cleaned chips, or ground powder, and at what particle size and moisture level — change how agarwood grinds, packs, burns, and stores. There is no universal correct value; the target depends on the formula and production line, and must be agreed with the supplier per order.

The same resin wood behaves very differently depending on how it has been prepared. A buyer should specify, and the supplier should state:

- **Form** — whole pieces, chips, or powder. Chips suit warming and resale; powder suits blending into sticks and cones. See [powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).
- **Particle size** (for powder) — uniformity drives even burning and consistent packing. The correct target depends on the binder and the other ingredients in the formula.
- **Moisture** — too much dulls the burn and risks mold in storage; too little makes the material brittle and fast-burning. The right level depends on the formula and the production environment.

These are legitimate specification points, but they have **no universal correct number** — a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another. The buyer's job is to state the intended use and ask the supplier to confirm the form, particle size, and moisture for *that* application. This is the same discipline described in the [manufacturing quality-control guide](/blog/incense-manufacturing-quality-control/).

## Adulteration: The Risk That Sits Behind Every Quote

> **Direct answer:** Because genuine agarwood is expensive, it is heavily faked — dyed white wood, unrelated woods treated with fragrance, and oil-injected low-grade material. Screening happens on a sample: burn test, gentle-heat aroma, and density, with laboratory testing reserved for high-value lots.

Adulteration is the single largest risk in agarwood sourcing, and it concentrates at the price points where buyers are most tempted. The documented fakes are:

- **Dyed or oil-soaked white wood** — non-resinous *Aquilaria* wood soaked in fragrance oil and darkened.
- **Foreign woods** — unrelated species treated with synthetic fragrance and sold under the agarwood name.
- **Oil-injected material** — low-grade wood pumped with resin or oil to add weight so it passes density checks.

Practical screening checks — burning a small piece for smoke character, smelling it on gentle heat, and testing density — are traditional and trade heuristics, not laboratory proof. A genuine piece tends to give white smoke with oil seepage and a clean, complex, sweet aroma; fakes often give black smoke and a "burnt wood" or one-note "perfume" smell. These screening methods sit inside the wider [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) and the [substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/).

## Sampling and Authentication: What to Verify, and How

> **Direct answer:** A sample verifies the product — species, grade, aroma, form — but not the supplier. The buyer pulls the sample, checks it against the written specification, keeps it as the reference standard, and reserves laboratory testing for lots where the value justifies it.

Sampling is the checkpoint between inquiry and commitment. The sequence for agarwood specifically:

1. **State the specification in writing** — species, origin, cultivated vs wild, grade, form, particle size, moisture.
2. **Request a sample against that specification** — and pull it yourself where possible, rather than accepting a hand-picked piece.
3. **Check aroma on gentle heat** — the decisive test; it should be complex and evolving, not flat or "burnt."
4. **Check density and smoke** — as screening signals, with the caveat that fakes are engineered to pass the sink test.
5. **Keep the approved sample as the reference standard** — every later batch is checked against it.
6. **Reserve laboratory testing** — for high-value or high-volume lots, documented verification of species and authenticity is the reliable route.

What a sample cannot tell you — batch-to-batch consistency, delivery reliability, and scale capability — is exactly what a trial order and supplier references verify. The general sampling logic is in the [samples program](/blog/samples-program/).

## Packaging, Storage, and Shipping: Protecting the Specification

> **Direct answer:** After the material is verified, the specification must survive transit and storage: moisture protection, aroma sealing, and controlled storage conditions. Packaging, storage, and shipping terms are agreed per order, alongside the documents that support legal movement of the material.

The specification you approve at the factory is only worth something if it arrives intact. Three considerations:

- **Packaging** — agarwood chips and powder need sealing against air and moisture, because botanical material loses scent and absorbs humidity in transit. Bulk versus retail presentation is a separate, per-order decision.
- **Storage** — controlled humidity and sealed containment protect both the aroma and the moisture specification; storage guidance is in the [storage & longevity guide](/blog/incense-storage-longevity/).
- **Shipping** — Incoterms define who bears cost and risk at each point, and for agarwood from CITES-listed species, the permits and documentation must be in order before the shipment moves. The mechanics are in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

## MOQ, Price, and Documentation: The On-Request Layer

> **Direct answer:** Minimum order quantity and price are confirmed per inquiry, because both depend on the grade, quantity, form, and destination — no honest supplier publishes a universal number. Documentation — species, origin, wild/cultivated status, and any CITES permits — is requested and verified per order.

This page deliberately publishes no MOQ, price, or capacity figures, because any number stated here would be fabricated. The honest position is the one that governs the whole commercial cluster: **these are confirmed per inquiry.** What a buyer *should* do instead of asking for a number is ask for the drivers:

- **What changes the price?** Grade, form (chips vs powder), quantity, and destination all move it.
- **What is the MOQ for this grade and form at this quantity?** A specific answer, not a generic one.
- **What documentation comes with the lot?** Species and origin statement, wild vs cultivated status, and — for CITES-listed material — the relevant permits.

This is the quotation discipline explained in the [MOQ & pricing guide](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/), and it matters more for agarwood than for almost any other ingredient, precisely because the spread between grades is so wide that an unqualified "agarwood" price is meaningless.

## A Buyer's Decision Sequence

The full sourcing decision, in order:

1. **Specify** — species, origin, wild vs cultivated, grade, form, particle size, moisture.
2. **Vet** — supplier identity, capability, and references (see [factory vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/)).
3. **Sample and authenticate** — pull a sample, check it against the specification, keep it as the reference.
4. **Negotiate** — MOQ, price, packaging, storage, shipping, and documentation, all per inquiry.
5. **Trial order** — verify the supplier can reproduce the sample at volume.
6. **Inspect** — pre-shipment, against the retained reference and the written specification.

## Traditional Use vs Modern Evidence

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood has a long record in Chinese materia medica, where it is described in traditional language — "moving qi and relieving pain" and warming the middle. That is traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence, and it is not a basis for any product claim.

- **Traditional Use** — agarwood is burned in ritual, meditation, and daily incense practice for its aroma and its cultural association with calm and refinement.
- **Historical Record** — agarwood appears in classical Chinese materia medica and incense manuals as a high-ranking aromatic, and the 沉水 density test is documented trade practice.
- **Scientific Evidence** — the resin's chemistry (sesquiterpenes and related compounds) is studied, but no clinical or medical efficacy claim is made here.
- **Modern Industry Practice** — the sourcing discipline on this page — specify, verify, sample, trial-order — is standard procurement practice applied to a high-value, heavily adulterated botanical.

No health or medical claims are made.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I buy agarwood for incense manufacturing?
Buy it as a specification, not as a name. Write down species, origin, cultivated vs wild status, grade, form (chips or powder), particle size, and moisture, then ask the supplier to confirm each and provide supporting documentation. Sample against that specification, authenticate the sample, keep it as the reference standard, and confirm MOQ, price, packaging, and shipping per inquiry. A trial order then verifies the supplier can reproduce the sample at volume. The sequence — specify, vet, sample, negotiate, trial-order — is the whole discipline.

### Q: Is cultivated agarwood suitable for incense manufacturing?
Yes, and it is the mainstream for commercial production. Cultivated (plantation) agarwood is produced by inoculating farmed *Aquilaria* trees to induce resin formation, which makes it renewable, more repeatable, and lower-cost than depleted wild material. It is typically lower in grade than old-growth wild agarwood, but for a product that must be reorderable to a consistent specification, that consistency is an advantage. A buyer should still ask which species, which origin, and which grade, because "cultivated" describes how it was produced, not its quality on its own.

### Q: What should I check when buying agarwood in bulk?
Check the specification first — species, origin, wild vs cultivated, grade, form, particle size, and moisture — then the material. Pull a sample and verify aroma on gentle heat, density, and smoke character as screening signals, with laboratory testing for high-value lots. Then check the supplier — identity, capability, references, and documentation, including any CITES permits for listed material. Finally, confirm MOQ, price, packaging, storage, and shipping terms per inquiry. Specification, sample, supplier, terms — in that order.

### Q: How do I avoid fake agarwood?
Start with the seller, not the material: a credible supplier can name the species, origin, and grade, and state whether the material is wild or cultivated. On a sample, screen with the burn test (white smoke with oil seepage vs black smoke), gentle-heat aroma (complex and sweet vs "burnt" or one-note "perfume"), and density — remembering that oil-injection fakes are engineered to pass the sink test. For expensive lots, laboratory verification is the reliable route. The full screening logic is in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

### Q: What documentation should I request for agarwood?
Request a species and origin statement, a wild vs cultivated declaration, and — for material from CITES-listed species — the permits the shipment requires. For powder, also confirm particle size and moisture. A certificate is a claim, not a guarantee, so verify it against the issuing body, and tie the document to the specific lot you are buying rather than accepting a generic statement. See [factory vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/) for the full document checklist.

### Q: Is there a minimum order quantity for agarwood?
There is no universal figure, because the minimum depends on the grade, form, quantity, and destination — which is why any honest supplier confirms it per inquiry rather than publishing a number. The useful question is not "what is your MOQ" but "what is the MOQ for *this* grade at *this* quantity," answered at the quotation stage. See the [MOQ & pricing guide](/blog/moq-pricing-guide/).
