**Direct answer:** Natural incense is made from **plant material** — woods, resins, herbs, and flowers — bound with a plant binder (like makko) and often scented with essential oil. Synthetic incense replaces all or most of that with a **charcoal or sawdust base** dipped in manufactured fragrance oil. The difference is real, but "natural" is an **unregulated word** — so the practical question is not "is it natural?" but "what is it actually made of?"

**Key facts:** There is no binding legal definition of "natural" on most incense labels. The honest signals are the **ingredient list**, the **burn behavior** (smoke, ash, after-smell), and **price** — real sandalwood or agarwood is far more expensive than fragrance-oil sticks.

## What "Natural" Actually Means (and Doesn't)

The confusion starts with the word itself. In incense, "natural" is not a regulated or certified claim in most markets — a seller can call a charcoal stick with a synthetic rose fragrance "natural" and face little formal challenge.

So a more useful framing is **material vs. fragrance**:

- **Material incense** — the scent comes from the actual plant (wood powder, resin tears, ground herbs). A masala agarbatti and a real sandalwood stick are material incense.
- **Fragrance incense** — the scent comes from a manufactured fragrance oil (often synthetic, sometimes built from natural isolates), carried on a neutral base (charcoal, sawdust, or a bare core).

Both are legitimate products. The problem is when the *label* implies material but the product is fragrance — which is exactly what happens with "sandalwood" and "agarwood" sticks.

---

| Signal | Real material (tends to) | Fragrance oil (tends to) |
|---|---|---|
| Ingredient list | Names the plant / resin / wood | Vague — "fragrance," "parfum," or nothing |
| Burn / after-smell | Complex, changes, fades naturally | One-note, uniform, lingers as "perfume" |
| Price | Higher for scarce woods (sandalwood, agarwood) | Cheap, uniform across "scents" |

## The Three Honest Signals

1. **Ingredients.** A real material list names the plant (e.g. "*Santalum album* wood powder, makko binder"). A vague "natural fragrance" is not the same thing.
2. **Burn behavior.** Material incense tends to produce a more complex, evolving aroma and a characteristic ash and after-smell; fragrance-oil sticks often burn with a louder, more uniform scent and can smell "perfumy" or chemical. (This is a qualitative signal, not a laboratory test.)
3. **Price.** Real sandalwood powder and genuine agarwood are expensive — on the order of dollars per gram for the wood, versus pennies for a fragrance-oil stick. If a "sandalwood" stick is very cheap, it is almost certainly fragrance.

## Where the Material Really Matters: Sandalwood & Agarwood

The two materials where this distinction matters most are also the two most substituted:

- **Sandalwood** — real *Santalum album* heartwood is scarce; cheap sticks are fragrance oil. See [sandalwood](/blog/sandalwood-incense/).
- **Agarwood** — one of the most heavily adulterated materials on earth; dyed wood, oil-injected wood, and foreign woods are common. See [agarwood](/blog/agarwood-incense/).

For both, the material-vs-fragrance distinction is not snobbery — it determines whether you are smelling the wood or a reconstruction of it.

3. **Assuming natural = safe.** Natural material still burns and produces smoke; ventilation matters.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I tell if incense is natural?
Read the ingredient list for actual plant names (for example, "*Santalum album* wood powder, makko binder") rather than vague "fragrance" or "parfum." Watch the burn and after-smell: material incense tends to evolve and fade naturally, while fragrance-oil sticks often smell uniform and "perfumy." Treat a very low price as a signal of fragrance rather than material. There is no single home test that proves "natural" — the three signals (ingredients, burn, price) work together.

### Q: Is "natural" incense safer than synthetic?
LanDeng makes no such claim. "Natural" describes what the material is, not a safety guarantee: plant material and synthetic bases both produce smoke and fine particulate matter when burned, so both belong in a well-ventilated space. Ventilation, short sessions, and keeping burning incense away from children and pets apply regardless of whether the stick is natural or synthetic. Equating "natural" with "safe" is a common misunderstanding this page does not endorse.

### Q: What is makko?
A plant-based binder and combustion aid made from the powdered bark of *Machilus thunbergii* (the tabu tree). It holds natural incense together and burns cleanly, which is why it is a common sign of a material-based stick rather than a charcoal or sawdust base soaked in fragrance. It is the reference natural binder in Japanese and Chinese incense. See [what makes incense natural](/blog/what-makes-incense-natural/).

### Q: Why is real sandalwood so expensive?
*Santalum album* is slow-growing and hemiparasitic — it needs host plants to grow — and its heartwood takes decades to develop the aromatic oil. Wild stands have also been heavily depleted, which adds to scarcity. Real sandalwood powder is therefore priced per gram, far above the cost of a charcoal stick scented with synthetic "sandalwood" fragrance. A suspiciously cheap "sandalwood" stick is almost always fragrance. See [sandalwood](/blog/sandalwood-incense/).
