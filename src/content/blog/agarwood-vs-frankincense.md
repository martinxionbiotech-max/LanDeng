**Direct answer:** Agarwood and frankincense are both premium aromatic materials, but they are **different substances from different plants**. Agarwood (沉香) is the resin-impregnated *heartwood* of *Aquilaria* trees — wood that turns fragrant only after injury — while frankincense (乳香) is a tapped *gum-resin* that *Boswellia* trees exude from scored bark. Agarwood is deep, sweet, and woody; frankincense is fresh, citrus, and resinous. In price and status they sit far apart: agarwood is the most prized and most heavily faked of all incense materials, while frankincense is comparatively abundant and rarely substituted.

**Key facts:** Agarwood is a condition of *Aquilaria* wood (Thymelaeaceae), frankincense a *Boswellia* resin (Burseraceae). Both are typically warmed or heated rather than burned with an open flame, but for different reasons — agarwood to protect its layered aroma, frankincense because resin melts and volatilizes under gentle heat. Neither is "better"; each serves a different role in a blend.

## Key Takeaways

- Agarwood is **resin-saturated heartwood**; frankincense is **tapped gum-resin** — different botanical origins entirely.
- Agarwood is a **base note** (deep, anchoring); frankincense is a **top note** (bright, lifting).
- Agarwood carries a **very high substitution risk**; frankincense is **low** (usually the real resin).
- Agarwood is **CITES-listed** and mostly cultivated now; frankincense is **wild/semi-managed** with over-tapping pressure in parts of its range.
- Both reward **gentle heat** over open flame.
- This page makes **no health or medical claims** for either material.

## What Each Material Actually Is

The first confusion is that both are loosely called "resins" in casual writing. They are not the same category of material:

- **Agarwood (沉香)** is *wood*. Healthy *Aquilaria* wood is pale and nearly scentless; only after the tree is wounded — by insects, weather, or deliberate inoculation — does it saturate the damaged heartwood with resin over years or decades, darkening, densifying, and scenting the wood. "Agarwood" therefore names a **condition**, not a species. See the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/).
- **Frankincense (乳香)** is *resin*. *Boswellia* trees are scored, and the tree exudes a milky resin that hardens into droplets ("tears") collected by hand. It is a gum-resin, not wood, and is graded by tear size, color, and clarity. See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).

The shared word "resin" is doing two different jobs: agarwood's resin is trapped *inside* the wood and is inseparable from it, while frankincense's resin *is* the material.

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* heartwood — wood transformed by injury into a fragrant material — while frankincense is *Boswellia* gum-resin tapped from scored bark and hardened into tears. One is wood with resin inside it; the other is resin alone.

## Botanical Source and Naming

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood comes from *Aquilaria* species (Thymelaeaceae), chiefly *A. sinensis* in southern China and *A. malaccensis* in Southeast Asia. Frankincense comes from *Boswellia* species (Burseraceae), chiefly *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, and *B. serrata*. Different families, different genera, different plant parts.

The botanical gap is the deepest difference:

- **Agarwood** — genus *Aquilaria*, family Thymelaeaceae; a genus of roughly 15–20 tree species across South and Southeast Asia. The two names buyers meet most are *Aquilaria sinensis* (白木香, southern China) and *Aquilaria malaccensis* (the main Southeast Asian commercial species, CITES Appendix II).
- **Frankincense** — genus *Boswellia*, family Burseraceae; small trees and shrubs of northeastern Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and India. The main commercial species are *B. sacra* (Arabia), *B. carterii* (the Horn of Africa), and *B. serrata* (India).

In Chinese, the names record their nature: 沉香 (chénxiāng) means "sinking fragrance" (the density test), and 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance" (the milky resin).

## Aroma: Deep Wood vs Bright Resin

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is woody, resinous, sweet, and complex, and it develops in stages as it warms — top note, body, dry-down. Frankincense is fresh, resinous, citrus (lemony) over a warm, creamy, earthy base. Agarwood is deep and evolving; frankincense is bright and clean.

The two aromas occupy opposite ends of a blend:

| | Agarwood (沉香) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| Character | Deep, layered, evolving | Fresh, bright, clean |
| Notes | Woody, resinous, sweet, complex | Citrus, resinous, earthy, creamy |
| Behavior on heat | Develops in stages (头香 / 本香 / 尾香) | Releases cleanly, melts and volatilizes |
| Role in a blend | Base note — anchors and deepens | Top note — lifts heavier woods and resins |

Because agarwood is a base and frankincense a top note, the two are **complements in the same blend**, not competitors: a small amount of frankincense can lift a heavy agarwood-centered formula, which is part of why both appear in classical 合香 (compound incense).

## Heating Method: Why Neither Is Usually Burned

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香) so its layered aroma unfolds without a charred note; frankincense is heated on a charcoal disc or electric heater because resin melts and releases scent below the point of open-flame combustion. Both are "heated, not burned," but for different reasons.

- **Agarwood** — the refined method rests chips above charcoal buried in ash, so the material releases scent without combusting. This preserves the top-note → body → dry-down progression that is the whole point of the material.
- **Frankincense** — tears are placed on a lit charcoal disc or warmed on an electric heater; the resin melts and volatilizes. An electric heater gives a cleaner, lower-smoke result.

The distinction matters in practice: a buyer who burns agarwood on open flame loses most of its value, while frankincense needs enough heat to melt the tear but not so much that it chars.

## Grades, Quality, and Adulteration

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is graded primarily by resin density — the sinking test (沉水 → 半沉半浮 → floating) — with 奇楠 (qínán) as the structurally distinct top grade, and it is among the most heavily adulterated aromatics on the market. Frankincense is graded by tear size, color, and clarity, plus species and origin, and is comparatively rarely faked.

This is where the two diverge most sharply for a buyer:

- **Agarwood quality** — resin content drives density, aroma intensity, and longevity. Origin (Hainan and Vietnamese material long favored), age, formation type, and aroma character all matter. The top grade, 奇楠, is structurally different ("oil wraps wood"). The full treatment is in the [agarwood grading guide](/blog/agarwood-grading-guide/).
- **Agarwood adulteration** — dyed white wood, foreign woods dressed up as agarwood, and oil-injected low-grade material are all common. Screening methods (burn test, gentle heat, water test) are in the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).
- **Frankincense quality** — larger, paler, cleaner tears rank higher; dust and mixed fragments are lower grade. Species sets the aroma profile (*B. sacra* classic, *B. carterii* clean and citrus, *B. serrata* earthier).
- **Frankincense substitution** — low. It is usually the real resin; the practical questions are species, grade, and form.

The shared lesson is the same one the [comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/) makes: the column that matters most is **substitution risk**, and here the two materials sit at opposite ends.

## Traditional Use and Context

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood and sandalwood are the two reference woods of Chinese incense, and agarwood is the rarer and more reverenced of the pair. Frankincense is a classic ritual and fumigation resin, habitually paired with myrrh across Chinese and Western tradition. Both appear in materia medica, but that language is traditional, not clinical.

- **Agarwood** — the premium anchor in 合香 and the material of refined appreciation; burned in ritual and meditation; recorded in classical materia medica for "moving qi" (traditional language, not clinical evidence).
- **Frankincense** — a classic heated resin in temple and church, ritual and fumigation; in Chinese materia medica it is classically paired with myrrh (没药).

The pairing of frankincense with myrrh is the counterpart to agarwood's pairing with sandalwood — two different reference pairs in two different traditions.

## What Buyers Should Look For

> **Direct answer:** For agarwood, pin down species, origin, density, and authenticity before price — the material is heavily faked. For frankincense, pin down species, origin, tear quality, and form — the material is usually genuine but grades and aroma profiles differ by species.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer, the checks diverge:

- **Agarwood** — which *Aquilaria* species and origin; wild vs cultivated (a legal and price question); aroma on gentle heat (the decisive test); particle size and moisture for powder. See the [agarwood buying guide](/blog/how-to-buy-agarwood-for-incense-manufacturing/).
- **Frankincense** — which *Boswellia* species and origin; tear quality (whole, pale, clean over dust); form (tears for heating, powder for blending, oil for fragrance). See the [wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/) for the sourcing framework.

| Criterion | Agarwood (沉香) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| Material origin | Resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* heartwood (a condition, not a species) | Tapped *Boswellia* gum-resin (hardened "tears") |
| Botanical source | *Aquilaria* spp., Thymelaeaceae | *Boswellia* spp., Burseraceae |

| Role in a blend | Base note — deep, anchoring | Top note — bright, lifting |

| Heating method | Warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香) | Heated on charcoal disc or electric heater (melts) |
| Typical forms | Chips, powder, whole pieces, oud oil | Tears, powder, essential oil |

Neither column is "better." The table describes what each material is and how it behaves — agarwood the deep, scarce, heavily-faked wood; frankincense the bright, abundant, rarely-faked resin.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming both are "resins" and therefore similar.** Agarwood is resin *inside* wood; frankincense *is* resin. Different materials, different behavior.
2. **Burning agarwood on open flame.** That destroys the staged aroma the material is prized for; it should be warmed.
3. **Skipping the substitution check on agarwood.** Frankincense you can mostly trust; agarwood you must verify — it is the most faked material in the category.

## FAQ

### Q: Is agarwood the same as frankincense?
No. Agarwood (沉香) is resin-impregnated heartwood from *Aquilaria* trees (Thymelaeaceae); frankincense (乳香) is tapped gum-resin from *Boswellia* trees (Burseraceae). They are different substances from different plant families. The confusion comes from both being loosely called "resins" in casual writing, but agarwood's resin is trapped inside wood, while frankincense *is* resin hardened into tears.

### Q: Which is more expensive, agarwood or frankincense?
Agarwood is far more expensive, and its top grade 奇楠 (qínán) sits at the top of the incense market. Frankincense is comparatively abundant and low-to-mid priced. The price gap follows scarcity and substitution risk: agarwood forms slowly inside only some *Aquilaria* trees and is heavily faked, while frankincense is tapped more readily and rarely substituted.

### Q: Do agarwood and frankincense smell the same?
No. Agarwood is deep, woody, resinous, and sweet, and it develops in stages as it warms — top note, body, dry-down. Frankincense is fresh, citrus, resinous, and earthy, with a bright, clean character. Agarwood is a base note that anchors a blend; frankincense is a top note that lifts it.

### Q: How do you use agarwood vs frankincense in incense?
Both are heated rather than burned. Agarwood is warmed over indirect heat (隔火焚香) — chips above charcoal buried in ash — so its layered aroma unfolds without a charred note. Frankincense tears are placed on a lit charcoal disc or warmed on an electric heater, where the resin melts and releases its scent. An electric heater gives the cleanest, lowest-smoke result for frankincense.

### Q: Is frankincense or agarwood more likely to be faked?
Agarwood, by a wide margin. It is one of the most heavily adulterated aromatics — dyed white wood, foreign woods, and oil-injected low-grade material are all common. Frankincense is comparatively low-risk: it is usually the real resin, and the practical questions are species, grade, and form rather than authenticity. See the [authentication database](/blog/incense-authentication-database/).

### Q: What is the difference between frankincense and myrrh, and where does agarwood fit?
Frankincense and myrrh are both tapped gum-resins from the same family (Burseraceae) but different genera — *Boswellia* (fresh, citrus, creamy) vs *Commiphora* (bitter, balsamic, earthy). Agarwood is a different thing entirely: resin-saturated *Aquilaria* wood. Agarwood pairs with sandalwood in Chinese incense, while frankincense pairs with myrrh in both Chinese and Western tradition. See [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/) and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).
