**Direct answer:** A sample verifies the **product** — what it smells like, how it burns, its format and material — but it does **not** verify the **supplier** — whether they can deliver the full order on time and to the same spec. Sampling is the standard checkpoint between inquiry and bulk commitment: you request a sample, evaluate it against your brief, and only then move to a trial order. Sample terms (what is provided, any cost, and shipping) are confirmed per request.

**Key facts:** This guide explains the sampling process, what to evaluate in a sample, and — just as important — what a sample cannot tell you. It publishes no pricing or sample-policy specifics, because those are confirmed on request per inquiry.

---

## Key Takeaways

- A sample answers **"what is this product like?"** — not "will this supplier deliver?"
- Evaluate a sample against your **brief**, not against a vague "do I like it."
- **Sampling is one checkpoint** in a longer process: brief → sample → trial order → bulk.
- Treat the sample as a **reference standard** for later batch checks.
- Sample terms — what's provided, any cost, and shipping — are **confirmed per request.**

## What a Sample Verifies

| What you can check | How |
|---|---|
| Aroma | Smell it on gentle heat, not just cold — see [authentication](/blog/incense-authentication-database/) |
| Format | Stick diameter, cone shape, coil size vs. your brief |
| Material | Real wood/resin vs fragrance oil — the key grade signal |
| Burn behavior | Burn time, smoke level, evenness |
| Craft | Hand-rolled vs machine-made construction — see [hand-rolled vs machine-made](/blog/hand-rolled-vs-machine-made/) |

Each of these is a *product* property. That is exactly what a sample is for.

---

## What a Sample Cannot Verify

A single sample is silent on the questions that matter most for a bulk commitment:

- **Batch-to-batch consistency** — one good sample doesn't prove the next 50,000 units match it.
- **Delivery reliability** — a sample doesn't tell you they'll ship on time.
- **Scale capability** — a hand-made sample doesn't prove they can produce at volume.
- **Material sourcing** — one sample doesn't prove the bulk run uses the same grade.

These are supplier properties. They are verified by references, a trial order, and the QC checks in the [factory vetting guide](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/).

## The Sampling Process

1. **Request** — state your product interest, format, and quantity target. See [the wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/).
2. **Confirm terms** — what will be sent, any cost, and shipping, confirmed per request.
3. **Evaluate against your brief** — aroma, format, material, burn, craft.
4. **Keep the reference** — the approved sample becomes your standard for later batch checks.
5. **Move to a trial order** — the step that actually tests the supplier.

---

## How to Evaluate a Sample Like a Buyer

Don't just ask "do I like it." Evaluate it against the specification you asked for:

- Does the **aroma** match the profile on gentle heat (not just unlit)?
- Is the **format** what you specified (diameter, shape, size)?
- Is the **material** real or a fragrance-oil substitute? See [natural vs synthetic](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).
- Is the **burn** within the range you need (time, smoke, evenness)?
- Is the **craft** consistent with the price you expect (hand-rolled vs machine-made)?

The more precisely you can state the gap between sample and brief, the better the supplier can close it.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** The sample is the buyer's most underrated document — not because it proves the product, but because it *defines* it, and becomes the standard every later batch is held against.

**Reasoning:**
- A buyer who keeps an approved reference sample can hold a supplier to a standard, rather than to a memory.
- The honest limit of sampling is the real lesson: trust the product from the sample, and trust the supplier only from the trial order and references that follow it.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I request an incense sample?
State your product interest clearly — format, material or aroma profile, and your target quantity — and confirm the terms of the sample (what will be sent, any cost, and shipping) with the supplier. The [wholesale guide](/blog/wholesale-guide/) covers the inquiry step, and the [quote request](/request-a-quote/) page lists the fields to provide. Sampling is a standard part of the process, not a special favor.

### Q: Do incense samples cost money?
It depends on the supplier and the product, which is why the terms are confirmed per request rather than assumed. Some suppliers provide small standard samples readily; for custom or high-value material, there may be a cost or shipping charge, or a sample fee that is credited against a later order. The honest answer is "on request" — ask the supplier to state the sample terms up front so there are no surprises.

### Q: Does a good sample mean the bulk order will be good?
Not by itself. A sample verifies the product — aroma, format, material, burn — but not the supplier's ability to reproduce that product consistently at volume, or to deliver on time. The gap between a hand-made sample and a bulk production run is exactly where quality can slip. This is why a trial order and batch-to-batch checks against your retained reference sample matter as much as the first sample. See [factory vetting](/blog/factory-vetting-guide/).

### Q: What should I check when I receive an incense sample?
Check it against your brief, not just a vague preference: smell the aroma on gentle heat (not only cold), confirm the format matches what you specified, verify the material is real rather than a fragrance-oil substitute, measure the burn time and smoke level, and check the craft (hand-rolled vs machine-made) against the price you expect. Note the gap between sample and brief precisely, because that gap is what the supplier needs to close in the next iteration.

### Q: How many samples should I request?
Request as many as you need to make a decision, but be intentional: one well-evaluated sample that you can measure against a clear brief is worth more than a dozen evaluated by feel. If you are comparing suppliers, sample the same product specification from each so the comparison is like-for-like. Keep the approved sample as a reference standard, and move to a trial order once the sample matches your brief — that trial order, not more samples, is what verifies the supplier.
