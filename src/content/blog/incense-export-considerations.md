**Direct answer:** Exporting incense means moving a combustible, sometimes botanically controlled product across a border, and the work breaks into four parts: **compliance documentation** (invoice, packing list, permits), **CITES and species controls** for botanicals such as agarwood and certain sandalwoods, **labeling and classification** for the destination market, and **packaging and transport** that protects the product in transit. Every specific — duty rates, HS codes, permits — is destination- and product-dependent and confirmed per shipment.

**Key facts:** This page explains the export layer at the level of standard trade practice, without inventing specific HS codes, duty rates, or permit numbers. It points to the authoritative sources — the CITES checklist, the WCO harmonized system, and national customs authorities — where a shipper confirms the exact requirements for their goods and destination.

## Compliance Documentation: The Paper That Moves the Goods

> **Direct answer:** The core export document set is a commercial invoice, a packing list, and any certificates or permits the destination requires — which depends on the materials and the market. The HS code appears on the declaration and drives the duty.

Every cross-border shipment rides on a short, standard document set, and the cost of a missing document is a shipment held at the border:

- **Commercial invoice** — value, origin, quantity, and a description matching the declaration.
- **Packing list** — what is in each carton, weight, and dimensions.
- **Certificates and permits** — destination-specific; for controlled botanicals, CITES or similar permits may be required.
- **Declaration with the correct classification** — the harmonized-system code that determines duty and any restrictions.

The reliable approach is to confirm the full document list with the supplier and the destination's customs authority before the shipment moves — the mechanics of the customs step are in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

## CITES and Species Controls: The Botanical Layer

> **Direct answer:** Some incense botanicals are regulated in international trade — agarwood-producing *Aquilaria* species are CITES-listed, and certain sandalwoods and other woods are controlled too. Exporting them requires permits, and the buyer must confirm the species and obtain the documents before shipping.

CITES (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) regulates international trade in listed species. Two incense families a buyer is most likely to hit:

- **Agarwood (*Aquilaria* spp.)** — heavily harvested for its resin, agarwood-producing species are CITES-listed, which is one reason cultivated plantation material has become the commercial mainstream. See the [agarwood ingredient page](/ingredients/agarwood/) and [agarwood incense guide](/blog/agarwood-incense/).
- **Sandalwood** — true *Santalum album* is scarce, and some sandalwoods are subject to controls; red sandalwood (*Pterocarpus santalinus*) is CITES Appendix II listed. See the [sandalwood](/ingredients/sandalwood/) and [red sandalwood](/ingredients/red-sandalwood/) ingredient pages.

The practical rule: a seller should be able to state the species and whether the material is wild or cultivated, and to provide the permits for any controlled species. The authoritative listing is the [CITES species checklist](https://checklist.cites.org/). This is a documentation question, not a footnote — shipping controlled material without the right permits is a compliance failure, not a delay.

## HS Classification: The Category, Not a Memorized Code

> **Direct answer:** Incense and similar odoriferous preparations fall under a harmonized-system heading, but the exact code depends on the product and can differ by destination. The exporter confirms the code against the authoritative schedule — for U.S. imports, the HTS — rather than memorizing a number.

The harmonized system (HS) is a standardized classification that determines the duty rate and any restrictions. The category matters more than any specific six-digit number a buyer might memorize, because:

- The exact code depends on the product — a botanical wood chip, a formed incense stick, and an essential-oil-based product can fall under different headings.
- The code can differ by destination and is revised over time.
- A mis-classification discovered after the goods are in transit is one of the most costly and avoidable export errors.

The authoritative sources are the [World Customs Organization](https://www.wcoomd.org/) (the body behind the HS) and, for U.S. imports, the [HTS search tool](https://hts.usitc.gov/). The packaging and customs mechanics are in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/), which is the deeper treatment.

## Labeling: The Destination Sets the Rules

> **Direct answer:** Labeling requirements are set by the destination market, not the exporter — they can include ingredients, country of origin, and warnings — and the label must be confirmed before production, not after the goods are packed.

Labeling is where a product built for one market can silently fail another. The requirements that commonly vary by destination:

- **Ingredient disclosure** — what must be listed, and in what language.
- **Country of origin** — a mandatory mark in many markets.
- **Warnings** — combustion-safety and other cautions that some regulators require.
- **Language** — the destination's official language or languages.

The rule is to confirm the label against the destination's requirements before the production run — retrofitting labels after the fact is an avoidable cost. See the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/) for the packaging-and-labeling interplay.

## Packaging and Transport: Protecting a Combustible Botanical

> **Direct answer:** Incense is fragile and combustible, and botanical incense loses scent when exposed to air and moisture, so export packaging must protect the product, seal the aroma, and satisfy transport rules for the mode of shipment.

Export packaging does three jobs at once, and each pulls differently:

- **Protection** — sticks and cones are fragile; coils and sachets need sealing.
- **Aroma preservation** — botanical material loses scent to air and moisture, so packaging must seal.
- **Transport compliance** — combustible goods have carrier and mode-specific rules; the shipper confirms these with the carrier.

Packaging specifics (bulk vs retail, sealing, fragility allowance) are agreed per order, and the full treatment — including Incoterms and the export checklist — is in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

## FAQ

### Q: What documents do I need to export incense?
The core set is a commercial invoice, a packing list, and any certificates or permits your destination requires — which depends on the materials and the market. You also need the correct harmonized-system classification, which appears on the declaration and drives the duty. For controlled botanicals such as agarwood, CITES or similar permits may be required. Confirm the full list with your supplier and the destination's customs authority before the shipment moves.

### Q: Does agarwood require CITES permits to export?
Agarwood-producing *Aquilaria* species are CITES-listed, so international trade in agarwood is regulated and may require permits, depending on the species and whether the material is wild or cultivated. A seller should be able to state the species and wild vs cultivated status and provide the permits the shipment requires. The authoritative listing is the [CITES species checklist](https://checklist.cites.org/). Confirm the specific permit requirements for your goods and destination before shipping.

### Q: What is the HS code for incense?
Incense and similar odoriferous preparations fall under a harmonized-system heading, but the exact code depends on the product and can differ by destination, so it should be confirmed against the authoritative schedule for your market. For U.S. imports, the [HTS search tool](https://hts.usitc.gov/) is the reference. The classification matters because it determines the duty rate and any restrictions — a mis-classified shipment is a costly and avoidable error. See the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

### Q: What labeling is required for exported incense?
Labeling is set by the destination market, not the exporter. Common requirements include ingredient disclosure, country of origin, and combustion-safety warnings, and the label must be in the destination's language. Confirm the label against the destination's requirements before the production run — retrofitting labels after the fact is an avoidable cost. The labeling and packaging interplay is covered in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

### Q: How should incense be packaged for export?
Package for three jobs at once: protection (sticks and cones are fragile), aroma preservation (botanical incense loses scent to air and moisture, so seal it), and transport compliance (combustible goods have carrier and mode-specific rules). Agree bulk vs retail, sealing, and fragility allowance per order. The full packaging and Incoterms treatment is in the [packaging & shipping guide](/blog/packaging-shipping-guide/).

### Q: Which incense materials are CITES-controlled?
The ones a buyer most commonly encounters are agarwood-producing *Aquilaria* species, which are CITES-listed, and certain sandalwoods — red sandalwood (*Pterocarpus santalinus*) is CITES Appendix II listed, and true *Santalum album* is scarce and subject to controls in some markets. The reliable approach is to check the species of any botanical against the [CITES species checklist](https://checklist.cites.org/) and to ask the supplier for the wild vs cultivated status and any permits.
