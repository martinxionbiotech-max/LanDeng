## What "Resinous" Means

> **Direct answer:** Resinous is the scent of gum-resin — the aromatic material a tree exudes from scored bark, which hardens into tears or blocks. These materials melt and volatilize under gentle heat rather than burning like wood, which is why they are heated, not burned.

The defining trait is physical form and behavior:

- **It is a gum-resin, not wood or flower.** Frankincense, pine resin, and sweetgum are exuded resins; agarwood is resin *inside* wood — a special case.
- **It melts under heat.** Resins release their scent by melting on a charcoal disc or electric heater, not by smoldering like wood.
- **It reads deep and clean.** Frankincense is bright and citrus-resinous; pine resin is piney and conifer-sharp; sweetgum is sweet and warm.

The resinous family is the *depth* register of a blend — the sticky, aromatic middle that bridges a woody base and a bright top note.

## Characteristic Chemistry (as published)

> **Direct answer:** The one resinous material with published, named chemistry is frankincense: its resin contains **boswellic acids** (notably AKBA), the subject of laboratory research — treated as preliminary, not clinical proof. Pine resin is described as resin acids plus a volatile turpentine fraction, and agarwood as sesquiterpenes and related compounds.

The published chemistry is fragmentary and honest:

- **Frankincense** — boswellic acids (AKBA), studied in laboratory settings; the entity page treats medicinal claims as preliminary, not established clinical fact.
- **Pine resin** — a mixture of resin acids and the volatile turpentine fraction; refined rosin/colophony is the solid left after turpentine is distilled off.
- **Agarwood** — sesquiterpenes and related compounds (shared with the woody family).
- **Sweetgum** — described only as "balsamic chemistry," with no named molecule.

Where a specific molecule is not named in the corpus, this page does not invent one.

## Representative Entities

### Frankincense (乳香) — the classic heated ritual resin

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense is the gum-resin of *Boswellia* trees, fresh, citrus-bright, and resinous over a warm, creamy, earthy base. It is the family's flagship: the classic heated resin of temple, church, and fumigation, habitually paired with myrrh.

Frankincense's role follows from its form: as a resin it melts and volatilizes under gentle heat, releasing a clean, bright scent. Its main species — *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, *B. serrata* — set the aroma profile. See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).

### Pine Resin (松香) — conifer-sharp and balsamic

> **Direct answer:** Pine resin is the oleoresin that exudes when pine bark is cut — piney, woody, balsamic, and resinous. Raw resin and refined rosin/colophony are stages of the same material with very different behavior.

Pine resin's practical distinction is raw gum versus refined solid: the gum melts and smokes like a resin, while rosin is a harder, higher-melting solid with adhesive properties. See the [pine resin entity page](/ingredients/pine-resin/).

### Sweetgum (枫香) — sweet and warm

> **Direct answer:** Sweetgum is the resin of *Liquidambar formosana* (Chinese sweetgum), sweet, balsamic, resinous, and warm. It is a cousin of storax, the classic balsamic resin.

Sweetgum's resin carries a sweet, warm character that overlaps heavily with the balsamic family. See the [sweetgum entity page](/ingredients/sweetgum/).

### Agarwood (沉香) — resin inside wood

> **Direct answer:** Agarwood is resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* heartwood — resinous *and* woody *and* sweet. Its resinous layer is what makes it the deepest material in the canon, but its resin is trapped inside wood, not a tapped tear.

Agarwood is the special case where resin and wood are inseparable — the resin saturates the heartwood after injury. See the [agarwood entity page](/ingredients/agarwood/).

### The balsamic neighbors

The resinous family's closest relatives are the **balsamic** resins — myrrh (没药) and benzoin (安息香):

- **Myrrh** — bitter, balsamic, earthy resin, classically paired with frankincense. See [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/).
- **Benzoin** — sweet, vanilla, balsamic resin, a classic fixative. See [benzoin](/ingredients/benzoin/).

These sit in the balsamic family because their character emphasizes sweetness and vanilla-amber over the clean resin register — but they are the same physical class of material.

## Resinous vs Balsamic: The Sibling Split

> **Direct answer:** Resinous and balsamic describe the same physical material — gum-resin — from two angles. Resinous is the deep, clean, aromatic register; balsamic is the sweet, vanilla-amber register. Many materials carry both, which is why the two families overlap.

The split is a matter of emphasis:

| | Resinous | Balsamic |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasis | Deep, clean, aromatic | Sweet, vanilla-amber |
| Flagship | Frankincense | Benzoin, myrrh |
| Shared members | Pine resin, sweetgum (both families) | Pine resin, sweetgum (both families) |

Frankincense is resinous (bright, clean) but not balsamic; benzoin is balsamic (sweet, vanilla) but also woody. The two families are neighbors on the scent map, not rivals. See the [aroma families hub](/blog/incense-aroma-families/).

## Why Resins Melt, Not Burn

> **Direct answer:** Resins melt and volatilize under gentle heat, so the classical method is to warm tears on a charcoal disc or electric heater rather than burn them in a flame. An electric heater gives the cleanest, lowest-smoke result.

The heating method follows from the physical form:

- **Charcoal disc** — the traditional method; tears melt and release their aroma.
- **Electric heater** — warmer and cleaner, releasing scent below combustion.
- **Open flame** — wastes the material's appeal; the resin chars rather than melts cleanly.

This is the practical difference between a resin and a wood: sandalwood can smolder, frankincense must melt. See the [frankincense incense guide](/blog/frankincense-incense/).

## FAQ

### Q: What is the resinous aroma family?
The deep, sticky, aromatic scent of gum-resin — materials that melt under heat rather than burn like wood. Its four canonical members are agarwood, frankincense, pine-resin, and sweetgum, listed in [aroma.json](https://data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/aroma.json).

### Q: What is the difference between resinous and balsamic?
Both describe gum-resin, but from different angles. Resinous is the deep, clean, aromatic register (frankincense); balsamic is the sweet, vanilla-amber register (benzoin, myrrh). Many materials — pine resin, sweetgum — carry both, which is why the two families overlap.

### Q: Is agarwood a resin?
Agarwood is resin-impregnated wood: resin saturates *Aquilaria* heartwood after injury, but the resin is trapped inside the wood, not a tapped tear. It is therefore resinous *and* woody *and* sweet — the deepest material in the canon.

### Q: Why are resins heated rather than burned?
Because they melt and volatilize under gentle heat. Burning them in an open flame chars the material rather than releasing its clean aroma. The classical method is a charcoal disc; an electric heater gives the cleanest result. See the [frankincense incense guide](/blog/frankincense-incense/).

### Q: What is the flagship resinous material in Chinese incense?
Frankincense (乳香), the classic heated ritual resin — fresh, citrus-bright, and resinous, habitually paired with myrrh. See the [frankincense entity page](/ingredients/frankincense/).

### Q: Does frankincense have any health benefits?
The entity page treats medicinal claims about frankincense as preliminary, not established clinical fact. Its boswellic acids are the subject of laboratory research, but burning it as incense is an aromatic/ritual practice, not a medical treatment. This page makes no health claims.
