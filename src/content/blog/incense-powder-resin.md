**Key takeaway:** Incense powder and resin are **loose aromatic materials** — ground woods (like sandalwood powder) and resin tears (like frankincense or myrrh) — that are **heated rather than burned** to release their scent with minimal smoke. This is the closest you can get to the raw material, and it is the format behind refined Chinese and Japanese practice (隔火焚香).

**Key facts:** Resin and wood chips are placed on a heat source — a lit charcoal disc (in ash or sand) or, more gently, an electric incense heater. Indirect heat releases the aroma without a flame, giving a cleaner, truer scent.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Powder & resin are **loose materials**, heated — not burned — for scent.
- This is the **pure-material** format: what you smell is the actual wood or resin.
- **Indirect heat** (隔火焚香, or an electric heater) is the refined method — aroma with minimal smoke.
- It is more hands-on than a stick, and better for small, focused sessions.

---

## The Two Materials

- **Resin** — dried tree resins like frankincense (乳香) and myrrh (没药). They melt and release a rich, fresh scent on heat. See [frankincense](/ingredients/frankincense/) and [myrrh](/ingredients/myrrh/).
- **Wood powder / chips** — ground sandalwood or agarwood chips. They release a slow, complex aroma as they warm. See [sandalwood](/ingredients/sandalwood/) and [agarwood](/ingredients/agarwood/).

The distinction matters: resin **melts**, wood **smolders-warms** — they behave differently on the same heat source.

## How to Heat It

1. **Charcoal method** — light a charcoal disc, place it in a heatproof burner on a bed of ash or sand, and drop a small piece of resin or wood on top. The material releases fragrant smoke.
2. **Electric heater (indirect heat)** — a purpose-built heater warms the material from below at a controlled temperature, releasing aroma with almost no smoke. This is the modern version of 隔火焚香.

Start with **a little** material — a few resin tears or a single chip. Too much produces harsh smoke.

---

## Why This Format Matters (Material, Not Fragrance)

Because you are heating the **actual material**, powder and resin are the formats where the material-vs-fragrance distinction disappears — there is no binder and no fragrance oil. It is also where authenticity matters most: real frankincense tears and genuine agarwood chips are exactly the materials that are substituted elsewhere. See [natural vs synthetic](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).

## Common Mistakes

1. **Burning resin directly in a flame.** Resin is meant to be heated on a charcoal disc or heater, not lit like a stick.
2. **Using too much at once.** A little material goes a long way; excess gives harsh smoke.
3. **Skipping the ash/sand bed.** The bed insulates the burner and protects the surface.

## FAQ

### Q: How do I burn frankincense resin?
There are two standard ways. The traditional method is to light a charcoal disc until it glows, set it in a heatproof burner on a bed of ash or sand, and drop a few resin tears on top — the resin melts and releases a rich, fragrant smoke. The gentler method is an electric incense heater, which warms the resin below combustion so it releases aroma with almost no smoke. Either way, use only a few tears at a time; too much material produces harsh, acrid smoke. See [frankincense](/ingredients/frankincense/) for the material details.

### Q: What is 隔火焚香 (indirect-fire incense)?
隔火焚香 (indirect-fire incense) is the refined Chinese method of warming fragrant material without touching it to the flame. A piece of charcoal is buried in ash, and a thin plate — often of mica or metal — is placed between the charcoal and the wood or resin, so the material is heated gently and releases its aroma with almost no smoke. This low-smoke, slow-release technique is the ancestor of Japanese kōdō, the incense-listening ceremony. See [what Chinese incense is](/blog/what-is-chinese-incense/) for the cultural context.

### Q: Do I need a charcoal disc for resin?
Not necessarily. A charcoal disc is the traditional option and gives a strong, immediate release, but it produces more smoke and runs hot, so it needs a heatproof burner on a bed of ash or sand. An electric incense heater is gentler — it warms the resin below combustion, releasing aroma with almost no smoke and better control over temperature. Both are standard; many people prefer the electric heater for everyday use and reserve charcoal for a stronger, more traditional session. See [incense holder & burner guide](/blog/incense-holder-burner-guide/).

### Q: Is heating resin better than burning a stick?
Not "better" — just different. Heating resin gives you the pure material with minimal smoke, which makes it the truest, cleanest scent and the format where authenticity matters most, since there is no binder or fragrance oil. But it is more hands-on: you need a heater or charcoal, an ash bed, and a bit of practice. A stick is far more convenient — self-contained, predictable, and easy to light — but may include a binder and sometimes a fragrance oil. Choose by what you value: purity and control, or convenience. See [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/).
