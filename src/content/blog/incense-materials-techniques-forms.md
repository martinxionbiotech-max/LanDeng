**Direct answer:** Chinese incense is built from three layers of knowledge — the **materials** (what a stick or blend is made of, from binders to aromatic woods), the **techniques** (how material is processed and how incense is prepared or appreciated, from indirect-fire heating to 合香 blending), and the **forms** (what shape the finished incense takes, from sticks to coils to backflow cones). This page is the readable hub that maps all three taxonomies, each backed by a machine-readable dataset.

**Key facts:** LanDeng structures this knowledge as three open datasets — **15 materials**, **12 techniques**, and **12 forms** — all served as stable JSON from `data.incenseherbs.com`. Each record carries a Chinese name, a pinyin reading, a definition, and a set of typed properties. This page summarizes them for a human reader and links to the full data.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Materials** = what incense is made *from* (15 records: binders, bases, fuels, loose aromatics).
- **Techniques** = how incense is made *and* appreciated (12 records: heating, forming, blending, appreciation).
- **Forms** = what finished incense looks *like* (12 records: sticks, coils, cones, powder, and prepared categories).
- All three are **open datasets** under `data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/`, licensed CC BY-SA 4.0.
- This page is the **human-readable index**; the JSON is the machine-readable source of truth.

## Materials (15)

> **Direct answer:** The 15 materials are the functional ingredients of incense — combustible binders (makko), plant bases and cores (bamboo core, wood powder), loose aromatics (resin, cypress seed, clove powder), and the fillers and oils that mark synthetic incense (charcoal, sawdust, fragrance oil).

Materials divide cleanly by function:

| Material | Chinese | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Makko | 抹香 | The traditional combustible wood-powder binder (Machilus thunbergii bark) |
| Bamboo Core | — | Plant-based central support for formed stick incense |
| Wood Powder / Chips | — | Loose pure material (sandalwood, agarwood) — heated, not burned |
| Resin | 乳香, 没药 | Frankincense and myrrh — melt and release scent when heated |
| Charcoal | — | Combustible filler base in cheap, synthetic incense |
| Sawdust | — | Combustible filler base in cheap, synthetic incense |
| Honey | 蜂蜜 | Traditional plant-derived binder and fixative (柏子香, 合香) |
| Guar Gum | — | Plant-derived natural binder (less central than makko) |
| Gum Arabic | — | Plant-derived natural binder |
| Gum Tragacanth | — | Plant-derived natural binder |
| Synthetic Binder | — | Chemical adhesive/gum in machine-made sticks |
| Fragrance Oil | — | Manufactured, often petroleum-derived scent (synthetic incense) |
| Cypress Seed | 柏子 | The single material of 柏子香, the scholar's incense |
| White Plum | 白梅 | Dried/pickled plum — the tart-sweet "plum" in 梅花香 |
| Clove Powder | 丁香 | Warm-spice aromatic, ground or whole in 合香 |

The binders tell the natural-vs-synthetic story at a glance: makko and honey are plant binders; charcoal, sawdust, and fragrance oil mark the synthetic route. See [makko as a natural binder](/blog/makko-natural-binders/) and [natural vs synthetic incense](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).

## Techniques (12)

> **Direct answer:** The 12 techniques cover two families — how incense is *prepared* (indirect-fire heating, seal incense, pill forming, 合香 blending, flower-scenting) and how it is *appreciated* (incense appreciation, incense ceremony, single-material vs compound formula).

| Technique | Chinese | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect-Fire Incense | 隔火焚香 | Heating material over buried charcoal without burning it |
| Seal Incense | 篆香 / 打香篆 | Pressing powder into a carved pattern that burns along a timed path |
| 合香 Blending | 合香 | Composing a blend on the 君臣佐使 (chief–minister–assistant–guide) principle |
| Incense Appreciation | 品香 | Attending to fragrant wood (chiefly agarwood) with sustained focus |
| Incense Ceremony | 香道 | The cultivated art of incense, formalized in Japan as kōdō |
| Pill Forming | 香丸 | Binding a compound blend with honey, rolling into pellets |
| Scented Sachet | 香囊 | A cloth pouch of fragrant herbs, worn or hung |
| Fumigation | 熏香 | Burning aromatic material to scent a space |
| Flower-Scenting | 花熏香 | A blossom scents a material by steaming (Ming-dynasty 香乘) |
| No-Flame Heating | 空熏 | Releasing aroma below combustion (electric heater) |
| Single-Material Appreciation | 单方 | Appreciating one material alone on its own quality |
| Compound Formula | 复方 | 合香 proper — multiple materials on the 君臣佐使 principle |

The indirect-fire technique (隔火焚香) is the ancestor of Japanese kōdō, and the seal-incense technique is the ancestor of the timed powder pattern. See the [hexiang blending system](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/) and [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/).

## Forms (12)

> **Direct answer:** The 12 forms are the finished shapes incense takes — sticks (bamboo-core and coreless), coils, cones, backflow cones, powder, pills, cakes, plaques, beads, plus the prepared low-smoke and smokeless categories.

| Form | Chinese | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Incense Stick | 线香 | The everyday default — bamboo-core or coreless |
| Incense Coil | 盘香 | A spiral burning for hours, laid flat |
| Incense Cone | 塔香 | Self-supporting cone that burns down completely |
| Backflow Cone | 倒流香 | Hollow cone channeling smoke downward (visual effect) |
| Incense Powder | 香粉 | Loose ground material, pressed into seal patterns or heated |
| Incense Pill | 香丸 | Classical 合香 pellet, honey-bound and air-dried |
| Incense Cake | 香饼 | Pressed-cake form (details unverified in corpus) |
| Incense Plaque | 香牌 | Pressed/carved tablet (details unverified in corpus) |
| Incense Bead | 香珠 | Porous beads scented with oil, worn — zero-smoke |
| Bamboo-Core Stick | 竹签香 | Bamboo splint coated in paste — sturdier, longer-burning |
| Low-Smoke Incense | 微烟香 | Prepared reduced-smoke category (details unverified) |
| Smokeless Incense | 无烟香 | Prepared no-smoke category (details unverified) |

For the buyer-facing decision of which form fits which session, see [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/). For the stick-vs-cone specifics, see [incense stick vs cone](/blog/incense-stick-vs-cone/).

## How the Three Layers Connect

> **Direct answer:** Materials, techniques, and forms are not three separate lists — they combine. A 柏子香 pill is a *material* (cypress seed) shaped by a *technique* (pill forming) into a *form* (incense pill). The three taxonomies describe one craft from three angles.

The classic example runs through all three layers at once:

- **柏子香 (cypress-seed incense)** — the *material* is cypress seed (柏子), the *technique* is honey-binding and (historically) forming, and the *form* is a loose material or a pellet. See the [cypress seed entity](/ingredients/cypress-seed/) and the [baizi recipe](/blog/baizi-incense-recipe/).

A stick of natural sandalwood is the same logic: sandalwood wood powder (material) → ground and bound with makko (technique) → a coreless stick (form).

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing materials with forms.** "Powder" is a form; "sandalwood" is a material. You can have sandalwood powder (a material in a form).
2. **Treating the three lists as exhaustive encyclopedias.** They are taxonomies — a curated index, not every possible material.
3. **Assuming every form is equally documented.** Cake, plaque, low-smoke, and smokeless are recorded terms with details marked unverified in the corpus.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the difference between a material, a technique, and a form in incense?
A material is what incense is made from (sandalwood, makko, honey, resin); a technique is how it is processed or prepared (grinding, indirect-fire heating, 合香 blending); a form is the finished shape (stick, cone, coil, powder, pill). The three describe one craft from three angles — a material is shaped by a technique into a form.

### Q: How many materials, techniques, and forms does LanDeng track?
LanDeng publishes three datasets: 15 materials, 12 techniques, and 12 forms. Each is served as Schema.org Dataset / DefinedTerm JSON from `data.incenseherbs.com/datasets/` and mirrored as static JSON under the main site. See the [knowledge map](/blog/landeng-knowledge-map/) for the full eight-dataset inventory.

### Q: What is the traditional binder in natural incense?
Makko (抹香), the powdered inner bark of the Machilus thunbergii (tabunoki) tree, is the reference natural binder and combustion aid in East Asian stick incense. It burns cleanly and evenly, holds the material together, and is the tell-tale sign of a material-based stick. See [makko as a natural binder](/blog/makko-natural-binders/).

### Q: What marks synthetic incense in the materials list?
Charcoal, sawdust, and fragrance oil are the synthetic markers — a combustible filler base carrying a manufactured scent rather than real plant material. A synthetic binder (chemical adhesive/gum) replaces plant binders like makko. See [natural vs synthetic incense](/blog/natural-vs-synthetic-incense/).

### Q: What are the main incense forms?
The everyday forms are stick (线香), coil (盘香), and cone (塔香), plus backflow cones, powder, pills, cakes, plaques, and beads, and the prepared low-smoke and smokeless categories. The form determines burn time, smoke, and holder. See [which incense format](/blog/which-incense-format/) for the decision guide.

### Q: What is indirect-fire incense (隔火焚香)?
Indirect-fire incense (隔火焚香) is the classical technique of warming material without burning it: a hot charcoal is buried in ash, a thin plate (silver leaf or mica) is placed over it, and the material sits on the plate, releasing aroma with very little smoke. It is the direct ancestor of Japanese kōdō. See [gehuo fenxiang setup](/blog/gehuo-fenxiang-setup/).
