> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed (芫荽籽, yánsuī zǐ) is the dried fruit of *Coriandrum sativum*, an annual herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae) native to the Mediterranean and western Asia. It is a warm, citrusy spice with a woody, faintly floral undertone, used in Chinese incense as a fresh spice accent. It is the seed of the same plant as cilantro (芫荽), but seed and leaf are very different aromatics.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed is warm and citrusy, with a woody, slightly spicy, faintly floral character that is completely different from the fresh, soapy-tasting leaf. The seed's aroma comes largely from the essential oil linalool, which gives it a clean, sweet-woody, citrusy lift. It is a fresh spice accent rather than a base note.

The seed-vs-leaf contrast is the defining fact: the seed is warm, citrusy, and woody, driven by linalool, while the leaf is fresh, green, and polarizing (a soapy taste many people perceive). This is a rare case where one plant yields two aromatics that are effectively *opposites* — the seed a sweet-citrus spice, the leaf a sharp green herb. For any aromatic use, this distinction is the first thing to get right.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 芫荽籽 (yánsuī zǐ) means "coriander seed" — 芫荽 for the coriander plant, 籽 for "seed." The older name 胡荽 (húsuī) carries the 胡 foreign-origin prefix, marking coriander as an imported aromatic. The leaf herb is separately named 香菜 ("fragrant vegetable"), reflecting its fresh culinary use.

The name 芫荽籽 is a two-part label: 芫荽 (the coriander plant) + 籽 (seed), explicitly naming the seed as distinct from the leaf. The older name 胡荽 carries the 胡 foreign-origin prefix — the same marker in 胡椒 (pepper) and 葫芦巴 (fenugreek) — recording coriander's arrival as a western import. The leaf herb, meanwhile, is commonly called 香菜 ("fragrant vegetable") in everyday use. This name structure is itself a disambiguation: 芫荽籽 names the seed, 香菜/芫荽 names the leaf, and 胡荽 records the plant's imported origin.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed appears as a fresh, citrusy spice accent in some 合香 (blended incense) formulas, alongside fennel and citrus peels, rather than as a classical defining material. Its linalool-driven, warm-citrus character brightens heavier blends, and it is the seed — not the leaf — that serves this role.

Coriander seed's incense role is a **fresh spice accent**: it appears in some 合香 blends alongside fennel and citrus peels, where its warm, citrusy, linalool-driven character brightens and lifts heavier compositions. It is a supporting note, not a lead — a clean, sweet-woody citrus lift beneath the resins and woods. The critical point is that this role belongs to the *seed*, not the leaf: the seed's warm-citrus spice is the aromatic contribution, while the leaf's fresh, green, polarizing character has no place in an incense blend. The two are not interchangeable.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed is produced by harvesting the ripe fruit and drying it whole; the dried, round seed-fruit is the traded form, used whole or ground. Drying concentrates the warm, citrusy, linalool character, and no extraction is required — though coriander essential oil can be steam-distilled from the seed.

The ripe fruits are harvested and dried whole, producing the round, ridged seed-fruit that is the traded form. Drying concentrates the warm, citrusy, linalool-driven character, and the material is used whole or ground like any seed spice. For essential-oil use, the seed is steam-distilled, but the spice itself needs no processing beyond drying. The whole seed retains its aroma far longer than pre-ground powder — a practical point, since the seed's linalool-driven note degrades faster once ground.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Coriander seed quality turns on the freshness and intensity of its citrusy, linalool aroma. Good seed is round, dry, and strongly warm-citrusy on crushing, without a stale or musty note; the whole seed retains its aroma far longer than ground powder.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **aroma intensity** (a strong, clean, warm-citrusy note on crushing — the linalool character — not a stale or musty edge), **freshness** (round, dry, sound seed rather than shriveled or old material), and **form** (whole seed retains its aroma far longer than ground powder). The crush test is the standard quality check, and whole seed is the reference form precisely because the volatile linalool escapes quickly once ground.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- Whole coriander seed should be round, dry, and fragrant with a warm, citrusy, slightly floral aroma; ground seed loses scent faster.
- Distinguish the seed (芫荽籽, warm and citrusy) from the leaf herb (芫荽 / 香菜, fresh and green) — different aromatics from the same plant.
- In incense, coriander seed is a fresh spice accent, usually blended, not burned alone.
- Specify "seed" explicitly — a label saying only "coriander" does not separate the seed from the leaf.

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, coriander is most interesting for the clean split it enacts within a single species. Three points follow:

- **The seed and leaf are effectively opposite aromatics.** The seed is warm, citrusy, and woody — driven by linalool — while the leaf is fresh, green, and polarizing (a soapy note many people perceive).
