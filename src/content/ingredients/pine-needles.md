> **Direct answer:** Pine needles (松针, sōngzhēn) are the long, slender leaves of pine trees (*Pinus* spp.). They carry the bright, conifer-fresh scent of a crushed evergreen branch, and are used in incense and traditional 香囊 (fragrant sachets) as a green, outdoorsy top note. They come from the very same tree that yields pine resin (松香/松脂) — same species, different part — and the two must be kept distinct.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles have a sharp, clean, conifer-fresh scent — the resinous-green smell of crushed pine foliage, brighter and more "green" than the warmer, balsamic note of the tree's resin. They read like a fresh evergreen branch or a winter forest, and work as a top note that lifts heavier resins and woods.

松针 has a sharp, clean, conifer-fresh scent — the resinous-green smell of crushed pine foliage, brighter and more "green" than the warmer, balsamic note of the tree's resin (松香). It reads like a fresh evergreen branch or a winter forest, and it works as a top note in blends, lifting heavier resins and woods with a crisp, outdoorsy edge. The same botanical identity underlies both the needle and resin entries here.

Pine trees (*Pinus*, family Pinaceae) are evergreen conifers native across the Northern Hemisphere, with many species in China — among them masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*), a common source of both resin and the aromatic needles used in Chinese tradition. The genus is recorded in Flora of China; the same botanical identity underlies both the needle and resin entries here. The genus-level identity is important: "pine needle" is not a single species but a leaf type across many *Pinus* species. For sourcing, the species matters less than the part — the fresh needle versus the hardened resin — but a buyer who wants a specific regional or species character should pin it down.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 松针 means "pine needle" — 松 (pine) + 针 (needle/pin), a literal description of the long, slender, needle-like leaves. The name specifies the part, which is exactly what distinguishes the needle from the tree's resin (松香) and seeds (松实).

The name 松针 (sōngzhēn) is literal: 松 "pine" + 针 "needle" — the needle-shaped leaves of the pine. Like several other part-names in this encyclopedia, it does the disambiguation work by specifying the *part*: the needle is not the resin (松香/松脂) nor the seed (松实) nor the node (松节), all of which the *Bencao Gangmu* records as separate parts of the same tree. The name is a part-specifier, and it is the first line of defense against confusing the fresh green needle with the warm balsamic resin.

## The 松针 / 松香 distinction (read this)

> **Direct answer:** 松针 and 松香 come from the same tree, different parts: 松针 is the long, slender evergreen needle (fresh, green, conifer-like, a top note); 松香 is the hardened oleoresin (warmer, balsamic, refined as rosin). The *Bencao Gangmu* treats them as separate parts of the pine.

松针 and 松香 come from the **same tree, different parts**:

- **松针 (pine needles)** — the long, slender evergreen leaves of *Pinus* spp. This entry.
- **松香 (pine resin)** — the hardened oleoresin of the same tree, refined as rosin/colophony. Its own entry, at [pine-resin](/ingredients/pine-resin/).

The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), tree section (木之一), records the pine under 松, distinguishing its parts: "松葉、松實，服餌所須；松節、松心，耐久不朽。松脂則又樹之津液精華也" — the needles and seeds are taken for consumption, the nodes and heartwood endure, while the resin (松脂) is the tree's vital essence. **This page describes 松针 = the needles, not the resin.** The distinction is aromatic as much as botanical: fresh green top versus warm balsamic base.

## Where do pine needles come from?

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles come from pine trees across the Northern Hemisphere, with masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*) a common Chinese source of both the resin and the aromatic needles. They are an abundant, renewable leaf material, unlike the slower-forming resin.

Pine needles are among the most abundant and renewable of aromatic materials — the evergreen foliage of a widespread genus, available wherever pines grow, from northern forests to the hills of southern China. Masson pine (*Pinus massoniana*) is a common Chinese source of both the resin and the aromatic needles. This abundance and renewability distinguish the needle from the resin: the needle is a continuous, low-value leaf crop, while the resin is a slower, higher-value oleoresin. For sourcing, the needle is an inexpensive, renewable green note.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles are processed simply — gathered and dried, the drying concentrating their fresh, conifer scent. They are used as dried foliage in sachets, or (less commonly) ground into blends; no distillation is involved in the leaf form, unlike the resin's refining.

The processing is minimal: the needles are gathered and dried, which concentrates their fresh, conifer-green scent. In the sachet tradition they are used as dried foliage, packed whole into cloth. For a blend they could be ground, but their primary documented form is the dried needle. There is no distillation step for the leaf itself — the essential oil (pine needle oil) is a separate, more refined product, distinct from the simple dried foliage that the sachet tradition uses.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality in pine needles turns on freshness — the bright, conifer-green scent should be clean and intact, not musty or faded — plus cleanliness and dryness. Because the needle is a fresh green note, staleness is the main failure mode.

The quality points are:

1. **Freshness of scent** — the bright, conifer-green character should be clean and sharp; a musty or faded scent signals old or poorly dried material.
2. **Cleanliness** — free of dirt, insects, and debris.
3. **Dryness** — properly dried, since moisture invites must and degrades the fresh note.

These are freshness and preparation checks, consistent with a material whose value is a volatile, green top note. The needle is a low-cost material, so the buyer's concern is less grading and more "is it fresh and clean."

## Common forms

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** A buyer should confirm it is the needle (松针), not the resin (松香), expect a fresh, conifer-green scent, and check freshness and dryness. The part-specification is the core check, because the same tree yields two very different aromatic materials.

For a sourcing buyer, the checks are:

- **Part** — 松针 is the needle; distinguish it from 松香 (pine resin), the same tree's warmer, balsamic resin with its own incense role.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Pine needles are a clean case study in a "same tree, opposite ends" principle: one botanical identity supplies both a fresh, volatile top note (the needle) and a warm, tenacious base (the resin), and the buyer's job is to know which end of the tree's fragrance they are buying. The part, not the tree, determines the aromatic function.

From a sourcing perspective, pine needles illustrate a recurring aromatic-structure point. Three observations follow:

- **One tree, opposite ends of the fragrance.** The needle is the fresh, green, volatile top; the resin is the warm, balsamic, tenacious base. They are not substitutes — they are two ends of the same tree's aromatic range, and they perform opposite functions in a blend. The needle lifts; the resin anchors.
- **The part is the spec.** "Pine" alone specifies nothing aromatic; "松针" (needle) versus "松香" (resin) specifies everything. This is the part-specification discipline again, and it matters here because the two pine materials are so easily lumped together under the word "pine."
- **Renewability shapes the market.** The needle is an abundant, renewable, low-value leaf crop; the resin is a slower, higher-value oleoresin. This abundance is why the needle is a humble sachet herb while the resin is a refined commodity — and why the needle's commercial reality is freshness and cleanliness, not grading.

In our view, pine needles are best understood as the **fresh, renewable top note of the pine** — a green, outdoorsy accent whose value lies in brightness and freshness, and whose correct specification is the part-name (松针), never the generic "pine." The tree's resin does the base work; the needle does the top.
