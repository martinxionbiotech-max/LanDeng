> **Direct answer:** Sweetgum (枫香, fēngxiāng) is the aromatic resin of the sweetgum tree, *Liquidambar formosana* (family Altingiaceae). It has a sweet, balsamic, warm aroma and is used in incense and perfumery, where it is closely related to (but distinct from) the imported storax (苏合香). The resin is properly called 枫香脂 (fēngxiāngzhī), and classical sources note that it was used both on its own and to adulterate frankincense.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Sweetgum resin has a sweet, warm, balsamic scent with a resinous depth — soft and comforting, in the same broad family as benzoin but with its own character. It reads as a sweet base or supporting note rather than a bright top, rounding out heavier woods and resins rather than leading a blend.

The classical *Xiangcheng* (香乘) gives a precise pointer to its character: 枫香 is so close in appearance to frankincense (乳香) that Li Shizhen noted the two are told apart only by burning — which tells us the material reads as a warm, resinous, frankincense-adjacent note rather than as a sharp or floral one.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Liquidambar formosana* is the Chinese sweetgum, a tree of the sweetgum family (Altingiaceae, formerly placed in Hamamelidaceae). Flora of China describes a tree to 30 m tall with a trunk to 1 m in diameter, palmately three-lobed leaves, and a March–June flowering season; its wounded bark yields the balsamic resin 枫香脂.

*Liquidambar formosana* is the Chinese sweetgum, a tree of the sweetgum family (Altingiaceae, historically placed in Hamamelidaceae). Flora of China describes it precisely: **trees to 30 m tall, the trunk sometimes 1 m in diameter**, with gray-brown bark; leaf blades **broadly ovate, palmately three-lobed and three-veined**, with a glandular-serrate margin; male inflorescences in short spikes, female inflorescences 24–43-flowered; infructescence globose, 3–4 cm wide; flowering **March–June**, fruiting July–September. It grows in sunny places, near villages, and in montane forests at **500–800 m**, from Anhui, Fujian, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hainan, Hubei, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, and Zhejiang south to Korea, Laos, and northern Vietnam. ## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 枫香 names the tree and, by extension, its fragrant resin — but the resin carries its own precise term, 枫香脂 (fēngxiāngzhī). The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records an older alternative name, 白胶香 ("white gum fragrance"), giving the resin a distinct identity from the tree.

The terminology needs a small precision:

- **枫香 (fēngxiāng)** — the tree, and loosely the material.
- **枫香脂 (fēngxiāngzhī)** — the resin, the actual fragrant substance.
- **白胶香 (báijiāoxiāng)** — "white gum fragrance," an older name the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records for 枫香脂, and which the *Golden Light Sutra* renders in transliteration as 须萨析罗婆.

Because the tree and the resin share the name 枫香, a buyer should pin down whether a label means the wood or the resin — almost always it is the resin (枫香脂), which is the part with commercial fragrance value.

## The storax / sweetgum distinction (buyer intelligence)

> **Direct answer:** "Sweetgum" resin and "storax" are often confused because both come from *Liquidambar* trees — but they are different species from different regions. 枫香脂 is *Liquidambar formosana* (the native Chinese sweetgum); storax (苏合香) is *Liquidambar orientalis* (the Oriental sweetgum of Asia Minor). They are related resins from related trees, not the same product.

Two *Liquidambar* resins sit side by side and are routinely conflated:

- **枫香脂 (sweetgum resin)** — *Liquidambar formosana*, the native Chinese sweetgum. This entry.
- **苏合香 / storax** — *Liquidambar orientalis*, the Oriental sweetgum of Asia Minor, the classical *imported* balsam (see [styrax-resin](/ingredients/styrax-resin/)).

They are related resins from related trees in the same family (Altingiaceae), but not the same product: one is the native Chinese resin, the other the imported storax. When a label says "sweetgum" or "storax," the species matters for the exact aroma, origin, and price.

## The frankincense-adulteration record (香乘考据)

> **Direct answer:** The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records a sharp practical fact: 枫香 was used to adulterate frankincense (乳香), and Li Shizhen noted the two are told apart only by burning. Both 枫香 and pine resin could pass for frankincense, but 枫香 was slightly whitish-yellow — so the burn test was the discriminating check.

The most useful classical observation about 枫香 is a supply-chain warning, and it reads like modern buyer intelligence:

- **枫香 fakes frankincense.** The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records, quoting Li Shizhen: "乳香，今人多以枫香杂之，惟烧时可辨" — today people adulterate frankincense with 枫香, and the two are told apart only by burning.
- **Both 枫香 and pine resin could fake it.** "枫香、松脂皆可乱乳香，但枫香微白黄色，烧之可见真" — both 枫香 and pine resin can pass for frankincense, but 枫香 is slightly whitish-yellow, and burning reveals the truth.

The same principle runs in both directions: 枫香 is close enough to frankincense to be used as an adulterant, which means a buyer should distinguish 枫香 from frankincense (乳香) on the one hand, and from the imported storax on the other. The burn test is the classical discriminator.

## Where does sweetgum come from?

> **Direct answer:** 枫香 resin comes from *Liquidambar formosana*, the native Chinese sweetgum, which grows across central and southern China and south to Korea, Laos, and northern Vietnam. Unlike storax (imported from Asia Minor), 枫香 is a native Chinese resin, which is part of its identity — and part of why it was cheaper and more available than the imported storax.

*Liquidambar formosana* grows natively across **Anhui, Fujian, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hainan, Hubei, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, and Zhejiang, and south to Korea, Laos, and northern Vietnam**, in sunny places, near villages, and in montane forests at 500–800 m. The resin (枫香脂) is tapped from the wounded bark. This native, widely available status contrasts with storax (苏合香, *Liquidambar orientalis*), which classical texts record as an *imported* resin from Central Asia — a difference that matters for price, availability, and identity.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** 枫香脂 is used in incense as a sweet-balsamic ingredient that adds warmth and roundness to 合香 blends, and as a supporting or fixative note that rounds out woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* records it in formulas, including a step where 枫香 is melted with cassia to blend the two resins.

枫香脂's role in incense is as a *sweet-balsamic warmth*: a supporting and fixative note that rounds out woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records it in actual formulas — for example "通明枫香二两，火上溶，间桂末一两，入香内搅匀" (two parts clear 枫香, melted over fire, with one part cassia powder stirred in) — showing it was handled as a meltable resin that could be blended warm with other aromatics. It adds warmth and roundness to a blend rather than leading it, and its closeness to frankincense made it both a substitute and (in the hands of the unscrupulous) an adulterant.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 枫香脂 is a resin tapped from the wounded bark of the sweetgum, hardening into tears or a soft mass. Processing is resin-tapping and, as the *Xiangcheng* shows, warm melting for blending — the resin is melted over fire and stirred into other aromatics rather than simply ground.

枫香脂 is a *resin*, obtained by tapping the wounded bark of the tree and letting the exudate harden. Processing therefore runs:

- **Tapping** — the bark is wounded, and the balsamic resin exudes and hardens into tears or a soft mass.
- **Warm melting** — the *Xiangcheng* records 枫香 being melted over fire and stirred into a blend with cassia ("通明枫香二两，火上溶，间桂末一两，入香内搅匀"), a warm-blending step suited to a meltable resin.

The key point is that 枫香脂 is a meltable balsamic resin, handled by tapping and warm blending, not by the grinding route of a wood or root. The wider resin-handling logic is in [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## What determines sweetgum quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on resin content and clarity — a clean, fragrant, balsamic resin rather than a woody or debris-laden one. The *Xiangcheng* records "通明枫香" (clear/translucent 枫香) as the good grade, and classical sources value the resin that reveals itself by burning; a pale, clean, aromatic resin beats a dark or woody one.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Resin content and clarity** — the aromatic value is in the balsamic resin; the *Xiangcheng* specifies "通明枫香" (clear 枫香) as the good grade.
2. **Aroma** — sweet, balsamic, warm; a rich, clean scent beats a flat or smoky one.
3. **Purity** — freedom from bark, wood, and debris; and correct identity (枫香脂 vs frankincense vs storax).
4. **Burn character** — the classical burn test discriminates 枫香 from frankincense, which is also a purity check.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder, but "clear, clean, and aromatic" is the durable signal.

## FAQ

**Is sweetgum the same as storax?** Closely related but not identical — sweetgum resin (枫香脂) comes from *Liquidambar formosana*, while storax (苏合香) comes from *Liquidambar orientalis*. They are related resins from related trees in the same family (Altingiaceae), which is the source of the confusion, but they are different products with distinct origins and, to an extent, different aromatic profiles and prices. When a label says "sweetgum" or "storax," the species matters for the exact aroma.

**Is sweetgum a wood or a resin?** In incense, "sweetgum" means the resin (枫香脂) tapped from the tree, not the wood itself. The sweetgum tree (*Liquidambar formosana*) yields a balsamic resin that is the actual incense and perfumery material, in the same broad family as storax and benzoin. When a label lists "sweetgum" without saying resin or wood, it almost always means the resin, since that is the part with commercial fragrance value.

**What does sweetgum smell like?** Sweet, warm, and balsamic with a resinous depth — a soft, comforting base note in the benzoin family. It reads as a sweet base or supporting note rather than a bright top, with a character that rounds out and warms heavier woods and resins. It sits in the same broad family as benzoin but carries its own distinct character rather than being a straight copy.

**Was sweetgum used to fake frankincense?** Yes. The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records, quoting Li Shizhen, that "乳香，今人多以枫香杂之，惟烧时可辨" — frankincense was often adulterated with 枫香, and the two were told apart only by burning. It also records that both 枫香 and pine resin could pass for frankincense, but 枫香 was slightly whitish-yellow, and burning revealed the truth. This is a 500-year-old supply-chain warning that still applies.

**What is sweetgum resin used for in incense?** It adds a sweet-balsamic warmth to blends and acts as a supporting/fixative note, rounding out woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* records it being melted over fire and stirred into a blend with cassia ("通明枫香二两，火上溶，间桂末一两，入香内搅匀"), showing it was handled as a meltable resin blended warm rather than simply ground.

**How do you burn sweetgum resin?** Like other resins — on a lit charcoal disc (in sand or ash) or gently on an electric incense heater, using small pieces at a time. The charcoal method is hotter and smokier; the electric heater is slower and lower in smoke. Burn in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep the resin stored away from flame and heat.

**What is the difference between 枫香 and 白胶香?** They are the same material under different names. 白胶香 ("white gum fragrance") is the older name for 枫香脂 (sweetgum resin) recorded in the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘), which also notes the *Golden Light Sutra* transliteration 须萨析罗婆. The tree is 枫香, and the resin is 枫香脂 or, classically, 白胶香.
