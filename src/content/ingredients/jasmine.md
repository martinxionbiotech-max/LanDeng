**Data summary:** Jasmine — *mòlì* (茉莉, also 茉莉花 *mòlìhuā*) in Chinese — is a sweet-scented white flower, most classically *Jasminum sambac*, used in Chinese tea, perfume, and incense. Jasmine tea is considered one of China's oldest scented-flower teas.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine is intensely sweet, floral, and slightly heady — richer than a light floral. The tea jasmine (*J. sambac*) reads fresh and tea-like, while the perfumery jasmine (*J. grandiflorum*) is more opulent and almost indolic in natural absolute form.

Jasmine has an intensely sweet, floral, slightly heady scent — one of the most recognizable floral notes in Chinese fragrance. In incense it is used to add a sweet, romantic floral lift.

It is richer and "heavier" than a light floral: where chrysanthemum is dry and cool and osmanthus is fruity-apricot, jasmine is **opulent and white-floral**, with a warm, almost indolic sweetness in its natural absolute form (though commercial jasmine notes are usually rendered as a cleaner, brighter accord). In a blend it pushes the scent toward "romantic" and "lush."

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine is the flower of *Jasminum* species in the olive family (Oleaceae). The classic Chinese tea-and-fragrance jasmine is *Jasminum sambac*; the Western perfume jasmine is *Jasminum grandiflorum*. They are two species of the same genus with different scent character.

"Jasmine" spans many *Jasminum* species, all in the olive family (Oleaceae). The two a buyer meets most often:

- ***Jasminum sambac*** — the small, white, tea-scented jasmine; the classic Chinese tea-and-fragrance species.
- ***Jasminum grandiflorum*** — the Spanish or Poet's jasmine, larger-flowered and headier; the classic Western perfumery jasmine and the source of much jasmine absolute.

The scent differs accordingly: *J. sambac* is the sweet, tea-scented jasmine; *J. grandiflorum* is the more common Western perfume jasmine. A label saying "jasmine" without a species is underspecified. ## Chinese name and terminology (茉莉 vs 素馨)

> **Direct answer:** Chinese 茉莉 (mòlì) is *Jasminum sambac*, the tea jasmine, while 素馨 (sùxīn) is *Jasminum grandiflorum*, the perfumery jasmine. Both are recorded as separate flowers in the incense compendium 香乘, and each has its own entry here.

Two *Jasminum* species sit at the centre of Chinese and global jasmine, and Chinese keeps them distinct:

- **茉莉 (mòlì)** — *Jasminum sambac*, the tea-scented jasmine. This entry.
- **素馨 (sùxīn)** — *Jasminum grandiflorum*, the Spanish/Poet's jasmine, the headier perfumery species. See [jasmine-grandiflorum](/ingredients/jasmine-grandiflorum/).

The classical record lists both — 香乘 names 素馨 and 茉莉 as distinct flowers (for example, in 心字香 and in the ten-flower recipe 逗情香). When a source says 素馨 it means *J. grandiflorum*; when it says 茉莉 it means *J. sambac*.

## Where does jasmine come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Jasminum sambac* is a South/Southeast Asian species long cultivated across southern China — Guangxi and Fujian are the classic jasmine-tea regions — while *J. grandiflorum* is native to South Asia and grown across warm regions for perfumery.

Jasmine's origin splits by species:

- ***Jasminum sambac*** — native to South and Southeast Asia, and cultivated for centuries across southern China, where Guangxi and Fujian became the classic jasmine-tea producing regions.
- ***Jasminum grandiflorum*** — native to South Asia, long cultivated across warm regions worldwide as the perfumery jasmine.

For tea, the flower is grown in vast fields and picked at night, just as the buds are about to open, when the scent is strongest — a timing that runs through the whole jasmine-tea trade.

## In Chinese tradition

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine's signature use is jasmine tea (茉莉花茶), made by layering fresh flowers with tea leaves so the leaves absorb the scent — a process called 窨制 (scenting). The flower also carries associations of purity and love and appears in perfume and incense.

- **Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶):** traditionally said to be China's oldest scented-flower tea, developed around the Song dynasty (960–1279). The classic method layers fresh jasmine flowers with tea leaves so the leaves absorb the scent (traditionally the flowers are then removed). The technique is called 窨制 (scenting/aromatizing), and fine jasmine tea may be scented in multiple passes.
- **Symbolism:** jasmine carries associations of purity, elegance, beauty, and love; it appears in weddings and festivals.
- **Fragrance:** used in perfume and incense.

## Why is jasmine used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine appears in incense as a sweet, romantic floral lift, almost always as an accord or blended with a wood/resin base. True jasmine absolute is expensive and delicate, so the note is usually rebuilt; jasmine is more common in modern and floral-forward blends than in the classical canon.

Jasmine appears in incense as a **floral note**, almost always as an accord or blended with a wood/resin base, because true jasmine absolute is expensive and delicate. It is more common in modern and floral-forward blends than in the classical wood-and-resin canon. Jasmine is also used to make jasmine absolute and concrete for perfumery.

## How is jasmine processed?

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine is processed in two paths: fresh flowers are layered with tea leaves for scenting (窨制), while the flowers are solvent-extracted to make jasmine concrete and absolute for perfumery. Historically, delicate flowers were also captured by enfleurage in fat.

Jasmine's processing splits by end use:

- **Tea scenting** — freshly picked buds, chosen just before they open, are layered with tea leaves (usually green tea) so the leaves absorb the volatile aroma. After each pass the spent flowers are traditionally removed, and premium tea is scented through several passes.
- **Concrete and absolute** — the flowers are solvent-extracted to yield jasmine concrete, then the absolute used in perfumery. Jasmine absolute is costly because the flowers are picked by hand and yield little extract.
- **Enfleurage (historical)** — a traditional method of capturing delicate flower scent in fat, now largely replaced by solvent extraction.

## What determines jasmine quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality rests on species (sambac vs grandiflorum), flower freshness, and picking timing — buds picked just before opening carry the strongest scent. For the tea, the number of scenting passes (窨制) sets the grade; for the absolute, purity is the issue.

The main quality factors:

1. **Species** — *J. sambac* (tea jasmine) vs *J. grandiflorum* (perfume jasmine); a different material, not a grade.
2. **Picking timing** — buds picked just before opening, at night, carry the strongest scent.
3. **Scenting passes** — for jasmine tea, the number of 窨制 passes sets the grade; more passes mean a deeper aroma.
4. **Purity** — for the absolute, the price should match the claimed purity; genuine jasmine absolute is never cheap.

## Common forms

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine is sold as fresh flowers (for tea scenting), jasmine concrete and absolute (for perfumery), and finished jasmine tea. In incense, the "jasmine" note is usually an accord rather than the raw flower.

| Form | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Fresh flowers | Tea scenting (窨制) |
| Jasmine concrete / absolute | Perfumery |
| Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶) | The finished, scented tea |
| Jasmine accord | Incense and fragrance (reconstructed note) |

The distinction between the raw flower, the extract, and the tea is functional — the same flower at different stages of processing.

## Jasmine vs related white blossoms

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine (茉莉, *Jasminum sambac*) is one of several white, fragrant flowers of southern China, and it is distinct from both 素馨 (*Jasminum grandiflorum*) and 含笑 (*Michelia figo*). Jasmine and 素馨 are jasmines (olive family); 含笑 is a magnolia-family flower, unrelated despite a similar "white fragrant blossom" role.

Three white, fragrant southern flowers are easy to blur:

- **茉莉 (jasmine)** — *Jasminum sambac* (Oleaceae), the tea jasmine. This entry.
- **素馨 (Spanish jasmine)** — *Jasminum grandiflorum* (Oleaceae), the headier perfumery jasmine. See [jasmine-grandiflorum](/ingredients/jasmine-grandiflorum/).
- **含笑 (banana shrub)** — *Michelia figo* (Magnoliaceae), a sweet, fruity, banana-scented flower that is unrelated to jasmine despite sharing a "small white fragrant blossom" role. See [michelia-figo](/ingredients/michelia-figo/).

The first two are the same genus; the third is a different family entirely. Chinese names keep all three separate.

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The two risks are naming — "jasmine" without a species is underspecified, since 茉莉 (*J. sambac*) and 素馨 (*J. grandiflorum*) differ — and, for the absolute, substitution: genuine jasmine absolute is expensive, and cheaper synthetic or extended "jasmine" notes are common.

Jasmine's risks mirror rose's:

- **Naming** — a label saying only "jasmine" is underspecified; 茉莉 (*J. sambac*) and 素馨 (*J. grandiflorum*) are different jasmines with different scent character.
- **Absolute substitution** — genuine jasmine absolute is costly, so cheaper synthetic jasmine notes (built on benzyl acetate and related molecules) or extended naturals are common. This is legitimate when labeled; it is a problem only when sold as natural absolute.

## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Jasmine serves scented tea (its largest single use), perfumery (as concrete and absolute), and incense (as a floral accord). The fresh flower scents tea; the extract serves fine fragrance; "jasmine" in incense is usually a rebuilt note.

- **Tea** — jasmine tea (茉莉花茶) is one of the great scented teas of China.
- **Perfumery** — jasmine concrete and absolute are prized natural fragrance materials.
- **Incense** — "jasmine" appears as a floral accord over a wood or resin base.
- **Aromatics** — jasmine notes appear widely in candles, room fragrance, and personal-care products.

## What buyers should look for

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

## Our Industry View

From a manufacturing perspective, jasmine is a floral whose **market form diverges from its natural form**, exactly as rose does. The flower is real, but in incense the note is almost always rebuilt, because genuine jasmine absolute is too expensive and too delicate to be a practical stick ingredient.

Two points follow:

- **Species is the first specification.** 茉莉 (*J. sambac*) and 素馨 (*J. grandiflorum*) are different jasmines with different registers — the first tea-like and fresh, the second opulent and heady. A formula built on one will not reproduce if the source switches to the other.
- **The accord is not a defect, but it must be labeled.** A well-built jasmine accord is usually more stable and consistent than the natural extract, which varies by harvest and is prohibitively expensive. The requirement is honesty: natural or accord, stated plainly, with the price matching the claim.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **clearly labeled jasmine note — natural or accord — bought in a consistent form**, used as a romantic floral accent rather than a lead. The exact dosing **depends on formulation**.

## Summary

Jasmine (茉莉) is the flower of *Jasminum* species in the olive family — most classically *Jasminum sambac*, the tea jasmine, distinct from the perfumery *Jasminum grandiflorum* (素馨). It is the signature scent of jasmine tea (茉莉花茶), made by the 窨制 layering method, and it serves perfume and incense as a sweet, romantic floral — usually a reconstructed accord over a wood or resin base. ## FAQ

**Is jasmine the same as jasmine tea?** Jasmine is the flower; jasmine tea is tea scented with that flower. The classic method layers freshly picked jasmine blossoms with tea leaves, most often green tea, so the leaves absorb the fragrance. After each scenting pass the spent flowers are traditionally removed, so the finished tea carries the jasmine character while containing little or no flower matter. The technique is called 窨制, and premium jasmine tea is scented through several passes to deepen the aroma. In short, jasmine is the raw botanical material and jasmine tea is the finished, scented product — closely related but not interchangeable terms.

**Is jasmine a wood or a flower?** Jasmine is a flower, not a wood or resin. The classic Chinese tea and fragrance jasmine is *Jasminum sambac*, a small white flower in the olive family (Oleaceae); a different species, *Jasminum grandiflorum*, is the more common Western perfume jasmine. The blossoms are picked just before they fully open, when the scent is strongest, then used fresh for tea scenting or processed into absolute and concrete for fragrance. In incense, jasmine is almost always a floral accord layered over a wood or resin base, because true jasmine absolute is costly and delicate. There is no jasmine wood.

**What does jasmine incense smell like?** Jasmine incense carries an intensely sweet, floral, and slightly heady scent — one of the most recognizable floral notes in Chinese fragrance. It reads as opulent and white-floral, with a warm, almost indolic sweetness in its natural absolute form, though most commercial jasmine notes are rendered as a cleaner, brighter accord. In a blend it pushes the scent toward romantic and lush rather than dry or green. Because true jasmine absolute is expensive, jasmine incense usually layers a reconstructed jasmine accord over a wood or resin base, so the exact character varies by maker — some lean clean and bright, others richer and more full-bodied.

**Is jasmine used in Chinese incense?** Yes, jasmine appears in Chinese incense as a sweet, romantic floral lift, and it is usually blended rather than burned alone. True jasmine absolute is expensive and delicate, so the flower's scent is more often carried as an accord over a wood or resin base. Jasmine is more common in modern and floral-forward blends than in the classical wood-and-resin canon, which favors agarwood, sandalwood, and resins. The same flower also scents jasmine tea and supplies jasmine absolute and concrete for perfumery. A label that says only "jasmine" without a species, and without noting whether it is natural or an accord, is underspecified.

**What is the difference between jasmine and osmanthus?** Both are sweet Chinese florals, but they sit at different points on the spectrum. Jasmine is heady, opulent, and white-floral, with a warm, almost indolic sweetness; osmanthus is brighter and more delicate, with a fruity apricot-and-honey character. In a blend, jasmine romanticizes and adds richness, while osmanthus lifts and sweetens without the heaviness. They also come from different plants: jasmine is a *Jasminum* species, and osmanthus is *Osmanthus fragrans* — both in the olive family, but different genera. The two are often paired with rose as the classic trio of Chinese floral notes.

**How is jasmine tea made?** Jasmine tea is made by scenting tea leaves with fresh jasmine flowers through a process called 窨制. Freshly picked blossoms, chosen when the buds are about to open, are layered with tea leaves — most often green tea — so the leaves absorb the flower's volatile aroma. After a scenting pass, the spent flowers are traditionally removed, leaving the tea fragranced but largely flower-free. Premium jasmine tea goes through this cycle several times, each pass deepening the scent, which is why higher grades cost more. The result is a tea whose jasmine character comes from repeated contact with the living flower, not from added flavoring.

**What is the difference between jasmine and 素馨 (Spanish jasmine)?** Both are *Jasminum* species, but distinct. Jasmine (茉莉) is *Jasminum sambac* — the tea-scented jasmine used for jasmine tea, with a fresh, tea-like sweetness. 素馨 is *Jasminum grandiflorum* — the Spanish or Poet's jasmine, with larger flowers and a headier, more opulent scent, the classic Western perfumery jasmine. The incense compendium 香乘 lists them as separate flowers, and each has its own entry here. See [jasmine-grandiflorum](/ingredients/jasmine-grandiflorum/).
