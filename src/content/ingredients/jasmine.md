# Jasmine (茉莉 / Mo Li)

**Direct answer:** Jasmine — *mòlì* (茉莉, also 茉莉花 *mòlìhuā*) in Chinese — is a sweet-scented white flower, most classically *Jasminum sambac*, used in Chinese tea, perfume, and incense. Jasmine tea is considered one of China's oldest scented-flower teas.

## Aroma profile

Jasmine has an intensely sweet, floral, slightly heady scent — one of the most recognizable floral notes in Chinese fragrance. In incense it is used to add a sweet, romantic floral lift.

It is richer and "heavier" than a light floral: where chrysanthemum is dry and cool and osmanthus is fruity-apricot, jasmine is **opulent and white-floral**, with a warm, almost indolic sweetness in its natural absolute form (though commercial jasmine notes are usually rendered as a cleaner, brighter accord). In a blend it pushes the scent toward "romantic" and "lush."

## In Chinese tradition

- **Jasmine tea (茉莉花茶):** traditionally said to be China's oldest scented-flower tea, developed around the Song dynasty (960–1279). The classic method layers fresh jasmine flowers with tea leaves so the leaves absorb the scent (traditionally the flowers are then removed). The technique is called 窨制 (scenting/aromatizing), and fine jasmine tea may be scented in multiple passes.
- **Symbolism:** jasmine carries associations of purity, elegance, beauty, and love; it appears in weddings and festivals.
- **Fragrance:** used in perfume and incense.

## Botanical note

"Jasmine" spans many *Jasminum* species; the classic Chinese fragrance/tea jasmine is *Jasminum sambac* (a different species from common European jasmine). The scent differs accordingly: *J. sambac* is the sweet, tea-scented jasmine; *J. grandiflorum* (Spanish/Poet's jasmine) is the more common Western perfume jasmine. A label saying "jasmine" without a species is underspecified.

## In incense

Jasmine appears in incense as a **floral note**, almost always as an accord or blended with a wood/resin base, because true jasmine absolute is expensive and delicate. It is more common in modern and floral-forward blends than in the classical wood-and-resin canon. Jasmine is also used to make jasmine absolute and concrete for perfumery.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Jasmine is used as a tea, fragrance, and traditional materia medica ingredient. We describe its aromatic and cultural use; we do **not** make medical claims.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke.

## FAQ

**Is jasmine the same as jasmine tea?** Jasmine is the flower; jasmine tea is tea scented with the flower (traditionally the flowers are removed after scenting).

**Is jasmine a wood or a flower?** A flower — *Jasminum sambac* (and other *Jasminum* species), used dried in tea, perfume, and incense.

## People Also Ask (Long-Tail)

**What does jasmine incense smell like?** Intensely sweet, floral, and slightly heady — one of the most recognizable floral notes in Chinese fragrance.

**What is the difference between jasmine and osmanthus?** Both are sweet florals, but jasmine is heady and white-floral, while osmanthus is apricot- and honey-like. They read differently in blends.

**Is jasmine a wood or a flower?** A flower — *Jasminum sambac* (and other *Jasminum* species), used dried in tea, perfume, and incense.

**How is jasmine tea made?** By layering fresh jasmine flowers with tea leaves so the leaves absorb the scent (a process called 窨制), traditionally with the flowers removed afterward. Fine jasmine tea may be scented in several passes.

**When did jasmine tea develop?** Jasmine tea is traditionally said to be China's oldest scented-flower tea, developed around the Song dynasty (960–1279), when the practice of scenting tea with fresh flowers became established.

**When are jasmine flowers harvested?** For jasmine tea, the flowers are typically picked early in the day, when the buds are about to open and their scent is strongest, then layered with tea leaves for scenting.
