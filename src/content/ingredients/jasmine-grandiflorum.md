> **Direct answer:** Jasmine grandiflorum (素馨, sùxīn) is the flower of *Jasminum grandiflorum*, the Spanish or royal jasmine, a climbing shrub of the olive family (Oleaceae) with large, intensely fragrant white flowers. It is the classic Western perfumery jasmine — the source of much jasmine absolute — and is recorded in the Chinese compendium 香乘 alongside its close relative 茉莉 (*Jasminum sambac*). The two jasmines are distinct species.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 素馨 is intensely sweet and white-floral, with a heady, almost indolic depth that is richer than 茉莉's tea-like sweetness. In natural absolute form it carries a warm, animalic undercurrent beneath the green-floral top; commercial jasmine notes are usually rendered cleaner and brighter.

In a blend it pushes the scent toward *opulent and romantic*, where 茉莉 reads fresher and more delicate.

This is the sensory core of the 素馨/茉莉 distinction, and it is why the two flowers, though both sweet white jasmines, serve different fragrance registers.

## The 素馨 / 茉莉 distinction (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Two *Jasminum* species sit at the centre of Chinese and global jasmine: 素馨 (*Jasminum grandiflorum*), the headier Western perfumery jasmine, and 茉莉 (*Jasminum sambac*), the fresher tea-scented jasmine. They are distinct species, and 香乘 lists them together as separate flowers.

Two *Jasminum* species sit at the centre of Chinese and global jasmine:

- **茉莉** (*Jasminum sambac*) — the tea-scented jasmine, the classic Chinese tea-and-fragrance jasmine. Its own entry.

This distinction is the page's core value: "jasmine" in English is not one plant, and a label saying only "jasmine" underspecifies which species — the heady perfumery jasmine (素馨) or the fresh tea jasmine (茉莉). The classical 香乘 record is itself evidence of the distinction, listing the two flowers side by side as separate materials.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Jasminum grandiflorum* is a scrambling evergreen shrub of the olive family (Oleaceae), native to South Asia and long cultivated across warm regions, including southern China. Its flowers are larger than those of *J. sambac*, white, and powerfully fragrant, opening at night. It is the principal source of commercial jasmine absolute.

Its flowers are larger than those of *J. sambac*, white, and powerfully fragrant, opening at night. Flora of China records the species (素馨花) as a scandent shrub 2–4 m, with pinnately compound leaves and white, salverform flowers, noting it is native to Arabia and widely cultivated in warm and tropical countries for its fragrant flowers. It is the principal source of commercial jasmine absolute and concrete for Western perfumery — the "jasmine" of fine fragrance in a way 茉莉 is not.

## Chinese name and terminology

The name 素馨 (also 素馨花) combines 素 ("plain, white, unadorned") with 馨 ("fragrance") — a name evoking the white flower's pure, unadorned scent. ## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 素馨's flowers are processed by picking the freshly opened blooms and, for perfumery, extracting them into jasmine absolute or concrete by solvent extraction; the classical Chinese method was steaming the flowers to capture their scent (蒸香). The flowers are delicate and must be picked at the right moment for their scent.

For perfumery, the freshly opened 素馨 flowers are picked and extracted — by solvent extraction into jasmine absolute or concrete, since the flowers' scent is too delicate for steam distillation. The classical Chinese method was different: sources describe steaming 素馨 and other fragrant flowers to capture their scent (蒸香), a gentler approach suited to the flower's fragility. The practical point is the flower's delicacy: 素馨 blooms must be picked at the right moment (just opened, at night or early morning) for their full scent, and the extraction method must not heat the flowers destructively.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** 素馨 quality turns on the intensity and richness of the flower's scent — a full, heady, white-floral character with the indolic depth that defines the perfumery jasmine. In absolute form, the quality is judged on the purity and richness of the floral note, not on the raw flower's appearance alone.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **scent intensity** (a full, heady, sweet white-floral character with the characteristic indolic depth), **richness** (the opulent, almost animalic undertone that separates 素馨 from the fresher 茉莉), and — for the absolute — **purity** (a clean floral note, not a synthetic or diluted substitute). Because 素馨's value is in its heady, indolic richness, a flat or merely "clean" jasmine note is likely the wrong material or a thinned product.

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, 素馨 is most interesting for the underspecification risk that the word "jasmine" itself creates. Three points follow:

- **"Jasmine" is a genus, not a material.** 素馨 (*Jasminum grandiflorum*) and 茉莉 (*Jasminum sambac*) are different species with different scent characters, and the English word "jasmine" collapses them. For a buyer, this means the "jasmine" that arrives too clean and bright may be a thinned or reformulated product, not the full 素馨 absolute. The richness of the indolic note is the quality signal.
