> **Direct answer:** Mustard seed (芥子, jièzǐ) is the pungent seed of mustards in the cabbage family (Brassicaceae): white mustard (*Sinapis alba*) and brown or leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*). It is a sharp, spicy culinary seed whose heat is released only when the ground seed meets water. The *Bencao Gangmu* records 芥 in its vegetable section, noting the white mustard seed (白芥子) is "甚辛美" — very pungent and fine.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Mustard seed is pungent and sharp, with a hot, green, horseradish-like bite that appears only when the crushed seed is moistened — dry, the seed is almost scentless. The heat is a chemical defense released on wetting, not a lingering spice warmth like pepper or cinnamon.

Mustard seed is pungent and sharp, with a hot, green, horseradish-like bite that appears only when the crushed seed is moistened — dry, the seed is almost scentless. The heat is a chemical defense (the enzyme myrosinase acting on glucosinolates), not a lingering spice warmth like pepper or cinnamon. It is a food aroma first, and its role in aromatics is as a sharp, corrective seasoning rather than a lead fragrance.

This is a genuinely unusual trait among the spice seeds: most spices — pepper, cinnamon, clove — release a stored, pre-formed aroma when crushed or heated. Mustard's heat does not exist until the crushed seed meets water. The dry seed is nearly inert, and the "pungency" is a reaction that the moisture unlocks. This chemical fact is the key to understanding both how mustard works in the kitchen and why it behaves differently from every other spice seed on this site.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** Mustard covers several cabbage-family (Brassicaceae) plants. The two most relevant to 芥子 are white mustard (*Sinapis alba*), native to the Mediterranean and West Asia, and brown or leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*), long cultivated across Asia for both its pungent seed and its leafy vegetable form (芥菜). The seed is the material of value.

The white-vs-brown distinction is practical, not pedantic: white mustard (*Sinapis alba*) yields the milder, paler, classic table mustard; brown/leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*) is the hotter seed behind many Asian mustards and the leafy vegetable 芥菜. Both carry the 芥 name, and both feed the 芥子 material, but their heat, color, and culinary roles differ.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Mustard seed is harvested and dried, then ground — sometimes after roasting — and mixed with liquid to develop its heat. The wetting step is essential: the enzyme myrosinase acts on glucosinolates only in the presence of water, releasing the sharp, hot bite. Dry, the seed is almost scentless.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Mustard quality turns on the seed's pungency potential and freshness. Good seed, when freshly ground and moistened, releases a strong, sharp, clean heat — not a stale or musty note. Because the heat is wet-activated, the "quality test" is really a fresh-grind-and-wet test.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **pungency potential** (a strong, sharp, clean heat when freshly ground and moistened — not a stale or weak bite) and **freshness** (dry, sound seed rather than old or musty material). Because the heat is wet-activated, the quality test is inherently a *fresh-grind-and-wet* test: you cannot judge a mustard seed by sniffing it dry, because the seed is almost scentless until ground and moistened. This is a distinctive quality-checking quirk among the spice seeds.

## Mustard vs pepper: the wet-activated heat

> **Direct answer:** Mustard and pepper are both "hot," but by different mechanisms. Pepper's pungency is a pre-formed, oil-soluble bite (piperine) released on crushing; mustard's heat is wet-activated — the enzyme myrosinase acts on glucosinolates only in water. One is a stored heat, the other a triggered reaction.

| | Mustard seed (芥子) | Pepper (胡椒) |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Sinapis alba* / *Brassica juncea* (Brassicaceae) | *Piper nigrum* (Piperaceae) |
| Heat mechanism | Wet-activated (myrosinase + glucosinolates) | Pre-formed (piperine) |
| Dry state | Nearly scentless | Aromatic even dry |
| Character | Sharp, green, horseradish-like | Hot, pungent, woody |

The contrast is instructive: pepper's heat is stored and released by crushing; mustard's heat is a reaction that water must trigger. See [pepper](/ingredients/pepper/), [cinnamon](/ingredients/cinnamon/), and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Mustard is a "latent-pungency" case — its heat does not exist until water triggers the myrosinase–glucosinolate reaction, which makes it the one spice seed you cannot judge by sniffing dry. This chemical quirk, combined with the *Bencao Gangmu* placement in the vegetable section rather than the incense canon, defines mustard as a culinary 香药, not a burned material.

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, mustard is most interesting for the mismatch between its reputation ("hot") and its dry reality ("nearly scentless"). Three points follow:

- **The heat is a reaction, not a stored aroma.** Mustard's pungency is produced on demand — myrosinase acting on glucosinolates in the presence of water. This is a genuinely different model from pepper, cinnamon, or clove, whose aromas are pre-formed. For a buyer, the practical consequence is that the standard "crush-and-sniff" quality check fails on mustard: the seed must be ground *and* wetted to reveal its character, which is why mustard is uniquely judged by a wet test.
- **The *Bencao Gangmu* placement is itself the category signal.** 芥 sits in the vegetable section (菜之一) — the food-and-cultivation layer — not in the incense or aromatic-wood layers. This placement is not incidental: mustard was understood as a cultivated food plant and seasoning 香药, not as a 香材. An honest knowledge resource should preserve that placement rather than promote mustard into a burned-incense role it never had.
- **"Sharp" and "hot" are not interchangeable across the spice table.** Mustard's green, horseradish-like bite is a different register from pepper's woody pungency or cinnamon's sweet warmth. The three are routinely grouped as "spices," but they operate by different chemistry and serve different culinary functions. Collapsing them into one "hot spice" category discards exactly the information a formulator or buyer needs.

In our view, mustard is best understood as a **wet-activated culinary 香药 whose heat is a triggered chemical reaction** — a cabbage-family seed that is nearly inert dry, sharp when ground and moistened, and correctly placed in the 本草 vegetable record rather than in the incense canon.

## FAQ

**What is 芥子 (mustard seed)?** The pungent seed of mustards in the cabbage family (Brassicaceae) — chiefly white mustard (*Sinapis alba*) and brown or leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*). It is a sharp, spicy culinary seed whose heat is released only when the crushed seed meets water, and it is used as a seasoning 香药 rather than a burned incense material.

**Why does dry mustard seed have little smell?** Because its pungency is a chemical defense, not a stored aroma. The enzyme myrosinase reacts with glucosinolates only when the crushed seed is moistened, producing the sharp, hot, horseradish-like bite. **What is the difference between white and brown mustard?** White mustard (*Sinapis alba*) has a milder, more evenly pungent seed and is the source of the classic pale table mustard; brown or leaf mustard (*Brassica juncea*) is hotter and is the seed behind many Asian mustards and the leafy vegetable 芥菜. Both contribute to the name 芥子, and they differ in heat, color, and culinary use.

**Is mustard a classical incense ingredient?** Not in the burned-incense tradition. Mustard's role is culinary — a sharp seasoning 香药 — rather than a material of 合香 (blended incense) or the 香乘 canon. Its aromatic value is as a hot, corrective food note, not a lead fragrance.

**How do I judge mustard seed quality?** By the fresh-grind-and-wet test, not by sniffing the dry seed. Good mustard seed, when freshly ground and moistened, releases a strong, sharp, clean heat; old or stale seed gives a weak or musty bite. Because the pungency is wet-activated, the dry seed is nearly scentless and cannot be judged by smell alone.
