## What is eupatorium?

> **Direct answer:** Eupatorium (佩兰, pèilán) is the dried aerial herb of *Eupatorium fortunei*, a daisy-family perennial native to East Asia. Its name — "worn orchid" — records its traditional job: the fragrant herb was carried in sachets and used to scent clothing rather than burned as a heavy incense. In a blend it reads as a light, fresh, green note.

Eupatorium is best understood through its cultural role rather than its chemistry. The plant is a medium-sized herbaceous perennial, and what is harvested and dried is the whole above-ground herb — leaves and stems — not a root, wood, seed, or resin. In the classical roster of Chinese scented herbs (香草), it sits alongside mugwort and agastache as a "green" aromatic: fresh, herbaceous, and mildly sweet, used to lift and brighten rather than to anchor.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Eupatorium fortunei* is a perennial herb of the Asteraceae (daisy) family. Flora of China records it at 40–100 cm tall, with procumbent reddish-brown rhizomes and stem leaves divided into three segments, topped by dense corymbs of small flower heads. The aromatic part used is the aerial herb.

Flora of China records *Eupatorium fortunei* as a **perennial herb 40–100 cm tall**, with procumbent, reddish-brown rhizomes and erect, green or reddish-purple stems. The median stem leaves are the distinctive feature: **3-sect or 3-partite**, with a large terminal lobe 5–10 × 1.5–2.5 cm and coarsely or irregularly toothed margins. The small flower heads (capitula) are borne in numerous apical compound corymbs. See [Eupatorium fortunei in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200023934) and [Eupatorium fortunei on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5403095).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 佩兰 (pèilán) means "worn orchid" — 佩 "to wear" + 兰 "orchid" — recording the herb's traditional use in sachets and clothing-scenting. The 兰 is cultural, not botanical: 佩兰 is a daisy-family herb, not an orchid, and it must be kept distinct from the same-genus 泽兰.

The name is a small etymology lesson that explains the herb's whole role. 佩兰 combines 佩 (pèi, "to wear at the belt") with 兰 (lán, "orchid"), and it belongs to a broad Chinese convention of calling many fragrant plants 兰 regardless of family. Two naming cautions follow:

- **佩兰 is not an orchid.** Despite the 兰 in its name, it is an Asteraceae herb, in the same tradition where 兰 names fragrance rather than taxonomy.
- **佩兰 ≠ 泽兰.** 泽兰 is the same-genus *Eupatorium japonicum* — a distinct species with a fresh, herbaceous scent, treated on its own page — and, in classical materia medica, 泽兰 often refers instead to the mint-family *Lycopus lucidus*. See [泽兰 (eupatorium-japonicum)](/ingredients/eupatorium-japonicum/).

## Where does eupatorium come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Eupatorium fortunei* is native to China, Japan, and Korea, where it grows in moist places. It is a wild-collected and small-scale-cultivated herb whose supply follows herb-trade patterns rather than a dedicated aromatic plantation industry.

*Eupatorium fortunei* is a herbaceous perennial native to China, Japan, and Korea, growing in moist, open, and semi-shaded situations. Unlike the tropical woods and resins of the incense trade, it is a temperate herb whose aromatic supply sits within the broader herb and materia-medica trade. This has a practical consequence: eupatorium is rarely a headline ingredient in its own right, and lots are more often identified by their Chinese materia-medica name than by a dedicated "incense grade."

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Eupatorium is fresh and herbaceous, with a gentle sweetness and a clean, lightly aromatic lift — a "green" note closer to a scented leaf than to a flower or wood. In traditional use it scents sachets and clothes, and in blends it reads as a light, fresh accent.

Eupatorium is fresh and herbaceous, with a gentle sweetness and a clean, slightly aromatic lift. It is a "green" note — closer to a scented-leaf character than to a flower or wood. Where mugwort reads camphoraceous and bitter, and agastache reads minty and sweet-anise, eupatorium reads **clean, mild, and softly sweet** — a note that brightens a blend rather than dominating it. In traditional use it scents sachets and clothes rather than being burned as a dominant incense note.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Eupatorium is used as a light, fresh "green" accent in incense blends, and — more centrally in Chinese tradition — as a sachet and clothing fragrance. Its job is to lift and freshen a formula rather than to carry it as a base or lead note.

Eupatorium occupies a modest but real niche in Chinese fragrance practice:

- **Incense (香):** as a fresh herbaceous accent, it lifts spice-forward and wood-forward blends with a clean, green brightness — a supporting note, never a base.
- **Sachet and clothing scent (佩/香囊):** its defining traditional role, and the origin of its name. The herb was worn and packed into sachets to scent garments, a non-burning aromatic use.

Because its scent is light, eupatorium is almost always **blended**, not burned alone; alone it offers a faint green note that is easily lost.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Eupatorium is processed by harvesting the aerial herb and drying it — whole or cut. There is no extraction or distillation step in the herb form; for incense blending it is then ground to powder, with particle size and moisture depending on the formula.

The essential step is simple: the above-ground herb (leaves and stems) is harvested and **dried** to a stable, storable form. From there it branches by end use — whole or cut herb for sachets and traditional use, or ground powder for incense blending. For powder buyers, the same practical variables as any leafy herb apply: **particle size and moisture** affect how the material packs, burns, and releases aroma, and the right target **depends on the formula** — the binder, other ingredients, and the production line. The grinding and forming mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process, and leafy-herb powder behavior is covered in [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## What determines eupatorium quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on cleanliness, colour, and aroma — dried eupatorium should be clean and green with a fresh, herbaceous scent when rubbed. There is no standardized numerical grading; the buyer evaluates the physical condition of the lot.

The practical quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Cleanliness** — freedom from stems, soil, and foreign herb material.
2. **Colour and freshness** — clean green material, not browned, faded, or musty.
3. **Aroma** — a fresh, herbaceous, mildly sweet scent when rubbed; stale material loses its lift.
4. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

## Eupatorium vs 泽兰 (related species)

> **Direct answer:** 佩兰 (*Eupatorium fortunei*) and 泽兰 (*Eupatorium japonicum*) are closely related but distinct species of the same daisy-family genus. Both are fresh, herbaceous aromatics; 佩兰 is the classical "worn orchid" sachet herb, while 泽兰 is a separate species — and the name 泽兰 is itself contested with the mint-family *Lycopus lucidus*.

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** Eupatorium is not a heavily faked aromatic in the way agarwood is; its risk is naming — confusion with the same-genus 泽兰 and with the classical 泽兰 of materia medica, which is a different, mint-family plant. The scientific name on the label resolves both.

Eupatorium is not an expensive, heavily faked material. Its risks are a **naming and identification matter**: 佩兰 (*Eupatorium fortunei*) must be distinguished from 泽兰 (*Eupatorium japonicum*), and both must be kept apart from the classical materia-medica 泽兰, which modern botanists identify as *Lycopus lucidus*, a mint-family herb. Specifying the scientific name — *Eupatorium fortunei* — is the practical fix.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, eupatorium is a light, fresh "green" accent, not a base. Its value is a clean herbal lift that brightens heavier blends — but because its scent is faint and easily lost, the dose and the blend context matter more than the nominal ingredient name.

From a manufacturing perspective, eupatorium's defining trait is **lightness**. It supplies a clean, fresh green note that heavier woods and resins cannot, but it will not carry a formula the way sandalwood or agarwood can.

Two points follow:

- **It is an accent, not a base.** Eupatorium brightens a blend but cannot anchor it. A buyer who expects it to lead a fragrance will be disappointed, while one who doses it as a fresh top accent gets real value.
- **Its faintness is the formulation variable.** Because the scent is light, the practical questions are dose and blend context — which itself **depends on formulation**, so a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another.

In our view, eupatorium's commercial value lies in its role as a **correctly identified, clean, fresh "green" accent** used to lift heavier blends — a supporting herb whose real value is character, not intensity.
