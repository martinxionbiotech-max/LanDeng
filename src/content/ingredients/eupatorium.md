**Technical answer:** Eupatorium — *pèilán* (佩兰) in Chinese — is the aromatic aerial part of *Eupatorium fortunei*, a herbaceous plant of the daisy family (Asteraceae) native to East Asia. It is a classical Chinese "fragrant herb" (香草) whose name literally means "worn orchid" — 佩 ("to wear") and 兰 ("orchid") — because the fragrant herb was traditionally worn in sachets and used to scent clothing. It is a fresh, herbaceous, mildly sweet aromatic used in incense, sachets, and traditional practice.

## Aroma profile

Eupatorium is fresh and herbaceous, with a gentle sweetness and a clean, slightly aromatic lift. It is a "green" note — closer to a scented-leaf character than to a flower or wood. In traditional use it scents sachets and clothes rather than being burned as a dominant incense note, and it reads as a light, fresh aromatic in blends.

## Botanical source and origin

*Eupatorium fortunei* is a herbaceous perennial of the Asteraceae family, native to China, Japan, and Korea, where it grows in moist places. The part used is the above-ground herb — leaves and stems — harvested and dried. The name 佩兰 captures the plant's cultural role: it was worn (佩) for its fragrance, in the same tradition as the "orchid" herbs of classical poetry.

> **A naming caution:** 佩兰 (*Eupatorium fortunei*) is not an orchid, despite the 兰 ("orchid") in its name. Chinese uses 兰 for many fragrant plants; 佩兰 is a daisy-family herb. It is also distinct from 泽兰 (*Lycopus lucidus*), a different aromatic herb with a similar-sounding name in traditional materia medica.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Scenting clothes and sachets:** the name 佩兰 and the classical "fragrant herb" (香草) tradition point to wearing the herb in sachets and using it to scent garments — a documented, non-medical aromatic role.
- **Fragrant-herb culture:** eupatorium belongs to the classical roster of scented herbs (香草) celebrated in Chinese poetry and ritual, alongside plants like 兰 and 艾.
- **Materia medica:** 佩兰 is recorded in traditional Chinese medicine as an aromatic herb. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The aromatic, sachet-and-clothing role is well documented. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity and the plant's long use as a fresh, herbaceous fragrance.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- Dried eupatorium should be clean and green, with a fresh, herbaceous scent when rubbed.
- Distinguish 佩兰 (*Eupatorium fortunei*) from 泽兰 (*Lycopus lucidus*) — a different aromatic herb with a similar name.
- As an incense ingredient, eupatorium is a light, fresh green note, usually blended rather than burned alone.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.
