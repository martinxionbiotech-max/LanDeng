**Technical answer:** Costus — *mùxiāng* (木香) in Chinese — is the dried root of *Saussurea costus* (now often *Dolomiaea costus*, also known by the older name *Aucklandia lappa*), a thistle-family herb (Asteraceae) native to the Himalaya. It is a classical Chinese incense and materia-medica root valued for a deep, earthy, slightly musky-animalic aroma that made it a traditional fixative and, historically, a lower-cost stand-in for musk. It is a CITES-listed species whose wild trade is restricted.

## Aroma profile

Costus root is earthy and woody at its base, with a musky, faintly animalic edge that deepens and persists — the quality that made it a traditional fixative and a lower-cost musk substitute. It is a *grounding* note, closer in spirit to spikenard or orris root than to a bright flower or spice. The aroma develops with gentle heat and lingers.

## Botanical source and origin

*Saussurea costus* is a herbaceous perennial of the Asteraceae (thistle/daisy) family, native to the high Himalaya — Kashmir, northern India, Nepal, and into Tibet. The part used is the thick, aromatic root. The plant's taxonomy has shifted: it has been known as *Aucklandia lappa*, *Saussurea lappa*, and *Saussurea costus*, with the currently accepted name *Dolomiaea costus*.

> **A naming caution:** 木香 in Chinese names several different aromatic roots. The classical incense and materia-medica 木香 is *Saussurea costus* (广木香 / 云木香). Other "muxiang" — including 川木香 (*Vladimiria souliei*) and 青木香 (*Aristolochia* species) — are distinct plants, and 青木香 carries a specific safety concern. A label saying only 木香 does not identify the species.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Fixative and musk-like note:** costus root's deep, musky-animalic character made it a classical fixative in incense and perfumery, and historically a lower-cost substitute for genuine musk.
- **Materia medica:** 木香 is a standard item in traditional Chinese medicine, recorded for aromatic and digestive uses. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **Trade history:** costus was a valued import from the Himalaya and Kashmir along historical trade routes.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The fixative and aromatic roles are documented. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the root's musky-fixative use, and the CITES restriction on wild trade.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Costus root is genuinely restricted: *Saussurea costus* is listed under CITES Appendix I (with the wild trade heavily restricted), reflecting over-harvesting of a slow-growing Himalayan plant. Practical points for buyers:

- The root should be clean, dense, and deeply earthy-musky when gently warmed.
- Because wild sourcing is restricted, cultivated material and substitutes appear in commerce; confirm species and legal origin when buying.
- Distinguish 木香 (costus) from 川木香 and 青木香 — different roots, and 青木香 carries its own safety concern.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is costus root?** The dried root of *Saussurea costus* (now *Dolomiaea costus*, formerly *Aucklandia lappa*), a thistle-family herb native to the Himalaya. It is a classical Chinese incense and materia-medica root valued for a deep, earthy, musky, slightly animalic aroma. It is called 木香 (mùxiāng) in Chinese, and its trade is restricted under CITES Appendix I.

**Why is costus described as a musk substitute?** Because its deep, musky-animalic character resembles musk's grounding, persistent quality, so it was historically used as a lower-cost fixative and stand-in for genuine musk in incense and perfumery. It is a botanical material, not an animal product, and its musky edge comes from the root's own aromatic chemistry.

**What does costus smell like?** Earthy and woody at the base, with a musky, faintly animalic edge that deepens and lingers. It is a grounding, persistent note — closer in spirit to spikenard or orris root than to a flower or spice — and the aroma develops with gentle heat. This persistence is what made it a traditional fixative.

**Is 木香 a single plant?** No — the name is used for several different aromatic roots. The classical incense and materia-medica 木香 is *Saussurea costus* (广木香 / 云木香). Other "muxiang" include 川木香 (*Vladimiria souliei*) and 青木香 (*Aristolochia* species), which are distinct plants — and 青木香 carries a specific safety concern. A label saying only 木香 does not identify the species.

**Is costus root endangered?** Yes, it is a conservation concern. *Saussurea costus* is a slow-growing Himalayan plant that has been over-harvested, and it is listed under CITES Appendix I, which heavily restricts international trade in wild-collected material. This drives cultivated material and substitutes into commerce, so species and legal origin should be confirmed when buying.
