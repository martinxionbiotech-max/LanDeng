> **Direct answer:** Costus (木香, mùxiāng) is the dried root of *Saussurea costus* (now often *Dolomiaea costus*, older name *Aucklandia lappa*), a thistle-family herb (Asteraceae) native to the Himalaya. It is a classical Chinese incense and materia-medica root valued for a deep, earthy, slightly musky-animalic aroma that made it a traditional fixative and, historically, a lower-cost stand-in for musk. It is a CITES-listed species.

A label saying only 木香 does not identify the species.

The name 木香 ("wood fragrance") is applied to several aromatic roots in Chinese usage, and that shared name is the material's defining risk:

- **木香 / 广木香 / 云木香** — *Saussurea costus* (syn. *Dolomiaea costus*, *Aucklandia lappa*), the classical incense and materia-medica root. This entry.
- **川木香** — *Vladimiria souliei* (also placed in *Dolomiaea*), a different Sichuan-region root.
- **青木香** — *Aristolochia* species, a distinct plant carrying a specific safety concern (aristolochic acids).

A label saying only "木香" is underspecified — the classical incense root, the Sichuan root, and the aristolochia root are different materials.

## 木香 vs 川木香 vs 青木香 (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Three different roots share the "木香" name, and one carries a real safety risk. 木香 (*Saussurea costus*) is the classical incense root; 川木香 (*Vladimiria souliei*) is a different Sichuan root; 青木香 (*Aristolochia* spp.) contains aristolochic acids, a documented toxicity concern. Species identity is not a detail here — it is the buying decision.

This is the key disambiguation, and it is more than a naming curiosity:

- **木香 (*Saussurea costus*)** — the classical incense and materia-medica root with a musky, earthy aroma. CITES Appendix I. This entry.
- **川木香 (*Vladimiria souliei*)** — a distinct Sichuan-region root sharing the 木香 name.
- **青木香 (*Aristolochia* spp.)** — a different plant whose root contains aristolochic acids, compounds associated with kidney toxicity; it is not the incense 木香 and is not interchangeable with it.

The practical consequence is that "木香" alone is not a safe or reliable identifier. A buyer — or a reader of an old formula — must determine *which* 木香 is meant, because the three are different plants and one carries a documented safety concern.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Costus root is harvested, cleaned, and dried whole, then used as cut pieces or ground to powder. The root may also be distilled for costus oil, a separate product. The root's dense, aromatic character concentrates in the dried root; no extraction step is involved in the root forms.

The part used is the dried root. Processing is straightforward:

- **Whole or cut root** — cleaned, dried root pieces, for slow aromatic release and for whole-root storage.
- **Powder** — ground root for blending into sticks and cones; particle size and moisture affect how the powder packs and burns.
- **Costus oil** — a separate distilled product with a stronger, more concentrated musky-earthy character.

For powder buyers, particle size and moisture are the practical variables, but the exact target depends on the formula and production line. The grinding mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process.

## What determines costus quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on root density and aroma: a good costus root is clean, dense, and deeply earthy-musky when gently warmed. Beyond quality, the decisive commercial question is species and legal origin — confirming it is *Saussurea costus* (not 川木香 or 青木香) and that wild material is legally sourced.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Root density and aroma** — clean, dense root with a deep, earthy-musky scent when warmed.
2. **Species identity** — *Saussurea costus* vs 川木香 (*Vladimiria souliei*) vs 青木香 (*Aristolochia* spp.); different plants, and 青木香 carries a safety concern.
3. **Legal origin** — wild *Saussurea costus* is CITES Appendix I; cultivated material and substitutes are the compliant mainstream.
4. **Cleanliness** — freedom from soil, rot, and foreign root.

Costus carries no standardized commercial grading ladder — it is a restricted root material — so quality means density, aroma, correct species, and legal origin.
