> **Direct answer:** Finger citron (佛手, fóshǒu) is the Buddha's hand, *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, a fingered variety of the citron in the rue family (Rutaceae). It is prized for its intensely fragrant, thick rind and is used as a 清供 (scholar's-table offering) fragrance fruit, scenting a room by its presence rather than being burned as incense. It is a variety of, and distinct from, the plain citron (香橼).

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* is a variety of the citron (*Citrus medica*), one of the oldest cultivated citrus, native to the foothills of the Himalaya and long grown across China. *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis* is a variety of the citron (*Citrus medica*), itself one of the oldest cultivated citrus, native to the foothills of the Himalaya and long grown across China. In the finger citron the fruit's carpels fail to fuse, so the rind develops into separate finger-like segments. Flora of China treats the fingered form within *Citrus medica* (recording *sarcodactylis*), and GBIF lists the variety name as a synonym of *Citrus medica*.

The origin point is the key to its identity: the finger citron is not a separate species but a *form* — a developmental variation of the citron in which the fruit, instead of forming a solid rind, splits into digit-like sections. That "fingers" form is both its botanical signature and the source of its name and symbolism.

## The citrus-family context (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron sits in the crowded citrus family of Chinese fragrance, where names do not map one-to-one: 佛手 (finger citron), 香橼 (citron), 佛手柑 (bergamot), and the citrus peels are all different materials with different Chinese identities. The name 佛手柑 is a notorious confusion point.

The citrus family is crowded in Chinese fragrance, and the finger citron is at the center of one of its best-known naming confusions:

- **佛手 (finger citron)** — *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, the fingered citron. This entry.
- **香橼 (citron)** — *Citrus medica*, the parent species, the plain fragrant fruit.
- **佛手柑 (bergamot)** — *Citrus bergamia*, a *different* citrus entirely, whose Chinese name 佛手柑 ("finger-citron-like") is a calque of the finger citron. See [bergamot](/ingredients/bergamot/).

The trap is 佛手柑: because 佛手 means "Buddha's hand," 佛手柑 *looks* like "finger citron," but it names the bergamot, a distinct species grown for its essential oil. The finger citron (佛手) and the bergamot (佛手柑) are not the same plant — a buyer must specify the species, not rely on the overlapping name.

## Why is finger citron used as a room fragrance?

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron scents a room by evaporation from its thick, oil-rich rind — a "fragrance of presence" that requires no burning. It is displayed as a 清供 offering, where its fragrance and its auspicious "hand of Buddha" form are equally the point.

The finger citron's role is entirely about **presence, not combustion**. Its thick, oil-rich rind slowly releases volatile fragrance, so a single fruit can scent a room for days or weeks simply by sitting on a scholar's table. This is why it is a 清供 (offering) fruit: displayed for the New Year and on scholar's tables, its value is doubled by its form — the "hand of Buddha" shape that gives it its name and its auspicious symbolism. It is the clearest example in the classical canon of a *non-combustible* fragrance material: a fruit that perfumes a space by existing in it, never by being burned.

## How is it used / processed?

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron needs no processing for its primary use — it is displayed whole, and its rind scents the room by evaporation. Where a preserved or edible form is wanted, the rind may be candied or dried, but the fragrance use is the fresh, whole fruit.

The processing is minimal, because the primary use is the whole fresh fruit:

1. **Display whole** — the fresh fruit is placed on a table or offering stand, scenting the room by evaporation.
2. **Drying (optional)** — the rind may be dried, though much of the volatile fragrance is lost.
3. **Candying (optional)** — the thick rind may be candied as a food.

There is no distillation or combustion step for the classical use; the finger citron's fragrance is released by evaporation, and its processing is essentially "display it."

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is a matter of rind condition and fragrance: a fresh, intact, thick-rinded fruit with a strong, clean citrus-floral scent. The oil-rich rind is the value, not the (nearly absent) flesh, and there is no standardized grading ladder.

The quality factors:

1. **Fragrance intensity** — the strength and cleanliness of the citrus-floral rind scent; the rind's oil is the value.
2. **Freshness** — the volatile fragrance fades as the fruit dries; a fresh fruit is markedly more fragrant.
3. **Rind condition** — an intact, thick, oil-rich rind without damage or rot.

There is no standardized grading system; fragrance, freshness, and rind condition are the practical signals.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** The finger citron is the clearest reminder that classical Chinese fragrance includes a whole non-combustion tradition — the 清供 room fragrance — that modern incense marketing largely ignores. Its value is the "fragrance of presence," and it has no burned-incense equivalent.

From a manufacturing and sourcing perspective, the finger citron illustrates a category that modern incense commerce barely serves: **the fragrance-of-presence material.** Three points follow:

- **Non-combustion is a real fragrance category.** The finger citron scents a room by evaporation, never by burning. This is a distinct logic — fragrance by presence — that the classical 清供 tradition embraced and that modern incense, with its focus on sticks, cones, and coils, largely ignores. It is a reminder that "incense" in the broad Chinese sense (香) includes materials that are smelled in place, not burned.

In our view, the professional framing for the finger citron is a **fresh, fragrant, correctly identified 清供 fruit** — a material whose value is honesty about its non-combustion role and its volatility, and whose chief risk is a name that overlaps with the bergamot.
