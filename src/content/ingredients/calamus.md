# Calamus (菖蒲 / Chang Pu)

**Direct answer:** Calamus — *chāngpú* (菖蒲) in Chinese — is the aromatic rhizome of *Acorus calamus* (sweet flag), with a warm, spicy, slightly sweet, earthy scent. It is a traditional aromatic herb, but it carries an important safety caveat: the essential oil of many calamus varieties contains **β-asarone**, which is restricted in food use in several jurisdictions.

## Aroma profile

Calamus has a warm, spicy, slightly sweet, earthy aroma with a subtle bittersweet undertone. It is a "rooty" herbal note — closer to a spice than a flower — and it is usually a supporting character in blends rather than a solo scent.

## The safety caveat (read this)

This is the one ingredient where the honest guidance goes beyond "ventilate":

- **β-asarone** — calamus essential oil can contain high levels of β-asarone, which is carcinogenic in rodent studies.
- **Food restrictions** — the US FDA banned calamus in food in 1968; the European Commission set strict limits on β-asarone in foods and beverages.
- **Variety matters** — diploid *Acorus calamus* varieties are reported to contain little or no β-asarone, while other varieties (notably tetraploid) are high in it.

The key point: this restriction is about **ingestion** (food and internal use), not about the well-understood smoke exposure that applies to *all* incense. We make **no claim** that burning calamus incense is harmless, and no medical claims of any kind.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Incense (香):** calamus is one of the classical aromatic roots of Chinese 合香, adding a spicy-herbal body.
- **Seasonal use:** 菖蒲 is traditionally hung or used around the Dragon Boat Festival (端午节), often alongside mugwort (艾草).
- **Traditional herb:** calamus has a long history in Asian and Ayurvedic traditions.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Calamus appears in traditional medical systems across Asia, but its uses are **traditional, not established clinical fact**. The one well-documented point is the β-asarone safety concern, which is a matter of toxicology, not a health benefit. We make **no medical claims**.

## Safety

Like all burning plant material, calamus smoke contains particulate matter — burn in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Beyond that, note the β-asarone ingestion concern above. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**Is calamus safe?** For **ingestion**, calamus is restricted in several jurisdictions because its oil can contain β-asarone (carcinogenic in rodent studies). For incense, the relevant caution is the same as any burning material — ventilate. We make no claim that calamus incense is safe or unsafe beyond this.

**What is the difference between calamus and sweet flag?** They are the same plant — *Acorus calamus* is commonly called "sweet flag" in English. The rhizome is the aromatic part.

## People Also Ask (Long-Tail)

**What does calamus smell like?** Warm, spicy, and slightly sweet with an earthy, rooty character — a herbal note closer to a spice than a flower.

**Is calamus the same as orris root?** No — calamus is *Acorus calamus* (Acoraceae), while orris root is the iris rhizome (*Iris* spp., Iridaceae). They are unrelated plants with different aromas.

**Why is calamus restricted in food?** Because its essential oil can contain β-asarone, which is carcinogenic in rodent studies. This is a food/ingestion restriction, distinct from the general smoke caution for incense.
