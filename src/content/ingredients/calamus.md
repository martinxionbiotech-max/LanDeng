**Definition:** Calamus — *chāngpú* (菖蒲) in Chinese — is the aromatic rhizome of *Acorus calamus* (sweet flag), with a warm, spicy, slightly sweet, earthy scent. It is a traditional aromatic herb, but it carries an important safety caveat: the essential oil of many calamus varieties contains **β-asarone**, which is restricted in food use in several jurisdictions.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Calamus has a warm, spicy, slightly sweet, earthy aroma with a subtle bittersweet undertone — a "rooty" herbal note closer to a spice than to a flower, and usually a supporting character in blends rather than a solo scent.

Calamus has a warm, spicy, slightly sweet, earthy aroma with a subtle bittersweet undertone. It is a "rooty" herbal note — closer to a spice than a flower — and it is usually a supporting character in blends rather than a solo scent. Where orris root reads sweet and powdery and angelica reads green and musky, calamus reads **dry, warm, and grounding** — a note that adds body to the lower register of a formula rather than lifting its top.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Calamus is the rhizome of *Acorus calamus*, a perennial, rhizomatous herb in the family Acoraceae (long placed in Araceae), recorded by Flora of China with ensiform leaves up to 150 cm long. It is a root material — the aromatic part is the underground rhizome, not a wood or resin.

*Acorus calamus* is a perennial, rhizomatous herb. Flora of China records its leaves as **ensiform (sword-shaped), (60–)70–100(–150) cm long** and mostly 1–1.5 cm wide. The genus *Acorus* is a small, taxonomically tangled one: it was long classified within the arum family (Araceae) but is now placed in its own family, **Acoraceae**, and the name *Acorus calamus* has accumulated many synonyms and named forms. The aromatic part is the **rhizome**, the underground stem. See [Acorus in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200027130) and [Acorus calamus on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/2873769).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 菖蒲 (chāngpú) is the standard Chinese name, and "sweet flag" is the usual English name — the same plant. The name covers a genus of closely related rhizomatous herbs, and a buyer's first job is to confirm the *Acorus* species and variety, since the safety-relevant chemistry (β-asarone) differs among them.

The name 菖蒲 is specific enough in everyday use, but the genus is more complicated than the single name suggests:

- **菖蒲 (chāngpú)** — *Acorus calamus*, sweet flag, the aromatic rhizome this page describes.
- **"Sweet flag"** — the standard English name for the same plant; there is no difference between "calamus" and "sweet flag."

Because the *Acorus* genus has many named forms and the β-asarone content varies by variety (see the safety caveat below), the practical question is less "calamus or sweet flag" than "which *Acorus* variety, and what is its β-asarone level."

## The safety caveat (read this)

This is the one ingredient where the honest guidance goes beyond "ventilate":

- **β-asarone** — calamus essential oil can contain high levels of β-asarone, which is carcinogenic in rodent studies.
- **Food restrictions** — the US FDA banned calamus in food in 1968; the European Commission set strict limits on β-asarone in foods and beverages.
- **Variety matters** — diploid *Acorus calamus* varieties are reported to contain little or no β-asarone, while other varieties (notably tetraploid) are high in it.

The key point: this restriction is about **ingestion** (food and internal use), not about the well-understood smoke exposure that applies to *all* incense. We make **no claim** that burning calamus incense is harmless, and no medical claims of any kind.

## Where does calamus come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Acorus calamus* is a widespread wetland and waterside perennial found across Asia, Europe, and North America, growing in or beside slow water. It is a common wild and cultivated herb rather than a plantation crop.

*Acorus calamus* is a semi-aquatic perennial of marshes, pond margins, and slow streams, with a broad natural and introduced range across temperate Asia, Europe, and North America. The rhizome is harvested and dried. Unlike the tropical woods and resins of the incense trade, calamus is a temperate wetland herb whose supply follows the patterns of wild collection and small-scale cultivation rather than a dedicated aromatic industry.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Calamus is one of the classical aromatic roots of Chinese 合香, where it adds a spicy-herbal body to the lower register of a blend. It is a supporting root, not a lead material — and it is also tied to seasonal use, hung alongside mugwort at the Dragon Boat Festival.

Calamus serves two distinct roles in Chinese tradition:

- **Incense (香):** calamus is one of the classical aromatic roots of Chinese 合香, adding a spicy-herbal body that complements other roots and herbs.
- **Seasonal use:** 菖蒲 is traditionally hung or used around the Dragon Boat Festival (端午节), often alongside mugwort (艾草).

That seasonal pairing with mugwort is worth knowing: the two are linked as a traditional herbal combination, not as a botanical relationship — calamus is *Acorus*, mugwort is *Artemisia*, and they meet in festival practice rather than in taxonomy. See [mugwort](/ingredients/mugwort/).

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Calamus is processed by harvesting the rhizome, cleaning it, and drying it — whole, sliced, or ground to powder. The drying method and the resulting moisture and particle size depend on the producer and the intended use, so no single specification applies across the trade.

The essential step is simple: the rhizome is dug, cleaned, and **dried** to a stable, storable form. From there it branches by end use — whole or sliced rhizome for traditional use, or ground powder for blending into incense. For powder buyers, the same practical variables as any root powder apply: **particle size and moisture** affect how the material packs, burns, and releases aroma, and the right target **depends on the formula** — the binder, the other ingredients, and the production line (see Our Industry View below). The drying and grinding mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process.

## What determines calamus quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on the variety (which determines the β-asarone-relevant chemistry), the cleanliness and dryness of the rhizome, and the strength of the characteristic warm, spicy aroma. There is no standardized numerical grading system; the buyer evaluates the physical condition of the lot.

The practical quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Variety** — which *Acorus* form, since β-asarone content differs among them (a food-use concern; see the safety caveat).
2. **Cleanliness** — freedom from soil, foreign roots, and mold.
3. **Dryness and storage** — a properly dried rhizome keeps its warm, spicy aroma rather than turning musty.
4. **Aroma** — a clean, warm, spicy-earthy scent, not stale or faded.
5. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

## Calamus vs related aromatics

> **Direct answer:** Calamus is often confused with orris root because both are aromatic rhizomes — but they are unrelated plants with different aromas. Calamus (*Acorus calamus*, Acoraceae) is spicy and earthy; orris root (*Iris*, Iridaceae) is sweet, powdery, and violet-like.

> **Direct answer:** Calamus is not a heavily faked aromatic in the way agarwood is; its risk is twofold — confusion with other aromatic rhizomes (such as orris root), and the variety-dependent β-asarone chemistry that matters for any food-adjacent use. The botanical name and variety on the label resolve both.

Calamus is not an expensive, heavily faked material. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Calamus reaches the market mainly as a traditional herb and a fragrance raw material, with a small role in herbal-incense blends. Because of the β-asarone restriction, its food and internal use is limited in several jurisdictions, which shapes where it can legally be sold.

Calamus' commercial life is split by the safety caveat. As an incense and fragrance material, the rhizome and its essential oil add a spicy-herbal body; as a food or internal ingredient, it is restricted in the US (FDA 1968 ban) and limited by European Commission β-asarone limits. For a sourcing buyer, this split matters because **a calamus product's allowable end use depends on its β-asarone content and the target market's regulation** — a vendor should be able to state the variety and its intended use rather than offering an unqualified "calamus."

## Safety

Like all burning plant material, calamus smoke contains particulate matter — burn in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Beyond that, note the β-asarone ingestion concern above. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, calamus is a supporting root, not a base. Its value is a warm, spicy-earthy body in the lower register — but its β-asarone chemistry makes the variety and the end use a compliance question first and a fragrance question second.

From a manufacturing perspective, calamus' defining traits are **body** and **caution**. It supplies a warm, spicy-earthy grounding that lighter herbs and florals cannot, but its β-asarone chemistry means it is the one incense root where the *variety* is a compliance variable, not merely a grade label.

Two points follow:

- **It is a supporting root, not a base.** Calamus adds body to the lower register but will not carry a formula the way sandalwood or agarwood can. Its role is to deepen and warm a blend; a buyer who expects it to anchor a fragrance will be disappointed, while one who doses it as a spice-root accent gets real value.
- **Variety is a compliance question.** Because β-asarone content varies by ploidy, a manufacturer's first question about a calamus lot is *which variety and what chemistry*, then the fragrance questions of particle size and moisture — which themselves **depend on formulation**, so a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **correctly identified, documented-variety calamus rhizome** used as a measured spicy-herbal body note — a material whose real cost is not the price per kilo but the diligence required to use it safely and within regulation.
