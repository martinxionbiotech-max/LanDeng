**Technical answer:** Spikenard — *gānsōng* (甘松) in Chinese — is the dried, fragrant root of *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a small perennial herb of the Himalayas and the mountains of western China. In Chinese incense it is a classical *huxiang* (合香) blending material, valued for a deep, earthy-sweet, slightly musky root note rather than for burning alone. It shares the same aromatic family as the "spikenard" (nard) of the ancient Mediterranean and Indian perfume traditions.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Spikenard root is earthy and woody at its base, with a sweetness that is more "rooty" than floral and a musky, faintly animalic edge. It is a supporting note that deepens and grounds a blend, and its aroma develops with gentle heat.

Spikenard root is earthy and woody at its base, with a sweetness that is more "rooty" than floral and a musky, faintly animalic edge. It is a *supporting* note: it deepens and grounds a blend rather than leading with a bright top note. The aroma develops with gentle heat, which is why it appears repeatedly in classical *huxiang* formulas alongside woods and resins.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** 甘松 is *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a small perennial herb of the valerian family (traditionally Valerianaceae, now placed in Caprifoliaceae). Flora of China records flowering stems 5–50 cm tall, with purple-red, pink, or magenta flowers, and the fragrant root is the part used.

*Nardostachys jatamansi* is a small herb native to the high Himalaya. Flora of China records its flowering stems at **5–50 cm tall**, with basal rosette leaves and **purple-red, pink, or magenta** flowers — and, crucially for incense, a fragrant rootstock, which is the material of value. The plant was traditionally placed in the valerian family (Valerianaceae) and is now treated under Caprifoliaceae in modern systems; both placements describe the same plant. See [Nardostachys jatamansi in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200022534).

The part used is the root and rhizome, which is harvested, cleaned, and dried.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 甘松 (gānsōng, "sweet *song*") records a taste-based naming convention — 甘 means "sweet" — rather than a description of its scent. It also travels as 甘松香, and shares the English "spikenard" with the nard of Western perfume traditions.

The name 甘松 ("sweet *song*") refers to its sweet taste (甘) — an older, taste-based naming convention rather than a description of its scent. The material also appears as **甘松香** (gānsōngxiāng). The English name "spikenard" links it to the *nard* of the ancient Mediterranean and Indian traditions — the same *Nardostachys* aromatic family under a different regional name.

## Where does spikenard come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Nardostachys jatamansi* is a high-Himalayan herb of Nepal, Bhutan, northern India, Tibet, and western Sichuan. Its high-mountain, wild-harvest habitat is the root of its scarcity and CITES monitoring.

*Nardostachys jatamansi* is a small herb native to the **high Himalaya** — Nepal, Bhutan, northern India, and into Tibet and western Sichuan. This high-mountain, wild-harvest habitat is central to its sourcing story: because the plant grows in sensitive alpine habitats and is harvested from the wild, genuine material has become scarce and its trade is monitored (see Sustainability below).

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Spikenard (甘松) is used in incense as a grounding, earthy body note — it deepens a blend from below, alongside woods and resins, rather than leading with a bright top note. This is why it is a classical *huxiang* (合香) blending material rather than a solo burn.

Spikenard's role follows directly from its aroma: an earthy, sweet, musky body note that **grounds** a formula. It works in combination, not alone — in classical *huxiang* (合香) it is paired with woods (sandalwood, agarwood), resins, and other roots, where it contributes a depth and warmth that the other ingredients do not supply on their own. This is why it is blended rather than burned solo for fragrance.

## What determines spikenard quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on the root's aromatic intensity and soundness — a clean, dry root with a noticeable earthy-sweet aroma when gently warmed beats a stale or woody one. Because wild sourcing is restricted, species and origin also matter.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Aromatic intensity** — the root should give a noticeable earthy-sweet, musky aroma when gently warmed; this is the decisive variable.
2. **Soundness and cleanliness** — freedom from rot, mold, and foreign matter.
3. **Species and origin** — because genuine wild *Nardostachys* is scarce and substitutes appear in commerce, confirming the species matters.
4. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

There is **no standardized grading system**; the buyer evaluates the physical condition, aroma, and provenance of the lot.

## Common forms

## In Chinese tradition

- **合香 (blended incense):** 甘松 is a classical supporting ingredient in blended incense formulas, contributing an earthy, grounding body. The Song-dynasty manual *Xiangpu* (香譜) records 甘松香 among the standard incense materials.
- **Scenting clothes (裛衣):** classical texts describe spikenard as usable to scent garments and for fragrant bathing.
- **Materia medica:** 甘松 appears in traditional Chinese medicine, where the *Bencao Shiyi* (本草拾遗) records it for various uses and notes "bathing makes the body fragrant" (浴人身令香). This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The classical references describe spikenard's aromatic and cultural roles — blending, scenting, bathing — and a traditional medicinal reputation. We treat the medicinal descriptions as **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity and the plant's long use as a fragrant root in Chinese, Indian, and Himalayan incense traditions.

## Spikenard vs related roots

> **Direct answer:** Spikenard is an earthy, musky, sweet root note, distinct from sandalwood (a creamy, warm wood) and vetiver (a dry, smoky root). All three ground a blend, but spikenard's musky sweetness is its own register.

| Material | Character |
|---|---|
| Spikenard (甘松) | Earthy, woody, sweet, musky — a grounding root note |
| Sandalwood | Creamy, sweet, soft — a warm, prized wood base |
| Vetiver | Dry, earthy, smoky — a rooty base with a cleaner edge |

Spikenard sits between the two in register: sweeter and more musky than vetiver, but a root rather than a wood like sandalwood. See [sandalwood](/ingredients/sandalwood/), [vetiver](/ingredients/vetiver/), and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The main risks are substitution and species confusion, driven by scarcity: because wild *Nardostachys* is restricted and CITES-monitored, cultivated material and substitutes — including the sedge *Cyperus* (香附子), a traditional *huxiang* root — appear under the "spikenard" name. The label should specify species and form.

Spikenard's scarcity creates its authenticity problem. Two risks recur:

- **Species and form confusion** — "spikenard" in Western aromatherapy is usually a steam-distilled essential oil, while Chinese 甘松 is the dried root; a label saying only "spikenard" specifies neither species nor form.
- **Substitution** — because wild sourcing is restricted, cultivated material and substitutes (including the unrelated sedge *Cyperus*, 香附子, a traditional *huxiang* root sometimes used in spikenard's place) appear in commerce.

Wild *Nardostachys* is harvested from sensitive high-Himalayan habitats, and the trade is monitored under **CITES Appendix II**. **Why is spikenard so associated with blended incense?** Because its aroma is a grounding, earthy body note rather than a self-sufficient fragrance, spikenard works best in combination.
