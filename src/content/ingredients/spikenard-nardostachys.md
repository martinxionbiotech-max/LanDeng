**Technical answer:** Spikenard — *gānsōng* (甘松) in Chinese — is the dried, fragrant root of *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a small perennial herb of the Himalayas and the mountains of western China. In Chinese incense it is a classical *huxiang* (合香) blending material, valued for a deep, earthy-sweet, slightly musky root note rather than for burning alone. It shares the same aromatic family as the "spikenard" (nard) of the ancient Mediterranean and Indian perfume traditions.

## Aroma profile

Spikenard root is earthy and woody at its base, with a sweetness that is more "rooty" than floral and a musky, faintly animalic edge. It is a *supporting* note: it deepens and grounds a blend rather than leading with a bright top note. The aroma develops with gentle heat, which is why it appears repeatedly in classical *huxiang* formulas alongside woods and resins.

## Botanical source and origin

*Nardostachys jatamansi* is a small herb native to the high Himalaya — Nepal, Bhutan, northern India, and into Tibet and western Sichuan. The part used is the root and rhizome, which is harvested, cleaned, and dried. The name 甘松 ("sweet *song*") refers to its sweet taste (甘) — an older, taste-based naming convention rather than a description of its scent.

> **A naming caution:** Chinese 甘松 is not the same material as "spikenard" sold as an essential oil in Western aromatherapy under the same English name, though both trace to *Nardostachys*. Labels that say only "spikenard" may come from different species or grades. See the note below.

## In Chinese tradition

- **合香 (blended incense):** 甘松 is a classical supporting ingredient in blended incense formulas, contributing an earthy, grounding body. The Song-dynasty manual *Xiangpu* (香譜) records 甘松香 among the standard incense materials.
- **Scenting clothes (裛衣):** classical texts describe spikenard as usable to scent garments and for fragrant bathing.
- **Materia medica:** 甘松 appears in traditional Chinese medicine, where the *Bencao Shiyi* (本草拾遗) records it for various uses and notes "bathing makes the body fragrant" (浴人身令香). This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The classical references describe spikenard's aromatic and cultural roles — blending, scenting, bathing — and a traditional medicinal reputation. We treat the medicinal descriptions as **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity and the plant's long use as a fragrant root in Chinese, Indian, and Himalayan incense traditions.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Genuine spikenard root is increasingly scarce because wild *Nardostachys* is harvested from sensitive Himalayan habitats and has been listed under CITES Appendix II (the trade is monitored). As a result, "spikenard" on the market can vary in species and grade. Practical notes for buyers:

- The material should be a clean, dried root with a noticeable earthy-sweet aroma when gently warmed.
- Because wild sourcing is restricted, cultivated material and substitutes (including the unrelated sedge *Cyperus*, a traditional *huxiang* root sometimes used in spikenard's place) appear in commerce.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What does spikenard smell like?** Spikenard root is earthy and woody with a sweet, slightly musky, faintly animalic undertone. It is a supporting, grounding note rather than a bright or floral one — in Chinese *huxiang* (blended incense) it deepens a formula from below, alongside woods and resins. The aroma is "rooty" and develops with gentle heat, which is why it is blended rather than burned alone for fragrance.

**Is Chinese 甘松 the same as Western spikenard essential oil?** They trace to the same plant family — *Nardostachys* — but the materials are not automatically identical. Chinese 甘松 is the dried root used in incense and materia medica, while "spikenard" in Western aromatherapy is usually a steam-distilled essential oil of the same genus. A label that says only "spikenard" does not specify species or form, so buyers should check what they are getting.

**What part of the spikenard plant is used?** The dried root and rhizome of *Nardostachys jatamansi* are the parts used. The plant is a small Himalayan herb; its root is harvested, cleaned, and dried, then used whole, sliced, or powdered in incense and traditional practice. The name 甘松 refers to the root's sweet taste rather than its scent — an older, taste-based naming convention.

**Why is spikenard so associated with blended incense?** Because its aroma is a grounding, earthy body note rather than a self-sufficient fragrance, spikenard works best in combination. The Song-dynasty manual *Xiangpu* (香譜) lists 甘松香 among the standard incense materials, and classical *huxiang* formulas pair it with woods (sandalwood, agarwood), resins, and other roots. It contributes depth and warmth that other ingredients do not supply on their own.

**Is spikenard rare?** Genuine wild spikenard has become scarce because *Nardostachys jatamansi* is harvested from sensitive high-Himalayan habitats and its trade is monitored under CITES Appendix II. This has led to cultivated material and substitutes appearing in commerce — including the traditional *huxiang* root *Cyperus* (香附子), sometimes used in spikenard's place. Rarity and sourcing pressure are worth checking when buying.
