> **Direct answer:** Chinese quince (木瓜, mùguā) is *Pseudocydonia sinensis* (long placed as *Chaenomeles sinensis*), a tree of the rose family (Rosaceae) native to China. The Chinese quince has a bright, fruity scent with a sweet, floral, faintly tart character — the classic "quince" perfume, released from the hard, yellow fruit. Like its relatives the citron and finger citron, it is not eaten fresh (the flesh is too hard and sour) but is prized for its fragrance, set on a table or in a room where its scent is experienced by proximity and evaporation rather than by burning.

The register is worth pinning within the fragrant-fruit group: the quince's perfume is fruitier and more floral than the citron's reserved, almost clean-lemony scent, and it reads as a *sweet-fruity room note* — closer to a ripening fruit bowl than to the sharp citrus of the citron. This is the classic "quince" character, and it is why the quince sits so naturally among the 清供 fruits.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Pseudocydonia sinensis* (long known as *Chaenomeles sinensis*) is a small tree of the rose family (Rosaceae), native to China and long cultivated for its large, hard, fragrant fruit. The fruit is too hard and sour to eat raw but is valued for its strong, sweet perfume. Flora of China records the species as a shrub or small tree to 5–10 m.

*Pseudocydonia sinensis* (long known as *Chaenomeles sinensis*) is a small tree of the rose family (Rosaceae), native to China and long cultivated for its large, hard, fragrant fruit. The fruit is too hard and sour to eat raw but is valued for its strong, sweet perfume and, cooked or preserved, for its flavor. Flora of China records the species — a shrub or small tree to 5–10 m, with purplish-red branchlets and solitary flowers — under *Chaenomeles sinensis*, with *Pseudocydonia* and *Cydonia* as the older names. The rose-family placement matters: the quince is a relative of the apple and pear, not a citrus, which is why its perfume is fruity-floral rather than citrus-sharp.

## Why is it used — 清供 and room fragrance?

> **Direct answer:** The Chinese quince is used as a 清供 (scholar's-table offering) and a room-fragrance fruit, displayed for its strong, sweet perfume rather than eaten or burned. Its fragrance is one of presence and evaporation, and its materia-medica use is traditional, not clinical.

The quince's role is the 清供 role: it is displayed on the scholar's table and in the home for its strong, sweet perfume and its golden, auspicious form, experienced by *proximity and evaporation* rather than by burning or eating. This places it squarely in the "fragrance of presence" tradition of the citron, finger citron, and kumquat — a fruit that scents a room by simply being there. Its materia-medica use is a separate, traditional layer: 木瓜 is recorded in the 本草, and its medicinal reputation is **traditional**, not established clinical fact.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** The Chinese quince needs no processing for its fragrance — the hard, fragrant fruit scents a room by evaporation from its skin. It is not eaten raw (the flesh is too hard and sour); for culinary use it is cooked or preserved, which softens the flesh and develops the flavor.

The quince needs no processing for its fragrance role: the hard, yellow fruit scents a room passively by evaporation, and it is displayed as-is. It is not eaten raw — the flesh is too hard and sour — so for culinary use it is cooked, candied, or preserved, which softens the flesh and develops the sweet flavor. This is the same pattern as the citron and finger citron: the fragrance is the raw fruit's gift, and the culinary form is a cooked transformation, not the material's primary identity.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quince quality turns on the fragrance and soundness of the fruit. A good quince is large, firm, golden, and intensely sweet-fruity-floral on the skin; a withered or bruised fruit has lost both its perfume and its display value. The fragrance, not the flesh, is the point.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **fragrance** (an intense, sweet, fruity-floral perfume from the skin), **soundness** (a firm, unblemished, golden fruit), and **size/form** (the large, melon-like, auspicious form that suits the 清供 display). Because the fruit is prized for fragrance and display rather than eating, its perfume and its appearance are the whole quality story — a bruised or withered quince has lost its value even if it is still technically sound.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Quince is a "name-drift false-friend" case — the classical 木瓜 is the hard, fragrant Chinese quince, but the everyday modern word has drifted to mean the papaya. This drift, plus the quince's place in the "fragrance of presence" 清供 group, defines it as a classical room-fragrance fruit that must be actively disambiguated from its modern namesake.

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, the quince is most interesting for the drift its own name has undergone. Three points follow:

- **The name drifted, and the drift is the trap.** 木瓜 ("tree melon") classically names the Chinese quince, but modern everyday Chinese uses it for the papaya. A buyer or reader who hears 木瓜 and assumes the tropical fruit has already made the error the name invites. The classical 清供-and-本草 material is the quince, and the qualifier 番 ("foreign") on 番木瓜 records the papaya's later, imported status — the same 番 foreign-origin marker the [fenugreek](/ingredients/fenugreek/) entry documents.
- **"Fragrance of presence" is the quince's mode, and it is a genuine category.** The quince, citron, finger citron, and kumquat form a cluster of fruits experienced by proximity and evaporation — displayed and smelled, not burned or eaten raw. This is a real fragrance model, distinct from the burned 香材 and from extracted oils, and it is the model the whole 清供 tradition runs on.
- **The rose-family placement is a sensory fact, not trivia.** The quince is a rose-family relative of apple and pear, not a citrus — which is why its perfume is fruity-floral rather than citrus-sharp. This placement explains the sensory difference from the citron group and is a useful check: a "木瓜" that smells sharply of lemon is probably the wrong plant.

In our view, the Chinese quince is best understood as a **rose-family 清供 fruit whose classical name has drifted onto the papaya** — a hard, fragrant room-fragrance fruit of the "presence" tradition, distinct from both the tropical papaya and the citrus 清供 fruits it is displayed beside.
