> **Direct answer:** Sophora flower (槐花, huáihuā) is the blossom of the pagoda tree, *Styphnolobium japonicum* (formerly *Sophora japonica*), a large legume tree (Fabaceae) cultivated throughout China. Its flowers carry a light, sweet, honey-like floral scent and are used in traditional materia medica and, to a lesser extent, as a fragrant flower. The *Bencao Gangmu* records the tree under 槐, folding in 槐花 (the flower).

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Styphnolobium japonicum* (the pagoda tree, long recorded as *Sophora japonica*) is a large legume tree (Fabaceae). Flora of China describes a tree to 25 m tall, with 15–25 cm leaves bearing 9–15 leaflets, terminal panicles to 30 cm of white or creamy-yellow (rarely purple-red) flowers in July–August, and characteristic moniliform (bead-like, constricted) legumes. Flora of China records it as cultivated throughout China, with a native range given as Japan and Korea.

*Styphnolobium japonicum*, the pagoda tree, is a large legume tree (Fabaceae). Flora of China describes it precisely: **trees to 25 m** with gray-brown, longitudinally striate bark; leaves 15–25 cm with **9–15 leaflets**, ovate-lanceolate to ovate-oblong, 2.5–6 × 1.5–3 cm; terminal panicles to **30 cm**; corolla **white or creamy yellow** (rarely purple-red); legumes green, **moniliform** (constricted between the seeds), 2.5–5 × ca. 1 cm, indehiscent; seeds 1–6; flowering **July–August**, fruiting August–October. Flora of China records the species as **cultivated throughout China, with the native range given as Japan and Korea**, and widely cultivated elsewhere — a useful nuance, since the tree's deep Chinese cultural associations (below) have often led to it being described as native Chinese. See [Styphnolobium japonicum in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200012322) and [Styphnolobium japonicum on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/2963569).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 槐 (huái) names the tree, and 槐花 (huáihuā) is the flower — one part of a tree whose parts are recorded separately. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 木之二, records the tree under 槐, folding in the flower (槐花) and the fruit (槐实 / 槐角) among its parts, so the name needs a part qualifier.

## The officialdom symbolism (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The pagoda tree carried a specific classical association with officialdom: the *Bencao Gangmu* records "面三槐，三公位焉" — the three pagoda trees marked the seats of the three highest ministers. This is a cultural-symbolic meaning, not a fragrance claim, and it explains the tree's prominence in Chinese culture despite its modest scent.

The pagoda tree's cultural weight comes from officialdom, not fragrance. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 木之二, records the tree under 槐 and its association with high office:

> "面三槐，三公位焉" — facing the three pagoda trees, the three highest ministers (三公) took their seats.

The three 槐 trees marked the seats of the three highest ministers of state, so the tree became a symbol of official standing and courtly rank. This is a cultural-symbolic meaning — a matter of status and setting, not of scent — and it explains why the tree is so prominent in the classical record while its flower's fragrance remains modest.

## What determines sophora flower quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness and gentleness of drying — a clean, well-dried flower that retains its light, honey-like sweetness. Because the scent is modest to begin with, a faded or musty batch has little aromatic value, and the flower should be judged as a gentle floral, not as a strong 香材.

The quality factors, in rough order:

4. **Part clarity** — the flower (槐花), not the fruit (槐实).

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for 槐花; aroma and condition are the practical signals, with the caveat that the scent is gentle by design.
