It is a modern fragrance material, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient — and its Chinese name needs a caution, set out below.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Because the sweet violet's scent is fleeting, the flower is rarely extracted at scale. The violet note is produced instead from orris root (a cured, aged rhizome) or from synthetic ionones — so the "processing" of the violet note is really the processing of its substitutes.

The sweet violet is not a material with a substantial extraction industry: its scent is too fleeting to extract efficiently, so there is little commercial "violet absolute" of consequence. The violet *note* is instead produced by two routes: **orris root**, which is harvested, cured, and aged for years to develop its powdery-sweet scent, and **synthetic ionones**, which reproduce the violet smell directly. The processing story of the violet note is therefore the processing story of its substitutes — a curing-and-aging route (orris) and a synthetic route (ionones).

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, violet is best read as the material where the *name* and the *note* have both detached from the *flower*. Three points follow:

- **The name points in two directions at once.** 紫罗兰 most often names the garden stock (*Matthiola incana*, Brassicaceae) in everyday Chinese, while the perfume violet is *Viola odorata* (Violaceae) — and neither is an orchid despite the 兰.
