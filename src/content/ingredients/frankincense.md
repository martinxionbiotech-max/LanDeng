**Quick answer:** Frankincense — *rǔxiāng* (乳香, "milk fragrance") in Chinese — is the aromatic gum-resin of *Boswellia* trees (family Burseraceae). It is one of the world's oldest incense resins, used for religious ritual, fumigation, perfume, and traditional medicine.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense has a fresh, resinous, slightly citrus (lemony) aroma over a warm, creamy, earthy base. When gently heated it releases a clean, bright scent, distinct from the deep sweetness of woods like agarwood or sandalwood.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense is a gum-resin, not a wood or flower. It is collected from *Boswellia* trees in the family Burseraceae — chiefly *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, and *B. serrata* — which exude a milky resin when their bark is scored.

*Boswellia* is a genus of small trees and shrubs in the family Burseraceae, native to arid regions of northeastern Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and India. The aromatic product is the **oleo-gum-resin** the tree exudes from wounds in its bark — a resin, not the wood of the tree. The three names that dominate commerce are *Boswellia sacra* (Arabia), *B. carterii* (the Horn of Africa, especially Somalia), and *B. serrata* (India). See [Boswellia sacra on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/8330171).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance," describing the milky resin that hardens into tears. It is classically paired with 没药 (myrrh) in Chinese materia medica — a pairing that also runs through Western religious and perfumery tradition.

The Chinese name 乳香 (rǔxiāng) means "milk fragrance" — a direct description of the milky resin that hardens into the aromatic tears. In Chinese materia medica, 乳香 is classically paired with **没药 (mòyào, myrrh)**, and the two are habitually named together. The pairing is not a Chinese invention alone: frankincense and myrrh travel together through Western religious and perfumery tradition as well, because both are gum-resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae) and complement each other's aroma. ## Where does frankincense come from?

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense comes from arid regions around the Red Sea and Arabian Peninsula — chiefly Oman and Yemen (*B. sacra*), Somalia and the Horn of Africa (*B. carterii*), and India (*B. serrata*) — where the resin is tapped by hand from scored trees.

The traditional and still-current producing belt runs from Oman and Yemen through Somalia, Ethiopia, and the Horn of Africa, to western India. **Omani and Yemeni material** (*B. sacra*) has long been regarded as the reference grade; **Somali material** (*B. carterii*) dominates commercial volume and reads clean and citrus; **Indian material** (*B. serrata*) is earthier and more balsamic. The resin has been traded across this region for thousands of years, which is how it reached China as an import.

## Why is frankincense used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense is used in incense for its clean, bright, resinous aroma and its ritual history. It is typically heated rather than burned directly, so the resin melts and releases scent without the charred note of an open flame.

Frankincense's role follows from its physical form: as a resin it **melts and volatilizes under gentle heat**, so the classical method is to warm tears on a charcoal disc or electric heater rather than burn them in a flame. Its fresh, citrus-bright scent makes it a "top" note that lifts heavier woods and resins, and its long ritual use — in temple, church, and fumigation — gives it a cultural weight that matches its aroma. See the [frankincense incense guide](/blog/frankincense-incense/).

## In Chinese tradition

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense has been burned in religious and ceremonial contexts, used as a classic heated resin in incense and fumigation, and recorded in Chinese materia medica — classically paired with myrrh and described for "activating blood" and relieving pain in traditional language.

- **Ritual and religious use:** frankincense has been burned in religious and ceremonial contexts for centuries, in China and across the ancient world.
- **Incense and fumigation:** it is a classic resin incense ingredient, often heated rather than burned directly.
- **Traditional materia medica:** in traditional Chinese medicine, 乳香 is classically paired with myrrh (没药) and described for "activating blood" (活血) and relieving pain.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense resin contains boswellic acids, the subject of modern laboratory research on inflammation. Medicinal claims — treating arthritis, asthma, cancer, or other conditions — are traditional use or preliminary research, not established clinical fact. Burning it is an aromatic/ritual practice, not a medical treatment.

## Harvest & processing

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense is harvested by scoring the bark of *Boswellia* trees; the milky resin hardens into droplets ("tears") over days to weeks. The tears are collected by hand and graded by size, color, and clarity, then burned whole, ground, or distilled for oil.

Frankincense is harvested by scoring the bark of *Boswellia* trees; the tree exudes a milky resin that hardens into droplets ("tears") over days to weeks. The tears are collected by hand and graded by size, color, and clarity — larger, paler, cleaner tears are generally considered higher grade, while dust and mixed fragments are lower grade. The resin is burned whole, ground, or steam-distilled for essential oil.

## Species & origins

> **Direct answer:** Not all frankincense is the same — different *Boswellia* species and regions produce different aroma profiles. *B. sacra* (Oman/Yemen) is classic, *B. carterii* (Somalia) is clean and citrus, and *B. serrata* (India) is earthier and more balsamic.

Not all frankincense is the same — different *Boswellia* species and regions produce different aroma profiles:

- *B. sacra* (Oman / Yemen) — the classic, often considered the reference grade.
- *B. carterii* (Somalia) — common in commerce, clean and citrus.
- *B. serrata* (India) — earthier and more balsamic.

The species and origin affect the aroma, so a label that names them is more informative than one that just says "frankincense."

## What determines frankincense quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is graded by tear size, color, and clarity — larger, paler, cleaner tears rank higher — plus species and origin, which set the aroma profile. Dust and mixed fragments are lower grade.

The quality factors:

1. **Tear size** — larger, whole tears are preferred over dust and fragments.
2. **Color and clarity** — pale, clean, translucent tears generally rank higher than dark or cloudy material.
3. **Species and origin** — *B. sacra* is the reference; *B. carterii* and *B. serrata* have their own profiles.
4. **Aroma freshness** — a clean, bright, citrus-resinous scent with no stale or smoky off-notes.

These are trade distinctions, not a single standardized grading system.

## Common forms

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense is sold as whole tears, powder, and essential oil. Tears suit gentle heating; powder suits blending into sticks and pastes; oil is a distilled extract. They are the same resin in different physical forms.

| Form | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Tears (whole resin) | Warmed on charcoal or an electric heater |
| Powder | Blended into incense pastes and sticks |
| Essential oil | Perfumery and scented products |

Tears are preferred for gentle heating because they melt and release aroma slowly, and whole pieces are easier to grade by size and color. Powder suits blending, where a uniform, fast-burning form is needed. See [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Frankincense vs myrrh

> **Direct answer:** Both are resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae), but from different genera. Frankincense (*Boswellia*) is fresh, citrus, and creamy; myrrh (*Commiphora*) is bitter, balsamic, and earthy. The two are classically paired.

## Sustainability & sourcing

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense supply depends on wild and semi-wild *Boswellia* trees in arid regions, and over-tapping is a documented concern in parts of the range. Sourcing should confirm species, origin, and ideally the harvest practice.

Frankincense is mostly tapped from wild or semi-managed trees rather than plantations, so long-term supply depends on the health of those stands. Over-tapping and the conversion of dryland forest have been reported as pressures in parts of the range. For a buyer, the practical questions are the same as for other resins: **which species, which origin, and how is it harvested** — with documentation, where available. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Frankincense goes into incense (loose resin and sticks), perfumery (frankincense oil), and personal care. The resin, the powder, and the oil serve different markets.

- **Incense** — loose tears for heating, and resin powder blended into sticks and cones.
- **Perfumery** — frankincense essential oil as a fresh, resinous note and fixative.
- **Personal care and cosmetics** — the oil in soaps, creams, and aromatherapy products.

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** Ask for species and origin, look for whole, pale, clean tears over dust, and confirm the form — tears, powder, or oil — since each behaves differently in a blend.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

- **Species and origin** — *B. sacra*, *B. carterii*, or *B. serrata*; a real seller names them.
- **Tear quality** — whole, pale, clean tears over dust and mixed fragments.
- **Form** — tears for heating, powder for blending, oil for fragrance; they are not interchangeable.
- **Aroma** — clean, bright, citrus-resinous, with no stale or smoky off-notes.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Do not consume resin based on traditional-medicine claims without professional guidance. See our [safety guide](/safety/).

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, the useful variable is not "frankincense" as a single material but species, grade, and physical form. A steady supply of one named species in a consistent grind or tear grade matters more than a generic label.

From a manufacturing perspective, "frankincense" is a family of materials, not one. What determines whether a formula is repeatable is **species, grade, and form consistency** — the same *Boswellia* species, the same tear grade, and the same particle size in powder, batch after batch.

Two points follow:

- **Species changes the aroma, not just the name.** *B. carterii* and *B. serrata* will read differently in the same blend, so a formula built on one will not smell the same if the source switches without notice.
- **Powder behavior depends on the formula.** Resin powder is sticky and heat-sensitive compared with wood powder; its grind, moisture, and how it is combined with a binder all affect burn, and the right settings depend on the specific stick. There is no universal figure — the buyer should sample against their own production.

In our view, the commercial value is a **documented, consistent species-and-grade supply**, not a generic "frankincense" label.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between frankincense and myrrh?** Both are resins from the same plant family, Burseraceae, but from different genera. Frankincense comes from *Boswellia* trees and has a fresh, citrusy, creamy aroma; myrrh comes from *Commiphora* trees and is bitter, balsamic, and earthy. Frankincense reads bright and clean, while myrrh is darker and heavier. The two are classically paired in Chinese tradition, where 乳香 (frankincense) and 没药 (myrrh) are used together, and in Western religious and perfumery tradition, where the pairing is equally old.

**Is frankincense a wood?** No — frankincense is a gum-resin, not wood. It is collected from the bark of *Boswellia* trees, which are scored so the tree exudes a milky resin that hardens into droplets called "tears." These tears are harvested by hand and graded by size, color, and clarity. The aromatic material is the hardened resin, not the wood of the tree itself. Frankincense is therefore classed as a resin incense ingredient, distinct from aromatic woods like sandalwood or agarwood.

**What does frankincense smell like?** Frankincense has a fresh, resinous, slightly citrus (lemony) aroma over a warm, creamy, earthy base. When gently heated, it releases a clean, bright scent that is distinct from the deep sweetness of woods like agarwood or sandalwood. The exact character varies by species and origin — *B. sacra* from Oman and Yemen is classic, *B. carterii* from Somalia is clean and citrusy, and *B. serrata* from India is earthier and more balsamic. Overall it reads bright and clean rather than dark and heavy.

**How do you burn frankincense resin?** Frankincense is traditionally burned on a lit charcoal disc. Place the disc on a heatproof surface in sand or ash, light it until it glows, and add small tears or granules of resin, which will melt and release their aroma. A gentler method is an electric incense heater, which warms the resin slowly without charcoal smoke and gives a cleaner scent. Small tears work best because they heat evenly; large lumps may only partially melt. Avoid inhaling the smoke directly and keep the area ventilated.

**Is frankincense the same as oud or agarwood?** No. Frankincense is a gum-resin from *Boswellia* trees (family Burseraceae), while oud, also called agarwood, is resin-impregnated *Aquilaria* wood. They are different materials from different plants with different aromas: frankincense is fresh, citrusy, and resinous, while agarwood is deep, sweet, and woody. The confusion sometimes arises because both are premium aromatic materials, but they are not interchangeable and come from entirely different botanical sources.

**What is the difference between frankincense tears and powder?** Tears are the whole dried resin droplets, while powder is the same resin ground fine. They suit different uses. Tears are preferred for gentle heating because they melt and release their aroma slowly, and whole pieces are easier to grade by size and color. Powder is used for blending into incense pastes and sticks, where a uniform, fast-burning form is needed. Both are the same material — *Boswellia* resin — simply in different physical forms, and neither is inherently higher quality.

**What are boswellic acids?** Boswellic acids are compounds found in frankincense resin, the most studied being AKBA (acetyl-11-keto-β-boswellic acid). They are the subject of laboratory and experimental research on inflammation. This is preliminary research interest, not established clinical proof, and it does not make burning frankincense a medical treatment. We mention them only to distinguish what is studied from what is proven.
