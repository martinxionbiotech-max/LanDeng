> **Direct answer:** Wintersweet (蜡梅, làmèi) is the flower of *Chimonanthus praecox*, a winter-flowering shrub of the wintersweet family (Calycanthaceae) native to central China. It is a sweet, honeyed, waxy floral that blooms in deep winter, prized in Chinese gardens and used as a winter floral note in incense. Despite its name, it is not a plum — it belongs to a different family from the true plum blossom (梅花, *Prunus mume*).

## Aroma profile

Wintersweet is sweet and honeyed, with a waxy, almost creamy floral character that carries its scent unusually well in cold air — a trait that makes its deep-winter bloom memorable. It is warmer and rounder than the delicate, almondy plum blossom, closer to a honeyed floral than a green one. In a blend it reads as a *soft winter floral*, adding a sweet, rounded bloom note.

## Botanical source and origin

*Chimonanthus praecox* is a deciduous shrub of the wintersweet family (Calycanthaceae), native to central China, where it has been cultivated as a winter-blooming ornamental for centuries. The family Calycanthaceae is small and distinct from the rose family (Rosaceae) that contains the true plum. The Chinese name 蜡梅 means "wax plum" — a reference to the flower's waxy, translucent petals, not to any botanical relation to the plum.

> **A naming caution:** 蜡梅 (wintersweet, *Chimonanthus praecox*, Calycanthaceae) is **not** 梅花 (plum blossom, *Prunus mume*, Rosaceae). They share a winter-blooming season and a "梅" in their names, but they are unrelated plants with different scent characters — and each has its own entry in this encyclopedia.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Garden culture:** wintersweet is a celebrated winter ornamental of classical Chinese gardens, valued for flowering in the coldest months when little else blooms.
- **Incense:** the flower is used as a soft, honeyed winter floral note in blends, its scent evoking the deep-winter bloom.
- **Materia medica:** 蜡梅 appears in traditional Chinese medicine with described uses. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
