> **Direct answer:** Lily of the valley (铃兰, línglán) is the flower of *Convallaria majalis*, a small perennial of the asparagus family (Asparagaceae) native to the northern temperate zone. Its bell-shaped flowers carry a delicate, green, sweet, fresh floral scent prized in modern perfumery. It is a modern fragrance flower, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient — and it is poisonous, never to be burned.

## A toxicity warning (read this first)

> **Direct answer:** Lily of the valley is poisonous. All parts of *Convallaria majalis* contain cardiac glycosides (including convallatoxin and convalloside), which are toxic if ingested and can be harmful through the skin or eyes. Do not ingest any part, do not burn it as incense, and keep it away from children and pets.

铃兰 (lily of the valley) is **poisonous**, and this fact comes before everything else on this page:

- All parts of *Convallaria majalis* contain **cardiac glycosides** (including convallatoxin and convalloside), which are toxic if ingested and can be harmful through the skin or eyes.
- **Do not ingest any part of the plant, and do not burn it as incense.** Combustion does not make it safe and can release harmful smoke.
- Keep away from children and pets; wash hands after handling.

## Botanical source and origin

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 铃兰 (línglán) means "bell orchid," naming the flower's bell shape — but the plant is not an orchid; it is an asparagus-family perennial. The name is ornamental, not a classical 香材 term, and it carries no incense or materia-medica connotation.

The name 铃兰 is a descriptive-ornamental coinage — 铃 "bell" + 兰 "orchid" — naming the bell-shaped flowers, even though the plant is not an orchid but an Asparagaceae perennial. The name has no classical 香材 or materia-medica status, which is consistent with the plant's actual position in China: an ornamental garden and cut flower, not an incense material. ## Why is the "muguet" note used in perfumery?

> **Direct answer:** The muguet (lily-of-the-valley) note is a classic fresh, green floral in fine fragrance, prized for its airy, clean, dewy character. It is almost always recreated synthetically — the flower's scent is fleeting and the plant is poisonous — so "muguet" in perfumery is an accord, not a flower extract.

## How is the note produced?

> **Direct answer:** The muguet note is produced synthetically, not from the flower. Perfumery uses muguet-building molecules — hydroxycitronellal, lilial, and related compounds — to construct the green, sweet, fresh floral accord, because the natural flower is both fleeting and poisonous.

There is no meaningful flower extraction for lily of the valley; the note is a synthetic construction. Perfumers build the muguet accord from dedicated muguet molecules (hydroxycitronellal, lilial, and related compounds) blended to reproduce the green, sweet, fresh, dewy character. This is a deliberate, well-established synthetic route, and it is the *only* responsible route: the natural plant is poisonous and must not be used as an aromatic raw material.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** "Muguet" quality is a synthetic-accord question, not a flower question. A good accord is clean, fresh, and true to the dewy green-floral character, without a harsh or chemical edge. Because the note is synthetic, the quality checks are purity, consistency, and correct odor.

For a buyer, "muguet" quality is judged at the accord level: the checks are **purity**, **consistency**, and **odor fidelity** (a clean, dewy, green-floral character without a harsh or chemical edge). There is no botanical-grade question, because the natural flower is not a legitimate raw material — it is poisonous and its scent is fleeting. The buyer's conversation is therefore entirely about the synthetic accord or the finished fragrance, not about sourcing a plant.
