> **Direct answer:** Juniper (杜松, dùsōng) is the common juniper, *Juniperus communis*, an evergreen conifer of the cypress family (Cupressaceae). It is best known in the West as the source of juniper berries — the flavor of gin — and as a clean, piney aromatic wood and needle material. In Chinese incense it is a modern crossover, absent from the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) and the *Bencao Gangmu*.

> **Direct answer:** Juniper is woody and piney, resinous and fresh, with a clean, bracing, slightly camphoraceous lift. It is brighter and drier than cedarwood, and less smoky than cypress. ## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 杜松 means "juniper" — 松 (the pine/conifer character) + 杜 (a qualifier). The 松 character, like 柏, names a broad conifer class, which is why 杜松 (juniper) and 雪松 (cedar) and the pines get run together in casual use despite naming different plants.

The name 杜松 (dùsōng) joins 松, the character for the pine/conifer class, with the qualifier 杜. As with 柏 in 崖柏 and 柏子, the 松 character is a *category* marker — "conifer" — not a species name. This is why 杜松 (juniper) sits confusingly near 雪松 (cedar) and the true pines in Chinese naming: they share a conifer register but are unrelated plants with different uses.

## A modern cross-over, honestly stated (read this)

> **Direct answer:** 杜松 is a modern aromatic borrowed from Western perfumery, not a classical Chinese incense material. Common juniper does not appear in the 香乘 or the 本草 incense tradition, and Flora of China does not record *Juniperus communis* among its native junipers. The honest, useful fact is this absence: any claim of an antique 杜松 lineage should be treated with skepticism.

杜松 must be understood for what it is: **a modern aromatic, not a classical Chinese incense material.** The common juniper *Juniperus communis* does not appear in the classical Chinese incense compendium 香乘, nor is it a documented 本草 incense ingredient, and Flora of China does not record the species among the native junipers it treats (FOC lists *J. formosana*, *J. chinensis*, *J. rigida*, and others, but not *J. communis*). Its presence in Chinese-language incense culture is a contemporary borrowing from Western perfumery and botanical aromatics, where juniper — through gin, bath products, and wood-based incense — is deeply familiar.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Juniper is used as the wood, the needles, and the berries, each carrying the plant's volatile oils. The wood is dried and cut or ground; the needles are dried and crushed; the berries are dried whole or crushed. No resin tapping or distillation step is needed for the incense forms — though juniper berry oil is a separate distilled product.

The three aromatic parts — wood, needles, and berries — are handled by drying and, where needed, grinding:

- **Wood** — dried, then cut into chips or ground to powder for blending and burning.
- **Needles** — dried and crushed, contributing a sharper, fresher green note.
- **Berries** — dried whole or crushed; the culinary part, best known for gin, and a distinct aromatic from the wood.

Juniper berry oil, obtained by distillation, is a separate commercial product from the dried plant material used in incense.

## What determines juniper quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness of the piney-resinous aroma and the condition of the material — clean, well-dried wood, needles, and berries that retain their volatile oils. Juniper volatiles fade with age, so a fresh, bracing batch beats a flat, woody one.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Aroma freshness** — a clean, bracing, piney-resinous character; juniper's volatiles fade with age and poor storage.
2. **Condition** — clean, well-dried material free of mold, dust, and excess stem.
3. **Part identity** — wood, needles, and berries carry different balances of the same character; specify the part when sourcing.
4. **Species clarity** — common juniper (*Juniperus communis*), distinct from "cedarwood" junipers such as *J. virginiana*.

## Juniper vs cedar vs thuja

> **Direct answer:** These are three conifer materials with woody aromas but different identities and sourcing profiles. Juniper is *Juniperus communis*, a modern cross-over; cedar is true cedar (*Cedrus*) or trade "cedarwood" (*Juniperus virginiana*); thuja is the scarce endemic wood of *Thuja sutchuenensis*. Juniper is the bright, fresh, widely available one of the three.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Juniper's commercial lesson is that a material can enter a tradition from outside it and still be fully legitimate — provided the label is honest about that entry. It is a bright, cheap, reliable conifer note; the buyer's real risk is not quality but provenance inflation, where a modern Western aromatic is dressed up as a classical Chinese 香材.

From a manufacturing perspective, juniper is a case in **provenance honesty rather than sourcing difficulty**:

- **It is abundant and cheap, and that is its strength.** As the world's most widely distributed conifer, juniper is a dependable, low-cost evergreen note. A manufacturer can build a repeatable product on it without the scarcity anxiety of agarwood or 崖柏.
- **The risk is narrative, not material.** The temptation with any modern aromatic is to attach an invented antique lineage to justify a premium. With 杜松, that would be fabrication — it has no classical Chinese incense history. The disciplined approach is to present it as what it is: a modern, Western-origin conifer note, and let it earn its place on aroma and cost rather than story.
- **Know the "cedarwood" trap.** Because trade "cedarwood" often means a *Juniperus*, juniper and cedar are routinely confused in sourcing. Pinning down the species is the practical first step.

In our view, juniper is a clean, bright, reliable conifer whose honest framing is itself the useful discipline: buy it by species, price it by its real abundance, and resist the temptation to invent a classical origin it never had.

## FAQ

**What is 杜松 (juniper)?** The common juniper, *Juniperus communis*, an evergreen conifer of the cypress family (Cupressaceae) native across the cool-temperate Northern Hemisphere. It is the source of juniper berries — the flavor of gin — and a clean, piney aromatic wood and needle material. In Chinese incense it is a modern cross-over, not a classical ingredient.

**Is juniper a classical Chinese incense material?** No. Common juniper does not appear in the Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) or the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), and Flora of China does not record *Juniperus communis* among the junipers it treats. Its presence in Chinese-language incense is a contemporary borrowing from Western aromatics. Any antique 杜松 lineage claim should be treated with skepticism.

**What does juniper smell like?** Woody and piney, resinous and fresh, with a clean, bracing, slightly camphoraceous lift. It is brighter and drier than cedarwood and less smoky than cypress. The berries are sharper and more culinary; the wood and needles give the familiar evergreen warmth.

**What part of the juniper is used?** The wood, the needle-like leaves, and the fleshy blue-black "berries" (botanically modified cones). All three carry the volatile oils that give juniper its clean, piney-resinous character. The berries are best known for flavoring gin.

**Is juniper the same as cedar?** Not botanically. Juniper is *Juniperus* (Cupressaceae); true cedar is *Cedrus* (Pinaceae). But in trade, "cedarwood" often means a *Juniperus* such as *J. virginiana*, which is why the two are frequently confused. Juniper is brighter and drier than true cedarwood.

**How should I buy juniper for incense?** Confirm the species (*Juniperus communis* rather than a "cedarwood" juniper), specify the part (wood, needles, or berries), and check for a fresh, bracing, piney-resinous aroma. It is abundant and inexpensive, so price it by its real availability — and do not accept a premium justified by an invented classical Chinese lineage.
