> **Direct answer:** Juniper (杜松, dùsōng) is the common juniper, *Juniperus communis*, an evergreen conifer of the cypress family (Cupressaceae). It is best known in the West as the source of juniper berries — the flavor of gin — and as a clean, piney aromatic wood and needle material. In Chinese incense it is a modern crossover, absent from the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) and the *Bencao Gangmu*.

## Aroma profile

Juniper is woody and piney, resinous and fresh, with a clean, bracing, slightly camphoraceous lift. It is brighter and drier than cedarwood, and less smoky than cypress. The berries carry a sharper, more culinary green-pine note; the wood and needles give the familiar evergreen warmth. In incense the wood reads as a *clear, northern conifer* — a clean-burning base or mid note in modern and Western-style blends.

## A modern cross-over, honestly stated (read this)

杜松 must be understood for what it is: **a modern aromatic, not a classical Chinese incense material.** The common juniper *Juniperus communis* does not appear in the classical Chinese incense compendium 香乘, nor is it a documented 本草 incense ingredient, and Flora of China does not record the species among the native junipers it treats (FOC lists *J. formosana*, *J. chinensis*, *J. rigida*, and others, but not *J. communis*). Its presence in Chinese-language incense culture is a contemporary borrowing from Western perfumery and botanical aromatics, where juniper — through gin, bath products, and wood-based incense — is deeply familiar.

**This page describes 杜松 = common juniper as a modern cross-over.** We do not invent a classical origin for it. That honesty is itself the useful fact: if a product or formula claims an antique 杜松 lineage, treat the claim with skepticism.

## Botanical source and origin

*Juniperus communis* is the most widely distributed conifer in the world, native across the cool-temperate and subarctic Northern Hemisphere — Europe, Asia, and North America — though it is not a Flora of China species. It is a shrub or small tree with sharp, needle-like leaves and, on female plants, the fleshy, blue-black "berries" (botanically modified cones) that flavor gin. The aromatic material is the wood, the needles, and the berries, all carrying the volatile oils that give juniper its clean, piney-resinous character.

## In modern incense and aromatics

- **Western aromatic staple:** juniper is a familiar clean, evergreen note in Western incense, room fragrance, and bath products, and the defining botanical of gin.
- **Wood and needle use:** the wood burns clean and piney; the needles and berries contribute a sharper, fresher lift.
- **Not a classical Chinese material:** 杜松 has no documented role in the 香乘 or 本草 incense tradition, and this entry records that absence honestly.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

There is **no classical Chinese incense tradition** for common juniper to describe. Its aromatic use is modern and Western in origin, and well documented in that context. We make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Cupressaceae placement, and the modern aromatic use of wood, needles, and berries.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 杜松 is common juniper (*Juniperus communis*); expect a woody, piney, resinous, fresh character, brighter and drier than cedarwood.
- It is a modern aromatic, not a classical Chinese incense material — verify any claim of antique provenance.
- Distinguish it from the true cedars (*Cedrus*) and from cypress-family relatives; "cedarwood" in trade often means a *Juniperus* (such as *J. virginiana*), which is why the two are easily confused.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is 杜松 (juniper)?** The common juniper, *Juniperus communis*, an evergreen conifer of the cypress family (Cupressaceae) native across the cool-temperate Northern Hemisphere. It is the source of juniper berries — the flavor of gin — and a clean, piney aromatic wood and needle material. In Chinese incense it is a modern cross-over, not a classical ingredient.

**Is juniper a classical Chinese incense material?** No. Common juniper does not appear in the Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) or the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), and Flora of China does not record *Juniperus communis* among the junipers it treats. Its presence in Chinese-language incense is a contemporary borrowing from Western aromatics. Any antique 杜松 lineage claim should be treated with skepticism.

**What does juniper smell like?** Woody and piney, resinous and fresh, with a clean, bracing, slightly camphoraceous lift. It is brighter and drier than cedarwood and less smoky than cypress. The berries are sharper and more culinary; the wood and needles give the familiar evergreen warmth.

**What part of the juniper is used?** The wood, the needle-like leaves, and the fleshy blue-black "berries" (botanically modified cones). All three carry the volatile oils that give juniper its clean, piney-resinous character. The berries are best known for flavoring gin.

**Is juniper the same as cedar?** Not botanically. Juniper is *Juniperus* (Cupressaceae); true cedar is *Cedrus* (Pinaceae). But in trade, "cedarwood" often means a *Juniperus* such as *J. virginiana*, which is why the two are frequently confused. Juniper is brighter and drier than true cedarwood.
