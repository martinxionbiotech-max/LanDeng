**Technical answer:** 砂仁 — *shārén* in Chinese — is the dried fruit (and its seeds) of *Amomum villosum* (recently reclassified as *Wurfbainia villosa* in some databases), a ginger-family herb (Zingiberaceae) native to southern China. It is a spicy, camphoraceous, warm aromatic used in Chinese incense as a **香药 (aromatic-medicine) spice accent**, and it is recorded in the classical compendium 香乘 as an incense-recipe ingredient. Its closest relatives in this encyclopedia are the other ginger-family aromatics — 白豆蔻 (cardamom) and 草果 (tsaoko).

## Aroma profile

砂仁 is spicy and camphoraceous, with a warm, slightly bitter depth that recalls cardamom but with a more medicinal, eucalyptus-like edge. It is a *warming spice* rather than a sweet one — closer to white cardamom or a sharp galangal note than to cinnamon's sweetness. In a blend it adds a camphoraceous warmth and a subtle bitterness beneath brighter spices, and it is more often part of a mixed 合香 recipe than burned alone.

## Botanical source and origin

*Amomum villosum* is a ginger-family (Zingiberaceae) herb native to southern China (Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan) and neighbouring parts of Southeast Asia. The part used is the dried, spiny-shelled fruit, whose aromatic seeds are the source of the spice. Flora of China records it as *Amomum villosum*; GBIF has recently transferred the name to *Wurfbainia villosa* — a reclassification worth noting, since older and Chinese materia-medica sources retain *Amomum villosum*. The ginger family also includes 白豆蔻 (*Amomum kravanh*), 草果 (*Amomum tsao-ko*), and 高良姜 (galangal).

## In Chinese tradition

- **香药 (aromatic medicine):** 砂仁 is a classic 香药, an aromatic used both as a spice and in traditional medicine, and it appears in incense recipes.
- **Incense recipes:** 砂仁 is recorded in 香乘 as an ingredient in blended incense (for example, in the 南阳公主熏衣香 recipe).
- **Materia medica:** 砂仁 is recorded in traditional Chinese medicine. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The 香药 and incense-recipe use is documented in the classical record (香乘). Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Zingiberaceae placement, and the spicy camphoraceous aromatic use.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 砂仁 is the fruit of *Amomum villosum*; the aromatic value is in the seeds, so intact, well-filled fruits are preferred.
- Expect a warm, camphoraceous, slightly bitter spice — distinct from the sweeter 白豆蔻 (cardamom) and the smokier 草果 (tsaoko).
- In incense, 砂仁 is a blending spice accent, usually part of a mixed recipe rather than a solo note.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is 砂仁 (sharen)?** The dried fruit and seeds of *Amomum villosum* (recently reclassified as *Wurfbainia villosa* in some databases), a ginger-family herb (Zingiberaceae) native to southern China. It is a spicy, camphoraceous, warm aromatic used in Chinese incense as a 香药 spice accent, and it is recorded in the classical compendium 香乘 as an incense-recipe ingredient.

**What is the difference between 砂仁 and cardamom (白豆蔻)?** Both are ginger-family *Amomum* fruits, but distinct species with different characters. 砂仁 is *Amomum villosum* — warm, camphoraceous, and slightly bitter. 白豆蔻 (cardamom) is *Amomum kravanh* — sweeter, more eucalyptus-bright. They are separate 香药 ingredients, each with its own entry.

**What does 砂仁 smell like?** Spicy and camphoraceous, with a warm, slightly bitter depth that recalls cardamom but with a more medicinal, eucalyptus-like edge. It is a warming spice rather than a sweet one. In a blend it adds camphoraceous warmth and subtle bitterness, and it is usually part of a mixed 合香 recipe rather than burned alone.

**Is 砂仁 in the classical incense record?** Yes — it is recorded in 香乘 as an ingredient in blended incense recipes (for example, 南阳公主熏衣香). As a 香药 it served both as a spice and an aromatic medicine, and its incense use is documented in the classical record rather than being a modern invention.

**Is 砂仁 a medicine?** It is recorded in traditional Chinese medicine as a 香药 (aromatic medicine). That is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence**, and this encyclopedia makes no medical claims. Its documented non-medical uses are the spicy aromatic and incense-recipe roles described here.
