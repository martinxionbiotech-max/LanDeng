> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf (桑叶, sāngyè) is the leaf of the white mulberry, *Morus alba*, a tree (Moraceae) cultivated across China for its silkworm-feeding leaves. It has a mild, green, tea-like, slightly sweet scent, and enters Chinese tradition chiefly as a fragrant tea and a materia-medica leaf — the "茶香" (tea-fragrance) context — rather than as a prominent incense material.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 桑叶 has a mild, clean, green, tea-like scent with a faint sweetness — the gentle aroma of a brewed mulberry-leaf tea. It is a quiet, leafy-green note, closer to green tea than to any strong aromatic, and it works as a subtle green accent in sachets and tea-fragrance contexts rather than as a dominant incense note.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Morus alba*, the white mulberry, is a shrub or small tree 3–10 m tall of the fig family (Moraceae), with ovate, irregularly lobed leaves 5–30 × 5–12 cm and a compound fruit (syncarp) that ripens from red to blackish-purple. It is originally endemic to central and northern China, now cultivated throughout China as silkworm fodder and for its fruit, bark fiber, and bark medicine.

*Morus alba*, the white mulberry, is a shrub or small tree of the fig family (Moraceae), native to China and cultivated for thousands of years as the food plant of the silkworm. Flora of China records it as a **shrub or tree 3–10 m tall**, with **ovate to broadly ovate, irregularly lobed leaves 5–30 × 5–12 cm**, and a **compound fruit (syncarp) that is red when immature and blackish-purple, purple, or greenish-white when mature, 1–2.5 cm**. It is recorded as **originally endemic to central and northern China, now cultivated throughout China**, and Flora of China adds that **"the leaves provide food for silkworms, the bark fiber is used for textiles and paper, and the bark is also used for medicine."** The leaf (桑叶) and the fruit (桑葚) are distinct parts of the same tree. See [Morus alba in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200006379).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 桑叶 (sāngyè) means "mulberry leaf" — 桑 "mulberry" + 叶 "leaf" — and it names the leaf specifically, as distinct from the fruit 桑葚 and the root bark 桑白皮. The tree 桑 is one of the most culturally loaded plants in China, tied to sericulture, and the *Bencao Gangmu* records it under 桑 in its tree section.

The Chinese terminology separates the parts of one culturally central tree:

- **桑 (sāng)** — the mulberry tree, the food plant of the silkworm, one of China's most economically and culturally important trees.
- **桑叶 (sāngyè)** — the leaf, the silkworm fodder and tea/materia-medica part.
- **桑葚 (sāngshèn)** — the fruit.
- **桑白皮 (sāngbáipí)** — the root bark, a separate materia-medica part.

The part-precision matters: 桑叶 is the leaf, not the fruit, and not the root bark — three different materials from one tree. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records the tree under 桑 in its tree section (木之三), with the leaf among its parts.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Tea (Traditional Use):** mulberry-leaf tea (桑叶茶) is a traditional fragrant, cooling drink, the "茶香" context in which the leaf's mild green scent is most familiar.
- **Materia medica (Traditional Use):** 桑叶 appears in traditional Chinese materia medica, and the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records the tree under 桑 (木之三), with 桑叶 among its parts. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **Incense (Traditional Use):** mulberry leaf is not a lead 香材; its role is a quiet leafy-green accent.

## Why is mulberry leaf used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf is not a lead incense material; where it appears, it lends a quiet, green, tea-like accent — a subtle leafy note in sachets and tea-fragrance blends. Its value is mildness and freshness, not intensity, and it is almost always a supporting element.

Mulberry leaf's incense role is a **quiet green accent**. Its mild, tea-like note can lift a sachet or a light blend with a fresh, leafy character, but it does not carry the intensity of the woods and resins, and it is not a lead 香材. In practice it is used as a supporting element — a subtle green note in the tea-fragrance register — rather than burned as a dominant material. This is consistent with its primary life as a tea leaf and silkworm fodder: a quiet, useful leaf whose value is mildness, not power.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf is processed by harvesting and drying the leaf — the same basic step as tea — and it is used whole or cut, or ground for blending. There is no distillation or extraction in the leaf form; drying is the processing question, and freshness governs the mild green note.

The processing of 桑叶 is leaf-drying, essentially the same step as tea:

1. **Harvest** — the leaves are harvested.
2. **Dry** — the leaves are dried (traditionally, as for tea).
3. **Use whole / cut / ground** — the dried leaf is used whole or cut for tea and sachets, or ground for blending.

There is no distillation or extraction step — mulberry leaf is a whole dried leaf. As with other leafy materials, **drying temperature and freshness** govern how much of the mild, green note survives.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on a clean, green, fresh leaf with a mild, tea-like scent and no mustiness. There is no standardized grading ladder; freshness, cleanliness, and the green tea-like character are the practical signals.

The quality factors:

1. **Freshness** — the mild green note fades with age.
2. **Cleanliness** — clean, sound leaf material without mustiness or foreign matter.
3. **Colour and character** — a clean green leaf with a tea-like, slightly sweet scent.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for 桑叶; freshness and the green tea-like character are the practical signals.

## Mulberry leaf vs other leafy materials

> **Direct answer:** Mulberry leaf (桑叶), bamboo leaves (竹叶), and arborvitae leaves (侧柏叶) are all leafy materials, but they differ by plant and role: mulberry leaf is a tea-fragrance, silkworm-fodder leaf; bamboo leaves are a green, fresh leaf; arborvitae leaves are a materia-medica leaf from a conifer. Leafy materials share a green register but are distinct species with distinct roles.

## FAQ

**What is 桑叶 (mulberry leaf)?** The leaf of the white mulberry, *Morus alba* (Moraceae), a tree cultivated across China for its silkworm-feeding leaves. It has a mild, green, tea-like, slightly sweet scent and is used chiefly as a fragrant tea and a materia-medica leaf.

**What tree does 桑叶 come from?** The white mulberry (桑, *Morus alba*), the food plant of the silkworm, native to China. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records the tree under 桑 in its tree section (木之三), with the leaf (桑叶) among its parts alongside the fruit (桑葚) and root bark.

**What does 桑叶 smell like?** Mild, clean, green, and tea-like, with a faint sweetness — the gentle aroma of brewed mulberry-leaf tea. It is a quiet, leafy-green note, closer to green tea than to a strong aromatic, best used as a subtle accent.

**Is mulberry leaf an incense ingredient?** Not a lead one. Its traditional role is in tea (桑叶茶) and materia medica, in the "tea-fragrance" context, and it can lend a quiet green accent to a sachet or blend, but it does not carry the intensity of the resins and woods.

**Is 桑叶 the same as 桑葚 (mulberry fruit)?** No — different parts of the same tree. 桑叶 is the leaf; 桑葚 is the fruit. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records both under the tree 桑, but they are distinct parts with distinct uses, alongside the root bark (桑白皮).

**Why is the mulberry culturally important in China?** Because it is the food plant of the silkworm, the foundation of China's sericulture industry for thousands of years. Flora of China records that the leaves feed silkworms, the bark fiber is used for textiles and paper, and the bark is also used for medicine — making the mulberry one of the most economically central trees in Chinese history.
