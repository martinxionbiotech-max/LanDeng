> **Direct answer:** Cassia twig (桂枝, guìzhī) is the young, slender branch of the cassia tree, *Cinnamomum cassia* (syn. *C. aromaticum*), the same tree that yields cinnamon bark (桂皮). It has a warm, spicy, sweet, woody-bark scent and is a classical Chinese materia-medica part. It is the young twig of the same tree as 桂皮 — same species, different part.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 桂枝 has a warm, spicy, sweet, woody-cinnamon scent — the familiar cassia aroma, but from the young twig rather than the mature bark. It is lighter and more woody-green than the dense, pungent bark (桂皮), carrying the same cinnamon warmth with a fresher, branch-like edge.

The aroma difference is a *part* difference: the bark concentrates the tree's pungent aromatic oils at their densest, while the young twig carries the same warmth more lightly, with a greener, woodier character. This is the same "same tree, different part" logic that separates 桂皮 from 桂枝 — and it is the whole reason the two are distinguished in the classical record.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Cinnamomum cassia* (syn. *C. aromaticum*), the Chinese cassia, is a laurel-family tree (Lauraceae) native to southern China and Vietnam. Its bark is the spice "cassia" (桂皮), and its young twigs (桂枝) are a distinct materia-medica part.

## Why is cassia twig used?

> **Direct answer:** Cassia twig is primarily a materia-medica part, but its warm, spicy, woody-cinnamon scent can lend a woody-spicy accent. It is the twig of the same tree as the incense spice cassia (桂皮), but its classical role is in materia medica rather than as a lead 香材.

Cassia twig's role is principally **materia medica**, not incense. Its warm, spicy, woody-cinnamon scent can lend a woody-spicy accent — the same cassia warmth as the bark, but lighter and greener — but the classical record places it in the materia-medica canon, not among the lead 香材. Where it does appear aromatically, it functions as a warm, spicy-woody support note, and its identity is inseparable from the bark of the same tree.

This is the key asymmetry: 桂皮 (the bark) is the famous spice and incense material; 桂枝 (the twig) is the materia-medica part of the same tree. The twig is not "lesser cassia" — it is a *different part with a different classical role*.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Cassia twig is processed by harvesting the young, slender branches, cleaning and drying them, and using them whole or cut. There is no distillation or extraction; it is a whole dried twig, and drying preserves its warm, spicy, woody character.

The processing is straightforward:

1. **Harvest** — the young, slender branches are cut from the cassia tree.
2. **Clean and dry** — the twigs are cleaned and dried to a stable, storable form.
3. **Cut or use whole** — dried twigs are used whole or cut into lengths.

There is no distillation or extraction step; cassia twig is a whole dried branch. The practical variables are **harvest age** (young twig vs older branch) and **drying**, which preserve its warm, spicy, woody character.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Cassia twig quality is a matter of part and condition: a clean, dry, young twig with a warm, spicy, woody-cinnamon scent. The key distinction is the part — twig (桂枝) vs bark (桂皮) — and freshness of the aromatic character.

The quality factors:

1. **Part** — the decisive factor; 桂枝 (young twig) vs 桂皮 (bark) are different materials with different characters.
2. **Condition** — clean, dry, intact twigs without mold or damage.
3. **Aroma** — a warm, spicy, woody-cinnamon scent, lighter and greener than the bark.

There is no standardized grading ladder; the part distinction and the aromatic freshness are the practical signals.
