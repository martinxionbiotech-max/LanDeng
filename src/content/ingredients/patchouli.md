# Patchouli (广藿香 / Guang Huo Xiang)

**Direct answer:** Patchouli — *guǎnghuòxiāng* (广藿香) in Chinese — is the dried leaf of *Pogostemon cablin*, a bushy mint-family herb, with a heavy, earthy, woody, balsamic aroma. It is an important ingredient in East Asian incense and a classic perfumery base note.

## Aroma profile

Patchouli has a heavy, earthy, woody-balsamic scent with a musty-sweet, spicy edge. It is a strong base note that lingers. Like vetiver, it tends to be a supporting/base note rather than the star.

The character is **earthy and "rooted"** — wet soil, dry wood, and a faint sweetness — rather than bright or floral. It is long-lasting and penetrating, which is why a little patchouli can anchor an entire blend and why it became the signature of a whole era of fragrance (the 1960s–70s association in the West).

## A Chinese naming distinction (广藿香 vs 藿香)

Chinese has two "huo xiang" herbs that English blurs together:

- **广藿香 (*Pogostemon cablin*)** — patchouli, the earthy incense/perfume material.
- **藿香 (*Agastache rugosa*)** — Korean mint / giant hyssop, a different herb used in Chinese medicine and cooking.

They share a name and a mint-family relationship but are different plants with different aromas and uses. In an incense or perfume context, 广藿香 means patchouli.

## In East Asian incense

Patchouli is an **important ingredient in East Asian incense**, not just a Western "hippie" scent (its 1960s–70s popularity in the West is a more recent chapter). In China, 广藿香 also appears in traditional materia medica.

- **Incense:** patchouli is used in Chinese and Japanese incense as an earthy base, often blended with woods and resins.
- **Perfumery:** it is a classic base note in Western perfumery (chypre, oriental families).
- **Insect repellent:** patchouli has a long folk use as an insect deterrent, especially for protecting textiles.

## Patchouli vs vetiver

Both are earthy base notes, but patchouli is the leaf (mint family) and vetiver the root (grass). Patchouli is mustier and spicier; vetiver is smokier and smoother. They are often used together to build a deep, earthy foundation under woods and florals.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Patchouli is used as an insect repellent and in traditional medicine; early research on its anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties exists but is **preliminary, not established clinical fact.** We describe its aromatic and incense use; we do not make medical claims.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Patchouli is potent — a little goes a long way.

## FAQ

**Is patchouli a wood?** No — it is the dried leaf of a mint-family herb (*Pogostemon cablin*).

**Is patchouli only a "hippie" scent?** Its 1960s–70s Western popularity is well-known, but patchouli has a much longer history in East Asian incense and perfumery.

**What is the difference between 广藿香 and 藿香?** 广藿香 is patchouli (*Pogostemon cablin*); 藿香 is Korean mint (*Agastache rugosa*). They share a name and mint-family relationship but are different plants.

## People Also Ask (Long-Tail)

**What does patchouli smell like?** Heavy, earthy, woody-balsamic with a musty-sweet, spicy edge — a strong base note that lingers.

**Where does patchouli come from?** Southeast Asia — patchouli (*Pogostemon cablin*) is native to the region and has long been an ingredient in East Asian incense and perfumery, well before its 1960s–70s Western popularity.

**What does patchouli pair with?** It anchors heavier blends — often paired with woods (sandalwood, cedar) and resins, or brightened with citrus. Like vetiver, it works as a base rather than a solo star.

**Is patchouli a wood?** No — it is the dried leaf of a mint-family herb (*Pogostemon cablin*), steam-distilled for its oil.

**How is patchouli oil made?** The dried leaves are steam-distilled. Interestingly, patchouli oil is often described as improving with age, mellowing from a sharp green note to a deeper, smoother earthiness.

**Is patchouli oil aged?** Patchouli oil is often described as improving with age, mellowing from a sharp, green note into a deeper, smoother earthiness. This is a perfumery observation, not a standardized grading rule.
