> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel (杏仁, xìngrén) is the kernel of the apricot, *Prunus armeniaca*, a stone fruit of the rose family (Rosaceae) long cultivated in China and Central Asia. The kernel has a sweet, nutty, almond-like scent and a bitter undertone from its amygdalin content. The *Bencao Gangmu* records 杏 in its fruit section, with 杏仁 as the material of value.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel is sweet and nutty, with a marzipan-like almondy character and a clean bitter edge — the scent family shared by bitter almonds, cherry laurel, and other *Prunus* kernels. The bitterness reflects the kernel's amygdalin, which releases benzaldehyde — the source of the almond note — when the kernel is crushed or moistened.

## Botanical source and origin

## The almond-note chemistry (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The "almond" scent of apricot kernel is a molecule, not a plant identity. Apricot, bitter almond, and cherry laurel are all *Prunus* kernels whose amygdalin releases benzaldehyde when crushed or moistened — and benzaldehyde is the almond note. So "almond-smelling" spans several species sharing one chemistry.

The almond note deserves a precise framing, because it is the source of the most common confusion around 杏仁:

- **Amygdalin** is a cyanogenic glycoside present in many *Prunus* kernels — apricot, bitter almond, cherry laurel, peach.
- **When crushed or moistened**, amygdalin is acted on by enzymes to release **benzaldehyde** (the marzipan-like almond aroma) and **hydrogen cyanide** (the toxicity concern).
- **The almond note is benzaldehyde**, so several different kernels "smell of almond" because they share the same chemistry, not because they are the same plant.

This is why apricot kernel can stand in for almond in fragrance contexts — the shared benzaldehyde — and why bitter kernels carry a documented safety concern that sweet kernels carry in lesser degree.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel is processed by removing the stone and shelling the kernel, then used whole, ground, or pressed for oil. There is no classical incense-processing record; processing is culinary or oil-pressing, and the sweet-bitter note is released by crushing, which is also when the cyanogenic chemistry becomes relevant.

The processing of 杏仁 is culinary and oil-oriented:

1. **Shelling** — the stone is cracked to extract the kernel.
2. **Use whole / ground / pressed** — the kernel is used whole, ground, or cold-pressed for its oil.

There is no classical incense-processing record for 杏仁; it is a food and oil seed. The practical point is that **crushing or moistening releases the benzaldehyde note — and simultaneously activates the cyanogenic chemistry** — which is why bitter kernels are handled as a safety matter, not a casual flavoring.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on kernel soundness and the sweet-versus-bitter distinction — sweet kernels are the food-grade material, while bitter kernels are higher in amygdalin and treated as a safety concern. There is no incense-grading standard, because it is not an incense material.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Sweet vs bitter** — sweet kernels are the food-grade material; bitter kernels are higher in amygdalin (a cyanide precursor).
2. **Soundness** — clean, well-filled kernels without rancidity or insect damage.
3. **Aroma** — a sweet, nutty, marzipan-like character for the food-grade kernel.

The sweet-versus-bitter distinction is the decisive quality and safety axis, not a fragrance-grading ladder.

## Apricot kernel vs bitter almond

> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel (杏仁) and bitter almond are different *Prunus* kernels that share the same almond-note chemistry. Both contain amygdalin, which releases benzaldehyde (the almond scent) and cyanide; bitter almond is the higher-amygdalin, more intensely bitter kernel, while apricot kernel exists in sweet and bitter forms. The "almond" smell is the shared molecule, not a shared species.

| | Apricot kernel (杏仁) | Bitter almond |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Prunus armeniaca* | *Prunus amygdalus* var. *amara* (and other *Prunus*) |
| Character | Sweet-bitter, nutty, marzipan | Intensely bitter, marzipan |
| Chemistry | Amygdalin → benzaldehyde + cyanide | Amygdalin → benzaldehyde + cyanide (higher) |

Both are *Prunus* kernels converging on benzaldehyde, which is why they smell alike despite being different plants. The safety logic is shared: amygdalin is a cyanide precursor, and bitter forms carry the higher concern.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Apricot kernel's lesson is that a scent can be a molecule, not a plant — and that the almond note is chemistry (benzaldehyde) shared across several *Prunus* kernels. For a buyer, this means the note is reproducible and the real specification is the kernel's sweet-versus-bitter profile and its cyanogenic safety, not the species name alone.

From a manufacturing and sourcing perspective, 杏仁 illustrates how a fragrance note can be **decoupled from the plant that carries it**:

- **The note is a molecule.** The "almond" scent is benzaldehyde, released when *Prunus* kernels' amygdalin is crushed or moistened. Because several species share the chemistry, "almond-smelling" is not a species specification — and a formulator can often reach the same note by other means. This is the opposite of a material like agarwood, whose value is tied to a specific wood.
- **Sweet versus bitter is the real spec.** The practical distinction is not apricot-versus-almond but **sweet versus bitter kernel** — because that axis determines both aroma and the cyanogenic safety profile. A buyer who specifies only "杏仁" has not specified the safety-relevant variable.
- **Safety drives sourcing discipline here.** Amygdalin's cyanide release means bitter kernels are a genuine handling concern, not a flavor nuance. This is a rare case where the chemistry that produces the aroma is also the chemistry that produces the risk, so the sourcing spec and the safety spec are the same question.

In our view, the useful takeaway is procedural: **specify the kernel (sweet or bitter), understand the note as benzaldehyde chemistry, and treat the cyanogenic safety boundary as part of the specification** — rather than treating 杏仁 as a simple "almond-scented seed."
