**Technical answer:** Mastic — *mǎtízhī* (玛蹄脂) in Chinese — is the aromatic resin of *Pistacia lentiscus*, the mastic tree, a small evergreen shrub of the cashew family (Anacardiaceae) native to the Mediterranean, most famously cultivated on the Greek island of Chios. It is a fresh, piney, balsamic resin used since antiquity in incense, perfumery, and as a chewing gum, and known in Chinese sources as a **Western aromatic resin**. Its Chinese name 玛蹄脂 is also spelled 乳香脂 or 洋乳香 in some sources, which invites confusion with frankincense (乳香) — a different resin.

## Aroma profile

Mastic is fresh and piney, with a clean, balsamic sweetness and a hint of lemon and cedar — a brighter, more astringent resin than frankincense. When chewed or burned it releases a crisp, resinous freshness. In a blend it reads as a *bright resin top note*, adding lift and a clean piney character rather than the deep, sweet warmth of heavier resins.

## The mastic / frankincense confusion (read this)

Mastic (玛蹄脂, *Pistacia lentiscus*) and frankincense (乳香, *Boswellia* species) are **different resins from different plant families**, but their Chinese names invite confusion:

- **乳香 (frankincense)** — *Boswellia* species (family Burseraceae), a sweet, citrus, earthy resin. This is the classic 乳香 of Chinese incense, with its own entry.
- **玛蹄脂 / 洋乳香 (mastic)** — *Pistacia lentiscus* (family Anacardiaceae), a fresh, piney, balsamic resin. Some sources call mastic "乳香脂" or "洋乳香" ("foreign mastic"), which is where the confusion arises.

**This page describes 玛蹄脂 = *Pistacia lentiscus* = mastic.** When a source means 乳香, it is describing frankincense (*Boswellia*), a different material.

## Botanical source and origin

*Pistacia lentiscus* is a small evergreen shrub of the cashew family (Anacardiaceae), native to the Mediterranean region. The resin is tapped from incisions in the bark and hardens into pale, translucent "tears." The most famous source is the Greek island of Chios, whose mastic has protected designation-of-origin status. The family Anacardiaceae also includes pistachio (*Pistacia vera*) and, more distantly, sumac — a different family from the frankincense-bearing Burseraceae.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Western aromatic:** mastic entered Chinese sources as a Western/foreign aromatic resin (the "洋" in 洋乳香 marks it as imported), used in incense and perfumery.
- **Incense and perfumery:** mastic's fresh, piney character made it a bright resin note in blends across the Mediterranean and, later, the global trade.
- **Materia medica:** 玛蹄脂 / 洋乳香 appears in traditional Chinese medicine with described uses. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The incense and perfumery use is well documented in Western and, later, Chinese sources. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Anacardiaceae family placement, and the fresh piney aromatic use.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- Distinguish 玛蹄脂 (*Pistacia lentiscus*) from 乳香 (*Boswellia* frankincense) — different resins, different families, and the Chinese names overlap in some sources.
- Quality mastic is pale, clean, and translucent with a fresh, piney-lemon scent; dark or stale resin is lower grade.
- Mastic's freshness makes it a bright top note; it is not a deep, sweet base resin.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.
