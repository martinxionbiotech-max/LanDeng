> **Direct answer:** Copaiba (古巴香脂, gǔbā xiāngzhī) is the oleoresin tapped from trees of the genus *Copaifera*, a legume-family (Fabaceae) group native to tropical South America. It yields a woody, balsamic, resinous, slightly sweet balsam used in modern perfumery and aromatherapy. It is a modern crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Copaiba has a soft, woody, balsamic, resinous scent with a gentle, slightly sweet, almost creamy warmth — a mild, honeyed-wood balsam rather than a sharp pine or smoke. It is smoother and lighter than the heavier, vanilla-sweet Peru balsam.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Copaifera* is a genus of large legume-family trees (Fabaceae) native to tropical South America and parts of Africa. The balsam is a natural oleoresin tapped from the trunk and used directly, or steam-distilled into copaiba oil. *Copaifera* is a genus of large legume-family trees (Fabaceae) native to tropical South America and parts of Africa. The balsam is a natural oleoresin tapped from the trunk, collected much like a tree sap, and used directly or steam-distilled for copaiba oil. GBIF records the genus *Copaifera* (several species, all South American natives). It is not a Chinese species and has no classical Chinese record.

One botanical precision separates copaiba from the other "balsams": copaiba is an **oleoresin** — a naturally fluid mixture of resin and volatile oil that flows from a tapped trunk — rather than a hardened gum-resin like frankincense or a semi-solid balsam like Peru balsam. This fluidity is why copaiba is often steam-distilled into an essential oil, a processing route the harder resins do not take as readily. The genus is species-rich, and "copaiba" as a trade material may come from several *Copaifera* species, so genus-level identity — not a single species — is the honest botanical statement.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Copaiba is produced by tapping the trunk of a *Copaifera* tree to collect the oleoresin, which may be used directly or steam-distilled into copaiba oil. The oleoresin and the distilled oil are two products of the same source, chosen by downstream use.

The trunk is tapped and the oleoresin collected, much like collecting a tree sap. From there the material branches by end use: the **oleoresin** may be used directly as a balsam, or it may be **steam-distilled** into copaiba essential oil. The oil is lighter, more fluid, and easier to dose in aromatherapy, while the oleoresin carries a fuller resinous body. This two-route processing — direct balsam versus distilled oil — is a defining feature of copaiba and distinguishes it from resins that are used only in solid or powdered form.
