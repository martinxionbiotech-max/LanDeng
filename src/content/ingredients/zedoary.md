**Technical answer:** 莪术 — *ézhú* in Chinese — is the dried rhizome of the zedoary group, *Curcuma zedoaria* (with *Curcuma phaeocaulis* as the Flora-of-China species most often cited for Chinese material), a ginger-family (Zingiberaceae) perennial of South and Southeast Asia. It is a warm, camphoraceous, earthy rhizome in the same genus as turmeric, used primarily in traditional medicine and recorded in classical incense formulas as a minor ingredient. It is one of three closely related *Curcuma* rhizomes — 姜黄, 郁金, and 莪术 — that are routinely confused, and this page disambiguates them explicitly.

## Aroma profile

莪术 is earthy and camphoraceous, warm with a slightly bitter, medicinal edge — the most strongly medicinal of the three common *Curcuma* rhizomes. Like 姜黄 and 郁金 it is a grounded, warm base accent in the ginger-family register, but its character leans more pungent and less sweet than turmeric. In incense it is a minor supporting note rather than a fragrance in its own right.

## The 郁金 / 姜黄 / 莪术 confusion (read this)

Three closely related *Curcuma* rhizomes share overlapping Chinese names and are routinely confused, even in some classical texts. This encyclopedia distinguishes them by species:

- **姜黄** (*Curcuma longa*) — "turmeric," the yellow-orange culinary rhizome and coloring material. Its own entry.
- **郁金** (*Curcuma aromatica*) — a distinct rhizome used in traditional medicine, sometimes called "wild turmeric." Its own entry.
- **莪术** (*Curcuma zedoaria* / *Curcuma phaeocaulis*) — another distinct medicinal rhizome in the zedoary group. This entry.

The confusion arises because all three are *Curcuma* rhizomes with warm, earthy, camphoraceous characters, and historical sources have not always kept them apart. **This page describes 莪术 = the zedoary group (*Curcuma zedoaria*, cf. *C. phaeocaulis*).** When a source says 姜黄 or 郁金, it is describing a different material.

## Botanical source and origin

The zedoary group belongs to the ginger family (Zingiberaceae), native to South and Southeast Asia. The name *Curcuma zedoaria* is the long-standing designation for zedoary; Flora of China treats the Chinese material most often under *Curcuma phaeocaulis*, a closely related species. The part used is the dried rhizome. The genus *Curcuma* also includes 姜黄 (*Curcuma longa*) and 郁金 (*Curcuma aromatica*).

## In Chinese tradition

- **Materia medica:** 莪术 is recorded in traditional Chinese medicine, classically described as a warming aromatic. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **Classical incense:** 莪术 appears as a minor ingredient in classical incense formulas — the Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 莪术 in a processing note (莪术醋煮過用鹽水浸出…為末蜜和同甘草膏為餅), indicating a prepared, minor addition rather than a principal scent.
- **Not a culinary turmeric:** 莪术 is a medicinal rhizome, not the standard culinary turmeric (姜黄).

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The medicinal and minor incense use is documented. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the *Curcuma* genus placement, and the minor aromatic use in the turmeric group.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- Distinguish 莪术 (the zedoary group) from 姜黄 (*Curcuma longa*) and 郁金 (*Curcuma aromatica*) — three distinct *Curcuma* rhizomes routinely confused.
- Expect a warm, camphoraceous, slightly bitter medicinal character — the most medicinal of the three common *Curcuma* rhizomes.
- In incense, 莪术 is a minor supporting note, usually processed and blended, not burned alone.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.
