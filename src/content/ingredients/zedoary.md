> **Direct answer:** Zedoary (莪术, ézhú) is the dried rhizome of the zedoary group, *Curcuma zedoaria* (with *Curcuma phaeocaulis* most often cited for Chinese material), a ginger-family (Zingiberaceae) perennial of South and Southeast Asia. It is a warm, camphoraceous, earthy rhizome in the same genus as turmeric, used primarily in traditional medicine and recorded in classical incense formulas as a minor ingredient. It is one of three closely related *Curcuma* rhizomes that are routinely confused.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 莪术 is earthy and camphoraceous, warm with a slightly bitter, medicinal edge — the most strongly medicinal of the three common *Curcuma* rhizomes. Like 姜黄 and 郁金 it is a grounded, warm base accent in the ginger-family register, but its character leans more pungent and less sweet than turmeric.

## The processing note as identity evidence (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 莪术 only in a processing note — 莪术醋煮過用鹽水浸出…為末蜜和同甘草膏為餅 — meaning the rhizome is cooked in vinegar, soaked out in salt water, ground, and mixed with honey and licorice paste into cakes. Reading this note tells us 莪术 was a *prepared, minor* addition, not a principal scent.

Here is a small piece of textual evidence that does real work. The Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 莪术 not as a fragrance in its own right but only in a processing note:

> 莪术醋煮過用鹽水浸出…為末蜜和同甘草膏為餅

That is: the rhizome is boiled in vinegar, soaked out in salt water, ground to powder, and mixed with honey and a licorice paste to form cakes. The significance of this note:

- **The processing is the identity.** A material that must be vinegar-boiled and salt-water-soaked before it enters a formula is being *tamed*, not showcased. The note tells us 莪术 was a prepared, minor, medicinal-flavored addition — a corrector or balancer in a formula — rather than a named aromatic.
- **"Minor ingredient" is read from the recipe, not asserted.** This is the difference between saying "莪术 was minor" as a vague claim and *reading the processing note* that demonstrates it. The note is the evidence.
- **It contrasts with 姜黄 and 郁金.** Turmeric is a culinary coloring rhizome; 郁金 has its own classical aromatic-oil record. 莪术's record is a *processing instruction* — which is exactly what a minor medicinal ingredient leaves behind.

This is how an encyclopedia reads a recipe as evidence: the way a material is processed reveals its rank in the formula.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 莪术 is processed before use — the classical record specifies boiling in vinegar, soaking out in salt water, and grinding, then mixing with honey and licorice paste into cakes. This preparation tames the rhizome's medicinal edge and marks it as a prepared, minor addition.

The processing of 莪术 is itself the informative detail:

- **Vinegar-boiling** — the rhizome is boiled in vinegar, a step that alters and softens its sharp, medicinal character.
- **Salt-water soaking** — the boiled rhizome is soaked out in salt water, further moderating it.
- **Grinding and binding** — the prepared rhizome is ground to powder and mixed with honey and a licorice paste (甘草膏) into cakes for storage and use.

This multi-step preparation is what marks 莪术 as a *prepared, minor* ingredient: a material showcased as a fragrance is not treated this way.
