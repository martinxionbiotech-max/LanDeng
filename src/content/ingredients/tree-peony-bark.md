**Direct answer:** Tree peony bark — *mǔdānpí* (牡丹皮) in Chinese — is the dried root bark of *Paeonia suffruticosa*, the tree peony (Paeoniaceae) native to China. It is a classical Chinese materia-medica part with a faint, rooty, earthy, woody scent. It is the **root bark of the very same tree as 牡丹 (tree peony, the flower) — same species, different part** — and the two must be kept distinct.

## Aroma profile

牡丹皮 has a quiet, rooty, earthy, woody scent with a faint, dry floral undertone — the muted aroma of a dried root bark, far less fragrant than the tree peony's flower. It is a subtle material, its fame in materia medica rather than in scent; in an incense blend it reads as a faint earthy-woody accent at most.

## The 牡丹皮 / 牡丹 distinction (read this)

牡丹皮 and 牡丹 come from the **same tree, different parts**:

- **牡丹皮 (tree peony bark)** — the dried root bark of *Paeonia suffruticosa*. This entry.
- **牡丹 (tree peony)** — the flower of the same tree, the celebrated "king of flowers" (花王). Its own entry.

The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之三, records the tree under 牡丹, with the root bark as its materia-medica part — "根似芍藥，肉白皮丹" (the root resembles the peony's, white flesh, red bark) — and notes that "惟山中單葉花紅者，根皮入藥爲佳" (only the red single-petaled mountain flower's root bark is good for medicine). **This page describes 牡丹皮 = the root bark, not the flower.**

## Botanical source and origin

*Paeonia suffruticosa* is a woody-stemmed shrub peony (Paeoniaceae), native to China and the ancestor of the garden tree-peony cultivars. The tree peony is famous for its flower (牡丹); its root bark (牡丹皮) is a distinct materia-medica part. Flora of China records the species (牡丹); the same botanical identity underlies both the flower entry and this root-bark entry.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Materia medica:** 牡丹皮 is a classical Chinese materia-medica part, recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之三, as the root bark (根皮) of 牡丹. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **Naming:** the *Bencao Gangmu* notes "肉白皮丹" — white flesh, red bark — which is exactly what gives 牡丹皮 its name (the red "丹" bark of the 牡丹).
- **Sourcing:** classical texts record that the red single-petaled mountain flower's root bark was preferred for medicine.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The materia-medica record is **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented facts are botanical identity, the Paeoniaceae placement, and the root-bark distinction from the flower.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 牡丹皮 is the dried root bark of *Paeonia suffruticosa*; expect a faint, rooty, earthy, woody scent.
- Distinguish it from the flower (牡丹, tree peony) — same tree, different part, different use.
- Specify the part (根皮/牡丹皮 vs 花/牡丹) when sourcing; the bark is a quiet, earthy material, not a fragrant flower.

## FAQ

**What is 牡丹皮 (tree peony bark)?** The dried root bark of *Paeonia suffruticosa*, the tree peony (Paeoniaceae) native to China. It has a faint, rooty, earthy, woody scent and is a classical Chinese materia-medica part — the root bark of the same tree whose flower is 牡丹.

**Is 牡丹皮 the same as 牡丹?** No — same tree, different parts. 牡丹皮 is the root bark; 牡丹 is the flower, the "king of flowers" (花王). The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之三, records the tree under 牡丹 with the root bark (根皮) as its materia-medica part.

**Why is it called 牡丹皮?** The *Bencao Gangmu* records the root as "肉白皮丹" — white flesh, red bark. The red (丹) bark of the 牡丹 gives 牡丹皮 its name. Classical texts also note that the red single-petaled mountain flower's root bark was preferred for medicine.

**What does tree peony bark smell like?** Quiet, rooty, earthy, and woody, with a faint, dry floral undertone — the muted aroma of a dried root bark. It is far less fragrant than the tree peony flower and reads as a subtle earthy-woody accent in a blend.

**Is tree peony bark an incense ingredient?** Not a lead one. Its classical standing is in materia medica, and its root-bark scent is faint. It could lend a muted earthy note, but its fame rests on the materia-medica record, not on fragrance.
