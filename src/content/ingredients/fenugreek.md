> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek (葫芦巴, húlúbā) is the small, hard seed of *Trigonella foenum-graecum*, a legume (Fabaceae) native to the Mediterranean and western Asia. Its roasted seed has a warm, sweet, maple-like, faintly nutty-bitter aroma. It entered Chinese materia medica as a late, "recent" aromatic rather than a classical 香材.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek's raw seed is mildly bitter, but roasting brings out a warm, sweet, maple-syrup note with a nutty, faintly celery-like edge — the compound sotolone is responsible for that characteristic maple character. It is a food-spice profile first; as a fragrant material it reads warm and gourmand rather than woodsy or resinous.

葫芦巴's raw seed is mildly bitter, but roasting brings out a warm, sweet, maple-syrup note with a nutty, faintly celery-like edge — the compound sotolone is responsible for that characteristic maple character. It is a food-spice profile first; as a fragrant material it reads warm and gourmand rather than woodsy or resinous.

The maple note is worth stating precisely: sotolone, the aroma compound behind it, is potent and distinctive, and it is what makes roasted fenugreek read as "maple syrup" rather than as a generic sweet spice. This is a modern-flavor register — the gourmand, caramel-maple warmth — and it explains why fenugreek sits oddly in any classical Chinese incense frame: the register it supplies was not a classical Chinese value.

## A provenance note (read this)

葫芦巴 is a **later addition** to the Chinese record, not a classical 香材:

- The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之四, records 葫芦巴 as a relatively recent materia-medica item: "今醫家治元臟虛冷為要藥，而唐已前方不見用，本草不著，蓋是近出" — it became an important medicine in later times, but does not appear in pre-Tang formulas, and is not in the older bencao, being a recent arrival.
- Its name points to a foreign origin: the text notes "種出海南諸番" — the seed came from overseas ("barbarian" regions).

This page records it honestly as a later aromatic and food-spice crossover — **no classical 香材 origin is claimed.**

This provenance note is unusual and valuable: the *Bencao Gangmu* itself performs the dating work, explicitly marking 葫芦巴 as a late arrival absent from older texts. Few materials carry such a precise, in-source record of their own lateness — and it is the cleanest possible evidence that fenugreek is a later crossover, not a classical 香材.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Trigonella foenum-graecum* is an annual legume native to the Mediterranean and western Asia, now cultivated widely across North Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and China. The seed is used as a spice and flavoring; Flora of China records the species as an introduced/cultivated plant.

*Trigonella foenum-graecum* is an annual legume native to the Mediterranean and western Asia, now cultivated widely across North Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and China. The seed is used as a spice and flavoring; Flora of China records the species as an introduced/cultivated plant — an annual herb with trifoliate leaves, small creamy-pale flowers, and long, slender pods carrying the brown, hard seeds. The name "foenum-graecum" means "Greek hay," a nod to the plant's hay-like scent when green — a detail that separates the fresh herb's character from the maple-sweet roasted seed.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek is harvested as seed and used whole or ground; the key processing step is roasting, which transforms the raw seed's bitterness into the warm, sweet, maple-like note. The raw seed is more bitter than aromatic; the sweet maple character develops on roasting.

The seed is harvested and dried, then used whole or ground. The decisive step is **roasting**: the raw seed is mildly bitter, and roasting develops the warm, sweet, maple-syrup note that defines the material — driving the sotolone character to the fore and mellowing the raw bitterness. The raw and roasted forms are thus genuinely different in character, not merely in texture. For any aromatic or flavor use, the roasted seed is the reference form, and the raw seed is the pre-processing state.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek quality turns on seed soundness and the maple note that develops on roasting. Good seed, when roasted, releases a clean, warm, sweet maple-syrup character without a stale or overly bitter edge; the raw seed's mild bitterness is normal and not a defect.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **soundness** (clean, dry, hard seed rather than broken or musty material) and **the roast character** (a clean, warm, sweet maple note when roasted, without a stale or harsh-bitter edge). The raw seed's mild bitterness is normal — it is the pre-roast state, and the maple sweetness is the developed character. Because the aroma is largely produced by roasting, quality is judged on the roasted result rather than the raw seed's faint scent.

## Fenugreek vs cumin vs coriander seed

> **Direct answer:** Fenugreek, cumin, and coriander seed are three different seed spices with distinct registers. Fenugreek is a legume (Fabaceae) with a sweet, maple-like roast note; cumin is a carrot-family seed with a warm, earthy, savory note; coriander seed is a carrot-family seed with a bright, citrusy note. Only the species separates them.
