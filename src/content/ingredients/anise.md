> **Direct answer:** Anise (茴芹, huíqín) is the seed-fruit of *Pimpinella anisum*, a herbaceous plant of the carrot family (Apiaceae) whose small dried fruits carry the sweet, licorice-like anethole note. It is a Mediterranean-West Asian spice, distinct from fennel (小茴香) and star anise (八角/大茴香), and its Chinese name is a modern botanical term rather than a classical incense ingredient.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Anise is sweet and licorice-like, warm and spicy, driven almost entirely by the aromatic compound anethole. It is the purest, most concentrated "sweet-licorice" note of the 茴 group — cleaner and sweeter than fennel's greener cast, and single-fruited rather than the star-shaped 八角.

Anise is sweet and licorice-like, warm and spicy, driven almost entirely by the aromatic *anethole* it shares with fennel and star anise. It is the purest, most concentrated "sweet-licorice" note of the three — cleaner and sweeter than fennel's greener herbaceous cast, and single-fruited rather than the star-shaped 八角. In aromatics it reads as a *warm, candied sweetness*, a classic note in Western liqueurs, pastries, and incense-adjacent fragrance.

The register is worth pinning down against its two relatives. Anise's sweetness is the "purest" of the three because the fruit is small, dense, and almost entirely anethole-driven; fennel carries a greener, more vegetal edge from its other constituents, and star anise — despite sharing the note — adds a warmer, more resinous body. This is why anise, not fennel or star anise, became the reference for the clean "licorice" note in Western flavor and fragrance.

- **小茴香 (fennel)** — *Foeniculum vulgare* (Apiaceae), a different carrot-family plant, the 茴香 of classical Chinese cooking and 合香 formulas. Its own entry.
- **八角 / 大茴香 (star anise)** — *Illicium verum* (Schisandraceae), the star-shaped fruit of an evergreen tree. Its own entry.

**This page describes 茴芹 = anise, *Pimpinella anisum*.** When a classical Chinese text or formula says 茴香, it means fennel (or star anise), not this Western anise; the anethole sweetness is the shared thread.

This three-way convergence is the single most useful fact about anise for anyone working with 茴-named materials: the shared name element 茴 records a *shared smell*, not a shared plant. A buyer, formulator, or reader who treats 茴芹, 小茴香, and 大茴香 as interchangeable has collapsed three distinct botanical species into one aroma, and the classical incense record — which uses 茴香 for fennel — does not map onto this Western seed at all.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Pimpinella anisum* is an annual herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae), native to the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia, cultivated since antiquity. The part used is the small, ridged, seed-like fruit, whose anethole content gives the characteristic sweet licorice scent. It reached Chinese usage as a modern botanical term, not as a classical incense material.

*Pimpinella anisum* is an annual herb of the carrot family, native to the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia and cultivated since antiquity across the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and India. The part used is the small, ridged, seed-like fruit, whose anethole content gives the characteristic sweet licorice scent. Flora of China records the species as a cultivated introduction — an annual 10–50 cm tall, strongly aromatic, with small white flowers — consistent with its status as a crop plant brought into Chinese usage under the modern botanical name 茴芹 rather than through any classical incense record.

The name itself is a modern coinage: 茴芹 was built to give the Western spice a Chinese botanical label, distinct from the older 茴香 (fennel) that already occupied the 茴 slot in the classical vocabulary. This is the key provenance point — anise did not enter China as a 香材 with a history; it entered as a modern botanical and culinary name attached to an imported seed.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 茴芹 is a modern botanical coinage — 茴 ("the anethole-scented group") + 芹 ("celery/carrot-family herb") — not a classical term. It belongs to the modern Chinese vocabulary for imported aromatics, a different naming layer from the classical 茴香 (fennel) and 大茴香 (star anise).

The name 茴芹 (huíqín) is transparent and modern: 茴 marks the anethole-scented group, and 芹 marks the carrot-family (celery-like) herb. It is a modern botanical term, not a classical 香材 name. This matters for disambiguation because Chinese has a separate, genuinely classical vocabulary for the same aromatic register — 茴香 (fennel) and 大茴香 (star anise) — and 茴芹 sits *outside* that canon as a modern label for the Western seed. A buyer who hears "茴" should not assume a classical 香材; the specific name must be pinned to the specific species, and for anise that species is *Pimpinella anisum*.

## Why is it used in fragrance?

> **Direct answer:** Anise is used as a sweet, warm, licorice-like accent and top note, not a classical Chinese incense material. Its anethole sweetness brightens and rounds sweeter blends, and it enters Chinese fragrance only as a modern crossover alongside other sweet aromatics — its role in the classical 合香 system belongs to fennel, not to this seed.

Anise's role in fragrance is the role of a *sweet spice accent*: its concentrated anethole note brightens and rounds sweeter compositions, lending a clean, candied licorice warmth. In Western perfumery and flavoring this is a long-established use — anise scents liqueurs (anisette, ouzo, raki), pastries, and confectionery, and its note is a recognizable top-and-middle sweet accent. Its absence from the classical Chinese 合香 canon is the key point: in the historical Chinese system, the licorice-sweet register was served by fennel and star anise, not by this Western seed, which arrived only as a modern import.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Anise is produced by harvesting the ripe fruit and drying it whole; the dried seed-fruit is the traded form, used whole or ground. Drying concentrates the anethole sweetness, and no extraction is required for the spice itself — anise oil, when produced, is steam-distilled from the dried fruit.

The ripe fruits are harvested and dried, producing the small, ridged, gray-green seed-fruit that is the traded form. Drying concentrates the anethole sweetness, and the material is used whole or ground like any seed spice. For essential-oil use, anethole is obtained by steam distillation of the dried fruit, but the spice itself needs no processing beyond drying. Because the aromatic is concentrated in a small, hard fruit, the whole seed keeps its scent far longer than pre-ground powder — a practical point for anyone dosing it into a blend.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Anise quality turns on anethole content and freshness. A good anise is clean, dry, and strongly licorice-sweet on crushing, without a musty or woody off-note. Because the whole fruit retains its aroma longer than ground powder, whole seed is the reference form for checking quality.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **anethole content** (a strong, clean licorice sweetness on crushing, not a faint or woody note), **freshness** (dry, aromatic fruit rather than stale, musty seed), and **form** (whole fruit retains its scent far longer than ground powder). The seed's small size and hard coat are natural protectors of the volatile anethole, which is why whole anise keeps better than most ground spices — and why the aroma-on-crushing test is the standard quality check.

## Anise vs fennel vs star anise

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Anise is a "chemistry-family, not botany-family" case — the 茴 name cluster groups three unrelated plants by a single shared molecule, anethole. This makes anise the clearest lesson in the whole 茴 group: a shared name element records a shared smell, and assuming it records a shared plant is the exact error the classical 茴香 record invites.

From a sourcing and editorial perspective, anise is most interesting for what the 茴 cluster reveals about how Chinese naming works — and where it fails. Three points follow:

- **The 茴 group is a chemistry family, not a botanical family.** Anise (Apiaceae), fennel (Apiaceae), and star anise (Schisandraceae) are not closely related, yet they share one name element because they share one molecule, anethole. Chinese aromatic naming here is *olfactory*, grouping by smell rather than by plant. This is a genuinely useful editorial fact, because it explains why a single 茴 in a name cannot be trusted to identify a species — and why disambiguation is the first job, not the last.
- **"Purest" is a formulation statement, not a ranking.** Anise carries the cleanest, most concentrated licorice note of the three, but that does not make it "better" — fennel's greener cast and star anise's resinous warmth are distinct registers for distinct jobs. For a formulator, the choice among the three is a *register choice*, and collapsing them into "licorice" discards exactly the information that matters.
- **Modern-term provenance is itself the sourcing signal.** 茴芹 is a modern botanical coinage, not a classical 香材 — which means its presence in a modern Chinese fragrance is an *import*, not a continuation of a Chinese tradition. For a buyer, the practical reading is that "anise" in a Chinese-sourced material spec should be pinned to *Pimpinella anisum* specifically, because the classical 茴香 word points elsewhere.

In our view, anise is best understood as the **purest anethole seed in a chemistry-family of three unrelated plants** — a Western spice whose Chinese name is modern, whose licorice note is the shared thread binding it to fennel and star anise, and whose sourcing conversation turns on register and species rather than on the shared 茴 name.
