> **Direct answer:** Linglingxiang (零陵香, línglíngxiāng) is a fragrant herb identified today as *Lysimachia foenum-graecum*, a moisture-loving plant of southern China whose dried herb smells sweet and hay-like. It is a classical Chinese incense herb, though its historical identity is tangled: the same name (and its alias 熏草) has been applied across several aromatic herbs over the centuries, a drift the Song *Xiangpu* itself acknowledges.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Lysimachia foenum-graecum* is a perennial herb of the primrose family (Primulaceae). Flora of China describes a plant 20–60 cm tall, ascending from a creeping base, with broadly ovate to elliptic leaves 4–11 × 2–6 cm and yellow flowers, flowering in May in wet mountain forests of southern China.

Flora of China describes *Lysimachia foenum-graecum* precisely: a **perennial herb 20–60 cm tall**, **curry-scented when dry**, with stems ascending to erect from a creeping base; leaves alternate, broadly ovate to elliptic, **4–11 × 2–6 cm**, obscurely undulate at the margin; flowers **solitary and axillary**, the corolla **yellow**, 1.2–1.7 cm; capsule subglobose, 6–7 mm; flowering in **May**. It grows in wet mixed forests and stream valleys on humus-rich soils at **800–1,700 m**, in northern Guangdong, Guangxi, southwest Hunan, and southeast Yunnan. See [Lysimachia foenum-graecum in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200017018) and [Lysimachia foenum-graecum on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/4005054).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 零陵香 is geographical — it names the herb after 零陵 (Lingling), the region of southern China where it grew — but it is really a name that has drifted across plants. The classical aliases 熏草, 蕙草, 燕草, and 黄零草 were applied to several aromatic herbs over the centuries, and the Song *Xiangpu* records the confusion directly.

The name 零陵香 is geographical: it names the herb after 零陵 (Lingling), the valley of southern China where the classical sources located it. The earliest references describe a plant called 熏草 ("fumigating herb"), said to have hemp-like leaves and a square stem, growing in the 零陵 valley — hence 零陵香. Over the centuries the same name, and its aliases (熏草, 蕙草, 燕草, 黄零草), were applied to several different aromatic herbs:

- **零陵香 (modern identification):** *Lysimachia foenum-graecum*, the sweet-hay herb, a Primulaceae plant of wet southern-China habitats.
- **佩兰 / 都梁香 (historical conflation):** *Eupatorium fortunei* (and related allies), a different aromatic herb that some classical sources treated as the same thing.

## A name with a tangled history (香谱考据)

> **Direct answer:** 零陵香 is the classic example of a name that has drifted across species. The Song *Xiangpu* (香譜) notes the confusion outright — "古今采用，自殊其类，各别" ("what was used then and now differ in kind") — and the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records both the geographical origin and the practice of calling only the "广零陵香" (the Lingnan kind) the true 熏草.

The classical record is unusually explicit that this is a drifting name. The Song-dynasty *Xiangpu* (香譜) by Hong Chu (洪芻) records the herb under 零陵香, citing:

- **《南越志》** — "一名燕草，又名薰草，生零陵山谷，葉如羅勒" (also called yancao and xuncao, growing in the Lingling valley, leaves like basil).
- **《山海經》** — "薰草似麻葉，方莖，氣如蘼蕪，可以止癘" (xuncao has hemp-like leaves and a square stem, its scent like meiwu, and can stop pestilence). "即零陵香。"

The *Xiangpu* also records the aroma-medicinal formula language: "味苦無毒，主惡氣、注心腹痛，下氣、令體香，和諸香或作湯丸用，得酒良" — bitter, non-toxic, dispelling bad qi, and used to make the body fragrant and to blend with other aromatics. And it flags the identity problem directly: **"古今采用，自殊其类，各别"** — what was used in ancient and modern times differ in kind.

The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) goes further, recording the geography and the naming practice: the herb grows "生零陵山谷，今湖岭诸州皆有之，多生下湿地，常以七月中旬开花至香"; the classical names "熏草、蕙草、香草、燕草、黄零草皆别名也"; and the key sourcing note that **only the "广零陵香" (the Lingnan kind) is the true 熏草** — "今人呼为广零陵香者，乃真熏草也" — while other regions' material is lesser ("不及岭南者芬熏耳").

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** 零陵香 is a classical sweet-herb ingredient in 合香 (blended incense), contributing a soft, hay-sweet note that blends smoothly with woods and resins. The classical record makes its role explicit — "和諸香" (blends with other aromatics) — and it appears alongside 甘松, 藿香, and 檀香 in standard formulas.

零陵香's role in incense is as a *sweet-herb blender*: a soft, hay-sweet, coumarin-like note that blends smoothly with woods and resins rather than leading with a sharp or heavy character. The *Xiangpu* states the function directly — "和諸香" (blends with the various aromatics) — and the herb appears in classical 合香 formulas alongside 甘松 (spikenard), 藿香 (agastache), 檀香 (sandalwood), and 白芷 (angelica). It was also used for scenting clothes and as a fragrant herb in its own right, and the *Xiangcheng* records that "古者烧香草以降神" — ancients burned fragrant herbs to summon the spirits, hence the name 熏 ("fumigate") and 蕙 ("harmonious").

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 零陵香 is used as the dried herb, and the *Xiangcheng* records the classical processing: the Lingnan material was fire-dried to a yellow color ("以火炭焙干令黄色乃佳"). The sweet-hay note is volatile, so drying temperature and freshness are the practical concerns.

The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records a specific classical processing standard: the Lingnan material was **fire-dried over charcoal to a yellow color** — "以火炭焙干令黄色乃佳" — a drying step that both preserved the herb and standardized its color. The sweet-hay, coumarin-like note is volatile, so drying temperature and freshness govern how much of the character survives; a faded or poorly dried batch reads flat. As with other leafy herbs, the whole dried herb is the traditional material of use, and processing is a drying question rather than a resin-tapping or distillation one.

## What determines linglingxiang quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on aroma — a sweet, hay-like, coumarin note from a clean, well-dried herb — plus species clarity. Because the name has drifted across plants, the single most important quality check is confirming which species the material actually is, since 零陵香 and its historical aliases have named more than one herb.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Identity** — *Lysimachia foenum-graecum*, not the historically conflated *Eupatorium fortunei* (佩兰) or another alias-carrier.
2. **Aroma** — sweet, hay-like, with a coumarin note; a fresh, sweet-green scent beats a faded or musty one.
3. **Condition** — clean, well-dried herb; the *Xiangcheng* records the classical yellow-dried standard ("以火炭焙干令黄色乃佳").
4. **Freshness** — the volatile sweet note fades with age and poor storage.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder; identity and aroma are the practical signals.
