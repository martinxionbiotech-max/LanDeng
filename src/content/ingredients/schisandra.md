> **Direct answer:** Schisandra (五味子, wǔwèizǐ) is the dried berry of *Schisandra chinensis*, a woody climbing vine of the family Schisandraceae native to China and the Russian Far East. Its name means "five-flavor berry," from the berry's combined sour, sweet, salty, bitter, and pungent tastes. It is a classical Chinese materia-medica fruit with a faint, fruity, sour-berry scent, not a lead incense 香材.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 五味子 means "five-flavor seed/berry" — 五味 (five tastes) + 子 (fruit/seed) — a taste-name, not a scent-name. This is the key to the material: it is named for what it tastes like, not what it smells like, which is precisely why it is a weak incense material despite its fame.

The name 五味子 (wǔwèizǐ) is a taste-name: 五味 "five flavors" + 子 "fruit/seed." This matters more than it first appears. Most incense materials are named for a color, a fragrance, or a place; 五味子 is named for a *taste* — the five flavors (sour, sweet, salty, bitter, pungent) said to meet in one berry. That naming is the whole story of the material's odd position: it is famous in materia medica precisely for a property (taste) that has almost nothing to do with incense, where the relevant sense is smell. The classical aliases — 荎蕏, 玄及, and 會及 — reinforce that it belongs to the materia-medica register, not the fragrance register.

## The 五味 (five-taste) naming — a taste-name, not a scent-name (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) 草之七 explains the name: the berry's skin and flesh are sweet and sour, its seed is pungent and bitter, and the whole carries a salty note — "皮、肉甘、酸，核中辛、苦，都有鹹味" — so all five tastes meet in one fruit. This is a taste-based definition, and it is the reason the berry is a famous medicine but a faint incense note.

The 本草考据 of 五味子 centers on a single, well-documented naming explanation. The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之七, records it through Su Gong (苏恭):

> 五味，皮、肉甘、酸，核中辛、苦，都有鹹味，此則五味具也。

That is: the skin and flesh are sweet and sour, the seed is pungent and bitter, and the whole carries a salty note — so the five tastes are complete in one fruit. The text adds that the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (《本經》) "但云味酸" — recorded only the sour taste — "當以木為五行之先也" (taking wood as the first of the five phases). The entry opens by classifying 五味子 as a top-grade (上品) drug of the *Bencao Jing*.

The point for an incense reader is the *category* this naming puts the material in: a taste-defined materia-medica fruit. A berry famous for five *tastes* is famous for a sense incense does not primarily engage. That is why 五味子 sits at the edge of the incense canon — present as a quiet fruity accent at most, never a driving 香材 — despite its large presence in materia medica.

## The north–south grading (本草考据, continued)

> **Direct answer:** Li Shizhen records a north–south division: southern berries ripen red (南產者，色紅), northern berries ripen black (北產者，色黑), and the northern ones were used for tonic preparations (入滋補藥必用北產者，乃良). This is a sourcing distinction — two color forms of the same species — not a different plant.

The *Bencao Gangmu* also preserves a sourcing distinction that reads as a grading system:

- **Li Shizhen (时珍):** 五味，今有南北之分，南產者，色紅；北產者，色黑，入滋補藥必用北產者，乃良 — "there is now a north–south division: the southern ones are red, the northern ones are black; for tonic preparations one must use the northern ones, which are good."
- **Tao Hongjing (弘景), on provenance:** 今第一出高麗，多肉而酸甜；次出青州、冀州，味過酸 — the best came from Goryeo (Korea), fleshy and sweet-sour; next from Qingzhou and Jizhou, too sour.
- **Su Song (颂), on the plant:** 今河東、陝西州郡尤多，杭越間亦有之…七月成實，叢生莖端，如豌豆許大，生青熟紅紫，入藥生曝不去子 — abundant in Hedong and Shaanxi, also around Hangzhou and Yue; fruits in the seventh month, green when raw, red-purple when ripe, used raw and sun-dried with the seeds left in.
- **Wang Ji (机), on use:** 五味治喘嗽，須分南北…宜用北者…風寒在肺，宜用南者 — the north and south forms were assigned to different uses.

These are the documentary layers: the origin record (《別錄》: 生齊山山谷及代郡，八月採實，陰乾 — grows in the Qishan valleys and Daijun, fruit harvested in the eighth month and dried in shade), the provenance ranking (Korea first), and the north–south color grading. **This page describes 五味子 = *Schisandra chinensis*.** The north–south division is a sourcing distinction within the one species, not a claim this page extends into any medical statement.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 五味子 is used as the dried ripe berry, harvested in late summer and dried whole with the seeds left in. The classical record specifies sun-drying (陰乾/曝), and — for the materia-medica tradition — splitting the berry with a copper knife and processing it; for incense and aromatic use, the dried whole berry is the material.

The classical record describes the handling of 五味子 with unusual specificity:

- **Harvest and drying** — the fruit is harvested in the eighth month (八月採實) and dried in shade (陰乾); Su Song records it used raw and sun-dried with the seeds left in (入藥生曝不去子).
- **Materia-medica processing** — Lei Xiao (雷斅) specifies splitting the berry with a copper knife, steaming it steeped in honey, then soaking it in rice-water overnight before drying (以銅刀劈作兩片，用蜜浸蒸…以漿浸一宿，焙乾用); Li Shizhen adds that it was cooked for tonic use and raw for cough use.

For aromatic and incense use, the dried whole berry is the material — the processing steps above belong to the materia-medica tradition, which this page notes but does not translate into any aromatic or medical claim.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** 五味子's commercial lesson is the taste-versus-scent boundary: it is a famous materia-medica berry named for five tastes, which is exactly why it is a weak incense material. A buyer should value it by its medicinal provenance, not as a fragrance — and never let the medicinal fame bleed into an incense or health claim.

From a manufacturing perspective, 五味子 is a clean illustration of **why fame and fragrance are different assets**:

- **A taste-name is not a fragrance.** The berry is named for five tastes and is famous in materia medica for them. But incense engages smell, not taste, and the berry's scent is quiet. A buyer who imports the berry's medicinal renown into an incense formula is paying for a property the format cannot use.

In our view, 五味子 is a materia-medica berry whose incense value is marginal and quiet: buy it by species if a subtle sour-berry accent is genuinely wanted, but do not let its five-taste fame be mistaken for fragrance value — and keep the medicine and the incense strictly separate.
