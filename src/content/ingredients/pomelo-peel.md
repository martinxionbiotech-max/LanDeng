> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel (柚皮, yòupí) is the peel of the pomelo (*Citrus maxima*), the largest citrus fruit, a tree of the rue family (Rutaceae) native to Southeast Asia and long cultivated in southern China. It is a bittersweet, floral-woody citrus peel used in Chinese incense as a citrus peel note and in cooking and tea. It is distinct from the aged mandarin peel 陈皮 (*Citrus reticulata*).

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel is citrus and bittersweet, with a floral, faintly woody depth and less of the sharp acidity of orange or mandarin. Its thick white pith gives it a mild bitterness that balances the bright citrus oil of the outer rind — a soft, rounded citrus note closer to dried citrus than to fresh zest.

Pomelo peel is citrus and bittersweet, with a floral, faintly woody depth and less of the sharp acidity of orange or mandarin. Its thick white pith gives it a mild bitterness that balances the bright citrus oil of the outer rind. In a blend it reads as a *soft citrus note* — the brightness of citrus rounded by a bitter-floral, slightly woody undertone, closer to dried citrus than to fresh zest. This is the defining contrast with 陈皮: where aged mandarin peel trades its brightness for a *mellow, tea-like* depth, pomelo peel keeps a firmer, more bittersweet edge from its pith.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus maxima* is the pomelo (also called shaddock or 柚子), the largest citrus fruit — a tree of the rue family (Rutaceae) with broadly ovate leaves 9–16 × 4–8 cm and a pale-yellow, globose-to-pyriform fruit usually more than 10 cm in diameter. It is native to Southeast Asia and long cultivated across southern China; the part used is the peel.

*Citrus maxima* is the pomelo, the largest of the citrus fruits, a tree of the rue family (Rutaceae) native to Southeast Asia and long cultivated across southern China. Flora of China records it as a tree with **broadly ovate or elliptic leaves 9–16 × 4–8 cm**, a winged petiole, and a **pale-yellow or yellowish-green fruit, globose to pyriform, usually more than 10 cm in diameter**, with **large, prominent oil dots** in the rind and **25–35 stamens** in the flower. The fruit's size is what matters for the peel: the pomelo's thick rind-and-pith is proportionally far heavier than a mandarin's, which is exactly what gives 柚皮 its characteristic bitterness. The part used is the fruit peel — the outer rind and pith — used fresh or dried. The rue family also includes mandarin/tangerine (*Citrus reticulata*, the source of 陈皮), orange, and lemon, all sharing the family's citrus-oil chemistry. See [Citrus maxima in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=242412666).

> **A naming caution:** 柚皮 (*Citrus maxima* pomelo peel) and 陈皮 (*Citrus reticulata* aged mandarin peel) are **different peels from different citrus species**. 陈皮 is the aged mandarin peel prized from Xinhui, Guangdong; 柚皮 is the pomelo peel. Both are citrus peels with their own entries, and they are not interchangeable.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 (yòupí) means "pomelo peel" — 柚 "pomelo" + 皮 "peel" — and it names the fruit by species and the part by function. The alternative 柚子皮 ("pomelo fruit peel") is a looser everyday form. The name's precision matters because it separates the pomelo's peel from the aged mandarin peel 陈皮.

The Chinese term is straightforward but worth pinning down: 柚 (yòu) is the pomelo (also called shaddock), and 皮 (pí) is the peel or skin, so 柚皮 names **the pomelo's peel specifically**. The everyday alternative 柚子皮 ("pomelo fruit peel") means the same thing. This matters in sourcing because the citrus family is crowded in Chinese fragrance, and 柚皮 must be kept apart from 陈皮 (the *aged mandarin* peel, a different species) and from the fresh-fruit context of other citrus. The species-and-part precision of 柚皮 is what prevents those conflations.

## The citrus-peel family (read this)

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 sits in a small Chinese family of citrus-peel materials alongside 陈皮 (aged mandarin peel) and the fruit-peel context of 荔枝壳. They share a citrus-peel register but differ by species, pith, and processing — the pomelo's thick pith, the mandarin's aging, and the litchi's sweetness.

The citrus-peel materials form a small but real family, and the distinctions are the point:

- **柚皮 (pomelo peel)** — *Citrus maxima*, bittersweet-floral with a thick white pith; this entry.
- **陈皮 (tangerine/mandarin peel)** — *Citrus reticulata*, the *aged* peel with a warm, mellow, tea-like depth.
- **荔枝壳 (litchi husk)** — *Litchi chinensis*, a soapberry-family pericarp, sweet and floral — a fruit-peel note outside the citrus family proper.

The family shares a citrus-peel register, but each member is a **different species with a different material logic**: the pomelo is defined by its thick pith, the mandarin by its aging, and the litchi by its delicate sweetness. ## Why is pomelo peel used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel is used for its soft, rounded citrus note — the brightness of citrus tempered by a bitter-floral, slightly woody undertone from the pith. It is a blending accent that adds citrus depth without the sharp acidity of fresh zest, and it is usually mixed rather than burned alone.

Pomelo peel's incense role is a *soft citrus accent*. Unlike a sharp, bright citrus top note (lemon or fresh zest), 柚皮 reads as a **rounded, bittersweet citrus** — the pith's mild bitterness balancing the rind's bright oil. This makes it useful in warm spice-and-wood blends, where it adds a citrus depth that does not clash the way a sharp acid note would. It is almost always **blended**, not burned alone, and its food-adjacent character means it sits comfortably alongside cooking-and-tea use — a peel that moves between the kitchen and the censer.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel is used fresh or, more commonly, dried — the outer rind and thick pith together. Processing is drying and (optionally) cutting or grinding, with no distillation or extraction; the pith's thickness is the variable that sets its bitterness and must be handled as part of the material.

The processing of 柚皮 is simple and drying-based:

1. **Peeling** — the outer rind and the thick white pith are separated from the fruit.
2. **Drying** — the peel is dried, used either fresh or dried.
3. **Cutting / grinding** — for blending, the dried peel is cut or ground.

There is no distillation or extraction — pomelo peel is the whole peel (rind plus pith). The practical point is that the **pith** is an integral part of the material, not a defect to be trimmed away: its thickness is what gives 柚皮 its characteristic mild bitterness. A buyer should know whether the material includes full pith, since a thinly-peeled lot will read brighter and less bitter.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness, cleanliness, and the intact pith — a clean, fragrant peel with a bittersweet citrus scent and an intact thick pith. The pith's presence is part of the material's identity, so a buyer checks for it rather than against it.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Freshness** — a clean, fragrant peel with a bittersweet citrus scent when warmed.
2. **Pith intactness** — the thick white pith should be present; it is part of the material's character, not a defect.
3. **Cleanliness** — freedom from mold, discoloration, and foreign material.
4. **Aroma balance** — a rounded, bittersweet citrus rather than a flat or musty note.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for 柚皮; freshness, pith intactness, and the bittersweet-citrus balance are the practical signals.

## Pomelo peel vs chenpi

> **Direct answer:** 柚皮 (pomelo peel) and 陈皮 (chenpi) are different peels from different citrus species. 柚皮 is the pomelo peel (*Citrus maxima*) with a thick pith and a bittersweet, floral-woody character; 陈皮 is the *aged* mandarin peel (*Citrus reticulata*) with a warm, mellow, tea-like depth from aging. Both are citrus peels, but they are not interchangeable.

| | Pomelo peel (柚皮) | Chenpi (陈皮) |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Citrus maxima* | *Citrus reticulata* (aged) |
| Character | Bittersweet, floral-woody, thick pith | Warm, mellow, tea-like, bittersweet-citrus |
| Defining variable | Pith thickness | Age |

The two are the same family's peels with different defining variables: the pomelo is defined by its **pith**, the mandarin by its **aging**. ## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Pomelo peel's lesson is that "citrus peel" is not one material but a spectrum defined by species, pith thickness, and aging. The pomelo's thick pith is the material's identity, not a defect — and a buyer who orders "citrus peel" without naming the species and the pith is ordering a category, not a material.

From a manufacturing and sourcing perspective, pomelo peel sharpens a lesson that runs through the whole citrus-peel family: **"citrus peel" is a category, not a specification.**

Three points follow:

- **Pith is identity, not waste.** Where a perfumer might strip the pith for a pure rind oil, 柚皮 keeps the thick white pith precisely because it is the source of the material's bittersweet-floral character. A buyer who treats the pith as a defect to be trimmed is discarding the very thing that makes pomelo peel pomelo peel rather than generic citrus.
- **Species and processing carry the value.** The pomelo's thick pith and the mandarin's aging are two different answers to the same question — how to turn a common citrus peel into a distinct 香材. 陈皮 and 柚皮 are both "citrus peel," but they are not interchangeable, and the name alone does not say which material or which variable is in play.
- **It is a soft accent, not a lead.** Pomelo peel's rounded citrus works as a blender in warm spice-and-wood formulas, and the right dose **depends on formulation**. A buyer specifies it as a supporting note, with the pith's bitterness as a deliberate balancing element.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **correctly identified, freshly dried pomelo peel with intact pith** bought as a soft, rounded citrus accent — where the species and the pith are named explicitly, rather than collapsed into a vague "citrus peel."
