## What amber is (and is not)

> **Direct answer:** Amber is fossilized tree resin — a geological material formed over millions of years from ancient tree resin that hardened and polymerized underground. It is not a living botanical, not a mineral in the strict sense, and not the perfumery "amber" accord. This page classifies it as "fossil resin, not botanical" and states that plainly.

琥珀 must be understood for what it is: **fossil resin, not a botanical material.** It does not belong to the living plant kingdom in the way that frankincense or pine resin do. The classical sources understood its origin as a transformation:

- The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records that "pine resin, given a thousand years, transforms into amber" (千年松脂化為琥珀), and that the resin of the sweetgum "enters the earth and in a thousand years becomes amber" — a folk explanation of fossilization.
- The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records amber among aromatics and ornaments — its static-electric attraction ("amber attracts mustard seeds," 琥珀拾芥) is noted as a classical observation of its property.

**This page describes 琥珀 = amber = fossil resin.** It is not a botanical, and we state that plainly rather than filing it under a living plant.

## Amber among the non-botanical resins

> **Direct answer:** Amber sits in a small group of incense materials that are not living-plant products: fossil resin (琥珀), insect lac resin (紫草茸 / shellac), and animal ambergris (龙涎香). They share a resin-like character but come from geology, insects, and animals respectively — a classification worth keeping explicit.

Amber is best understood as part of a small, distinctive group of incense materials that are **not living botanicals**:

- **琥珀 (amber)** — fossil resin, geological in origin. This entry.
- **紫草茸 (shellac)** — lac resin, a secretion of the lac insect (*Kerria lacca*), not a plant. See [shellac](/ingredients/shellac/).
- **龙涎香 (ambergris)** — an animal secretion from the sperm whale, not a plant. See [ambergris](/ingredients/ambergris/).

These three share a warm, resinous, sweet register in incense, but their origins — geological, insect, and animal — set them apart from the living plant resins like pine resin and frankincense. Keeping the classification explicit matters for both accuracy and sourcing.

## How is it processed?

It is not ground into a bulk incense powder the way a living resin would be; its value is as a gemstone and a historical aromatic.

## What determines amber quality?

> **Direct answer:** Amber quality is a gemstone matter — clarity, color, and freedom from fracture — rather than an incense-grade matter. The commercial question is also one of identity: true amber (fossil resin) must be distinguished from the perfumery "amber" accord and from imitation or younger resins.

The quality points are gemmological rather than incense-related:

1. **Clarity and color** — clear, well-colored amber beats cloudy or fractured material.
2. **Identity** — true amber (fossil resin) vs the perfumery "amber" accord (labdanum, benzoin, vanilla) vs imitation or younger resins.
3. **Age and polymerization** — genuine amber is fully fossilized and hardened; younger or treated resins are not the same material.

Amber carries no incense grading ladder — it is a gemstone and fossil, so quality is a gemmological and identity question rather than an aromatic-grade question.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Amber's commercial lesson is classification honesty: it is fossil resin, not a living botanical, and the word "amber" in fragrance usually means a labdanum-benzoin-vanilla accord, not the fossil. A buyer's first question is therefore "which amber?" — the geological material or the perfumery character — because the two are entirely different products.

From a manufacturing perspective, amber is a material where **the name hides two different products**. Two points follow:

- **"Amber" in fragrance is usually not amber.** The warm, sweet, resinous "amber" note in perfumery and incense is overwhelmingly a *accord* built from labdanum, benzoin, and vanilla — not fossil resin. A formulator asking for "amber" almost always wants the accord; a collector asking for "amber" wants the gemstone. The word must be resolved to which is meant.
- **Classification honesty is a sourcing discipline.** Amber is fossil resin — geological, not botanical — and it belongs with the other non-botanical resins (shellac, ambergris) rather than with living plant resins like pine resin or frankincense. Stating that plainly is more useful to a buyer than filing amber under a living plant, because it changes how the material is sourced, priced, and regulated.

In our view, amber is a historical aromatic and a gemstone whose chief commercial lesson is disambiguation: ask "which amber — fossil or accord?" before anything else, and keep its geological origin explicit rather than blurring it into the living-plant resins.
