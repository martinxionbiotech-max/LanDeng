> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia (香薷, xiāngrú) is a leafy aromatic herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae), chiefly *Elsholtzia ciliata*. It is a classical Chinese aromatic-medicinal herb, recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu* under its aromatic names 香柔 and 香茸, and listed among the aromatics of the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘). Its character is a fresh, minty-spicy green note that sits between mint and perilla.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is minty and herbal, with a spicy, faintly sweet, camphor-adjacent lift — a fresh green note close to mint and perilla but with its own softer, warmer cast. In incense it reads as a clean green aromatic that freshens a blend rather than leading it.

香薷 is minty and herbal, with a spicy, faintly sweet, camphor-adjacent lift — a fresh green note close to mint and perilla but with its own softer, warmer cast. It is not as sharply cooling as mint, nor as anise-leaning as perilla. In incense it reads as a *clean green aromatic* that freshens a blend, a bright herbal top or mid note in the mint-family register. ## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Elsholtzia ciliata* is an annual mint-family herb, and Flora of China describes an erect plant 30–50 cm tall whose purplish flowers open from July to October. The genus *Elsholtzia* groups several East Asian aromatic herbs, of which *E. ciliata* is the most widely encountered; related species such as *E. splendens* are also gathered.

Flora of China describes *Elsholtzia ciliata* precisely: **erect herbs 30–50 cm tall**, with glabrous or pilose stems that turn purple-brown with age; leaf blades **ovate to elliptic-lanceolate, 3–9 × 1–4 cm**, with a serrate margin and an acuminate apex; **purplish corollas** about 4.5 mm long in dense spikes 2–7 cm; and yellow-brown nutlets. It flowers **July–October** and fruits October–January. The species is the type of a genus that carries several East Asian aromatic herbs, of which *E. ciliata* and the related *E. splendens* are both gathered for their volatile oils. See [Elsholtzia ciliata in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200019619) and [Elsholtzia ciliata on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/2927075).

The *Bencao Gangmu* explains that 薷 is originally 葇, "its smell is fragrant and its leaves soft, hence the name" (其氣香，其葉柔，故以名之), and records the aliases 香柔, 香茸, and 蜜蜂草. The name is an aroma: the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), in its fragrant-herb section (草之三), records 香薷 with the alternative names 香柔, 香茸, and 蜜蜂草, and explains:

> "時珍曰：薷，本作葇。……其氣香，其葉柔，故以名之。" — the character 薷 is originally 葇; its smell is fragrant and its leaves soft, hence the name.

The herb was moved by Li Shizhen from the vegetable section into the fragrant-herb (芳草) section, a correction that itself signals its standing as an aromatic rather than a mere potherb. The Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) also lists 香薷 among aromatics, confirming its place in the classical Chinese fragrance vocabulary.

## The Lamiaceae green-aromatic triangle

> **Direct answer:** 香薷 (elsholtzia), 藿香 (agastache), and 紫苏 (perilla) are three mint-family herbs whose green notes overlap closely enough that a buyer must distinguish them by species. Elsholtzia is minty-spicy and slightly sweet, agastache is minty-anise, and perilla is shiso-like and anise-leaning — three genera, one aromatic family.

The mint family (Lamiaceae) contributes a cluster of green aromatics to Chinese incense, and three of them — 香薷, 藿香, and 紫苏 — sit close enough in profile that their names are worth pinning to species:

- **香薷 (elsholtzia)** — *Elsholtzia ciliata*; minty-spicy, slightly sweet, camphor-adjacent. This entry.
- **藿香 (agastache)** — *Agastache rugosa*; minty-anise, with its own same-name tangles (see [agastache-rugosa](/ingredients/agastache-rugosa/)).
- **紫苏 (perilla)** — *Perilla frutescens*; anise-leaning with a shiso character (see [perilla](/ingredients/perilla/)).

The family relationship is the trap: three different genera share the "minty green herb" register, so a label that says only "aromatic herb" does not identify the material. This is a species question, not a stylistic detail.

## Where does elsholtzia come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Elsholtzia ciliata* is broadly distributed across East and Southeast Asia. Flora of China records it in every Chinese province except Qinghai and Xinjiang, from sea level to 3,400 m, on hills, waste ground, sunny terraces, riverbanks, and forest edges — and notes it has been introduced to Europe and North America.

The species has a wide native and adventive range. Flora of China records it in **all provinces except Qinghai and Xinjiang**, in habitats from **hills, waste areas, sunny terraces, riverbanks, and forests at 0–3,400 m**, with an accompanying range across Cambodia, India, Japan, Laos, Malaysia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Russia, Thailand, and Vietnam, and introductions into Europe and North America. That breadth matters for sourcing: it is a common, widely gathered herb rather than a scarce or regionally restricted one, which keeps it an accessible green aromatic rather than a premium rarity.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Elsholtzia is used as the dried aerial herb — leaves and flowering tops — gathered and dried to preserve the volatile oils. Processing is a drying question rather than a resin-tapping or distillation one: the goal is to hold the fresh, minty-spicy note against the losses that come with heat and age.

As a leafy mint-family herb, 香薷 is handled by drying rather than tapping or distilling. The aerial herb — leaves and flowering tops — is gathered and dried to preserve the volatile oils that carry the minty-spicy note. The practical concern is volatility: the aromatic oils are heat- and age-sensitive, so drying temperature and storage condition affect how much of the fresh green character survives. This contrasts with resin materials (which are tapped and hardened) and with distilled oils (which concentrate a single fraction); the whole-herb form is the traditional material of use. ## What determines elsholtzia quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness and aroma intensity — a clean, well-dried herb that retains its minty-spicy, slightly sweet green scent. Faded, musty, or heat-damaged material has lost the volatile note that is the whole point of the ingredient, so aroma on gentle warming is the decisive check.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Aroma intensity** — the minty-spicy, slightly sweet green note is the value; a clean, fresh scent beats a faded one.
2. **Condition** — clean, well-dried herb, free of mold, excess stem, and foreign matter.
3. **Freshness** — volatile oils fade with age and poor storage, so an older or badly stored batch reads flat.
4. **Species clarity** — *Elsholtzia ciliata* (and relatives) rather than an unspecified "mint-family herb," so the buyer knows what green note is actually in hand.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for 香薷; the practical signal is the aroma on gentle warming, which should be minty-spicy and slightly sweet, not musty or faded.
