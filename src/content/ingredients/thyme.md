> **Direct answer:** Thyme (百里香, bǎilǐxiāng) is the culinary thyme, *Thymus vulgaris*, a low mint-family (Lamiaceae) subshrub native to the Mediterranean. Its leaves carry a warm, herbal, green, spicy, slightly medicinal scent, familiar from cooking and, more recently, modern aromatherapy and incense. It is a modern crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient.

## Aroma profile

百里香 (thyme) has a warm, green, herbaceous scent with a spicy, slightly medicinal, almost clove-like edge — the pungent, aromatic smell of the culinary herb rather than a flower. It is greener and warmer than sage, and more savory than the sweet-floral lavender.

## A provenance note (read this)

百里香 (thyme) is a **modern crossover**, not a classical Chinese 香材:

- *Thymus vulgaris* is a Mediterranean species, not native to China; Flora of China does not record it as native.
- The Chinese name 百里香 ("hundred-li fragrance") is a broad term also applied to native Chinese thyme relatives (such as *Thymus mongolicus*); culinary thyme in the European sense is specifically *Thymus vulgaris*.
- Thyme's incense and aromatherapy use is a **modern Western practice**, absent from the classical 香材 canon.

This page records it honestly as a modern culinary and aromatherapy herb — **no classical Chinese origin is claimed, and none is fabricated.**

## Botanical source and origin

*Thymus vulgaris*, culinary or garden thyme, is a low, woody subshrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to the western Mediterranean. The fragrant part is the small leaf, used fresh or dried and distilled for thyme essential oil. The genus *Thymus* includes both the Mediterranean culinary thyme and native Chinese species, but "thyme" as a cooking and fragrance herb is *T. vulgaris*. GBIF records the species.

## In modern use

- **Cooking:** thyme is a classic savory culinary herb in Mediterranean cooking.
- **Aromatherapy:** thyme oil is a warm, herbal, slightly medicinal aromatherapy note.
- **Incense:** thyme appears in some modern incense as a dry herbal accent — a Western addition, not a classical 香材.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

There is **no classical Chinese incense record** for 百里香 (thyme); its position is entirely modern. We make **no medical claims**. The documented facts are botanical identity, the Lamiaceae placement, and its modern culinary and aromatherapy role.
