It is used in cooking, tea, and traditional materia medica for a bittersweet citrus aroma that mellows with age.

## What is chenpi?

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi (陈皮, "aged peel") is the sun-dried, deliberately aged peel of the mandarin/tangerine, *Citrus reticulata*. It is the aged form of citrus peel — not fresh zest — valued for a bittersweet, woody-mellow aroma that deepens with years, and it is most prized from Xinhui (新会), Guangdong.

Chenpi is best understood as a **transformation product**, not a raw ingredient. Fresh mandarin peel is a bright, zesty citrus top note; chenpi is that same peel deliberately dried and aged — commonly 1 to 30+ years — until the brightness is traded for a warm, sweet-spicy, almost tea-like depth. It is a staple of Cantonese cooking and a citrus-woody note in some incense blends, and it sits in a small family of Chinese citrus-peel materials alongside pomelo peel (柚皮) and the fresh-fruit context of litchi husk (荔枝壳).

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi comes from *Citrus reticulata*, the mandarin/tangerine, a member of the rue family (Rutaceae). Flora of China records it as a small tree whose fruit is orange or red with a thin-to-thick, easily removed pericarp — the peel that becomes chenpi — flowering April–May and fruiting October–December.

*Citrus reticulata* (mandarin/tangerine) is a small tree of the rue family (Rutaceae). Flora of China records the leaves as one-foliolate, lanceolate to broadly ovate; the flowers with petals **usually 1.5 cm or less** and **20–25 stamens**; and the fruit **pale yellow, orange, red, or carmine**, oblate to subglobose, with a **pericarp thin to thick and easily removed** — the peel that, when dried and aged, becomes chenpi. It flowers **April–May** and fruits **October–December**, and is extensively cultivated in China south of the Qin Ling. See [Citrus reticulata in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200012434) and [Citrus reticulata on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3190172).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 陈皮 (chénpí) means "aged peel," from 陈 "aged/matured" + 皮 "peel"; the un-aged name is 橘皮 (júpí, "tangerine peel"). The name itself records the key fact: chenpi is defined by aging, not by the fruit alone.

The Chinese terms sort by age:

- **橘皮 (júpí)** — "tangerine/mandarin peel," the fresh or un-aged peel.
- **陈皮 (chénpí)** — "aged peel," the matured, darkened product that commands the higher value.

The name 陈皮 is a definition: 陈 means "aged, matured," and it is precisely the aging that converts a common citrus peel into the prized chenpi. This is why "orange peel" and "chenpi" are not interchangeable — chenpi is a specific, deliberately aged product.

## Where does chenpi come from?

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi is made across southern China from mandarin/tangerine peel, but the benchmark origin is Xinhui (新会), Guangdong, which holds geographical-indication status for its peel. Origin is usually the first thing a buyer checks.

*Citrus reticulata* is extensively cultivated in southern China, and chenpi is produced wherever mandarins are grown. The benchmark, however, is **Xinhui (新会)** in Guangdong, whose peel carries geographical-indication status and is credited with a deep, complex bittersweet-citrus character from the region's soil, climate, and local mandarin variety. Peel from elsewhere is generally considered lower grade. In practice, a chenpi label leads with two facts — **origin** and **stated age** — because those two drive almost all of the value.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Fresh mandarin peel is bright and zesty; chenpi is the aged form, darker and softer, with a warm, sweet-spicy, almost tea-like depth. The prized Xinhui peel is known for a deep, complex bittersweet-citrus character.

Fresh orange peel is bright and citrus; **chenpi** is the *aged* form, which becomes darker, softer, and more mellow-woody over years. The prized Xinhui (新会) peel is known for a deep, complex bittersweet-citrus character.

The key word is **transformation**: fresh peel is a sharp, zesty top note, while aged chenpi trades that brightness for a warm, sweet-spicy, almost tea-like depth. In a blend, chenpi reads as a "mellow citrus" — the brightness of orange rounded by age, closer to dried-fruit-and-spice than to a fresh zest.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi is used in incense as a citrus-woody note that reads as softened, dried-fruit-and-spice citrus rather than sharp fresh zest. It is far better known for cooking and tea, so in incense it appears as a supporting note in warm spice-and-wood blends, not a lead material.

Chenpi's incense role is a niche extension of its culinary fame. Its aged, mellow character suits warm spice-and-wood blends, where it adds a softened, dried-fruit-and-spice citrus that rounds out heavier woods without the sharpness of fresh zest. It is a *supporting note*, usually blended rather than burned alone, and it shows up less often than wood, resin, and floral materials. ## How is chenpi processed?

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi is made by peeling the mandarin, then sun-drying and aging the peel — commonly 1 to 30+ years — during which it darkens and its aroma mellows. The processing is drying and time, not distillation or extraction.

The processing of chenpi is fundamentally **drying and time**:

1. **Peeling** — the mandarin is peeled, usually keeping the peel in clean pieces (often a three-part "clover" cut).
2. **Sun-drying** — the peel is dried, traditionally in the sun.
3. **Aging** — the dried peel is stored and aged, commonly 1 to 30+ years, during which it darkens, thins in aroma, and develops the warm, tea-like depth.

There is no distillation or extraction — chenpi is the whole aged peel, and the aging step *is* the value. Exact aging practices vary by producer, so a stated age is a rough guide rather than a fixed standard.

## What determines chenpi quality?

> **Direct answer:** Two variables drive chenpi quality and value: origin and age. Xinhui (新会) peel is the benchmark; within that, older peel — darker, thinner-skinned in aroma, more expensive — is considered better in traditional framing.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Origin** — Xinhui (新会) peel is the benchmark; other-region peel is generally lower grade.
2. **Age** — older peel (commonly labeled by year or bracket, e.g. "three-year," "ten-year") is darker and more expensive; in traditional framing, older is better.
3. **Aroma** — a deep, complex bittersweet-citrus character without mustiness or off-notes.
4. **Physical condition** — clean, intact peel pieces.

There is no single standardized numerical grading system; age brackets and origin are the market's working vocabulary.

## Grades and age brackets

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi is commonly sold by stated age bracket — "three-year," "ten-year," and so on — with older peel darker, thinner-skinned in aroma, and more expensive. These are market age labels, not a fixed regulatory standard.

Chenpi's "grading" is really an **age ladder**, not a quality standard in the regulatory sense:

- **Younger peel (a few years)** — brighter, lighter, less expensive.
- **Older peel (10+ years)** — darker, more mellow and tea-like, more expensive.

Because aging practices vary by producer, the stated age is a rough guide — a buyer verifies the peel by aroma and appearance rather than trusting the label alone.

## Common forms

## In Chinese tradition

- **Cooking:** chenpi is a staple of Cantonese cooking — in soups, braises, and congee — where it adds a warm, mellow citrus note. **Traditional Use** — documented culinary practice.
- **Tea:** brewed as an ingredient in herbal tisanes, often with pu-erh or other teas. **Traditional Use** — documented.
- **Materia medica:** in traditional Chinese medicine, chenpi is commonly described for digestive support and "regulating qi" — traditional language, not modern clinical evidence. **Traditional Use** — not clinical evidence.
- **Incense/fragrance:** chenpi can appear as a citrus-woody note in blends, though it is far better known for cooking and tea. **Traditional Use** — niche aromatic use.

## Chenpi vs other citrus peels

> **Direct answer:** Chenpi (陈皮) is the *aged* mandarin peel, *Citrus reticulata*; pomelo peel (柚皮) is the *pomelo* peel, *Citrus maxima*; and litchi husk (荔枝壳) is a fruit *pericarp*, *Litchi chinensis*, from a different plant family. They are all citrus-peel-adjacent materials but different species with different aromas.

| Material | Botanical source | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Chenpi (陈皮) | *Citrus reticulata*, aged peel | Warm, mellow, tea-like, bittersweet-citrus |
| Pomelo peel (柚皮) | *Citrus maxima* peel | Bittersweet, floral-woody, thick pith |
| Litchi husk (荔枝壳) | *Litchi chinensis* pericarp | Sweet, fruity, faintly floral |

Chenpi and pomelo peel are different peels from different citrus species; litchi husk is a fruit pericarp from the soapberry family (Sapindaceae), a fruit-peel incense in the classical 香谱 but botanically distinct. They are not interchangeable. See [pomelo peel](/ingredients/pomelo-peel/) and [litchi husk](/ingredients/litchi-husk/).

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The main risks are origin and age mislabeling — non-Xinhui peel sold as Xinhui, or young peel sold as older — and the confusion between chenpi (aged peel) and fresh zest. Both are label risks, not safety risks.

- **Origin mislabeling** — non-Xinhui peel passed off as Xinhui, the benchmark origin.
- **Age mislabeling** — young peel sold as older (and pricier) material; aging is hard to verify from appearance alone.
- **Fresh zest vs chenpi** — chenpi is a distinct, aged product, not dried-out supermarket orange peel.

These are commercial-integrity risks, verified by aroma, appearance, and a trustworthy supplier rather than by a label.
