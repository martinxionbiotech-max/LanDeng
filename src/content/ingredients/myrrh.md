**Technical answer:** Myrrh — *mòyào* (没药) in Chinese — is the aromatic gum-resin of *Commiphora* trees (family Burseraceae), most classically *Commiphora myrrha*. It has been traded and used for millennia in perfume, incense, and traditional medicine, and it is habitually paired with frankincense (乳香), which it resembles in origin but not in scent.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is a warm, balsamic, earthy resin note with a bitter, slightly medicinal edge — darker and heavier than frankincense. It reads grounding rather than bright or citrus, and it is often blended with frankincense to brighten and balance it.

Myrrh has a warm, balsamic, earthy aroma with a bitter, slightly medicinal edge — darker and heavier than frankincense. It is often blended with frankincense, which brightens and balances it. The exact profile varies by *Commiphora* species and grade, but the reference character is resinous, bitter-balsamic, and grounding rather than fresh.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is a gum-resin, not a wood or flower. It is collected from *Commiphora* trees in the family Burseraceae — the same plant family as frankincense — which exude a resin when their bark is wounded. *Commiphora myrrha* is the classical source.

*Commiphora* is a genus of shrubs and small trees in the family Burseraceae, native to arid regions of northeastern Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. The aromatic product is the **oleo-gum-resin** the tree exudes when its bark is wounded — a resin, not the wood of the tree. *Commiphora myrrha* is the classical source, but other *Commiphora* species are also traded under related names, which is the source of most confusion around "myrrh." See [*Commiphora myrrha* on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3994082).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 没药 (mòyào) is the Chinese name for myrrh, classically paired with 乳香 (frankincense) in Chinese materia medica — a pairing that also runs through Western religious and perfumery tradition. The two are habitually named together as a matched pair of resins.

In Chinese materia medica, 没药 (mòyào, myrrh) is classically paired with **乳香 (rǔxiāng, frankincense)**, and the two are habitually mentioned together. The pairing is not a Chinese invention alone: frankincense and myrrh travel together through Western religious and perfumery tradition as well, because both are gum-resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae) and complement each other's aroma — myrrh's bitter-balsamic depth against frankincense's fresh, citrus brightness. See [frankincense (乳香)](/ingredients/frankincense/).

## Where does myrrh come from?

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh comes from arid regions of northeastern Africa and the Arabian Peninsula — chiefly Somalia, Ethiopia, and the Horn of Africa, plus parts of Arabia — where *Commiphora* trees are tapped for their resin.

The producing belt runs through the Horn of Africa (Somalia, Ethiopia) and the southern Arabian Peninsula — the same arid, resin-bearing region that supplies frankincense, which is one reason the two resins have always traveled together in trade. Myrrh reached China as an import along the same routes that carried frankincense, and its Chinese name reflects that imported, transliterated status rather than a native plant.

## Why is myrrh used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is used in incense for its bitter-balsamic, grounding depth, almost always blended rather than burned alone. As a resin it melts and releases aroma under gentle heat, so it is typically warmed, not burned in a flame.

Myrrh's role follows from its physical form and its scent: as a resin it **melts and volatilizes under gentle heat**, and its bitter-balsamic character makes it a depth note rather than a lead. In Chinese 合香 (blended incense) it is matched with frankincense, whose brighter, fresher note prevents the myrrh from feeling too heavy or medicinal. Its long ritual and funerary history — myrrh is one of the oldest traded resins — adds a cultural weight that matches its aroma. See the [frankincense incense guide](/blog/frankincense-incense/).

## Harvest & processing

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is collected by making incisions in the bark of *Commiphora* trees; the tree exudes a pale resin that hardens into reddish-brown tears or irregular lumps, which are harvested by hand. The resin is then sold whole, ground to powder, or steam-distilled for essential oil.

The resin is then sold whole, ground to powder, or steam-distilled for essential oil. This is the same general tapping process as frankincense, though the two resins come from different genera.

## What determines myrrh quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is graded by the resin tears — size, color, and cleanliness — plus species and origin. Larger, well-formed, reddish-brown tears with little dust or bark debris grade higher; mixed fragments and dust grade lower.

The quality factors:

1. **Tear quality** — larger, well-formed tears preferred over dust and fragments.
2. **Color and clarity** — clean, reddish-brown tears generally rank higher than pale or dark, mixed material.
3. **Species and origin** — *Commiphora myrrha* is the reference; other *Commiphora* resins have their own profiles.
4. **Aroma freshness** — a warm, bitter-balsamic scent with no stale or smoky off-notes.

These are trade distinctions, not a single standardized grading system.

## Forms & grades

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is sold as whole tears, powder, and essential oil. Tears suit gentle heating, powder suits blending into incense pastes, and oil is a distilled extract — the same resin in different physical forms.

Myrrh reaches the market in a few forms:

- **Tears / lumps** — the whole dried resin, preferred for gentle heating.
- **Powder** — ground resin, used for blending into incense pastes.
- **Essential oil** — the steam-distilled volatile fraction, used in perfumery.

Higher grades are clean, well-formed tears; lower grades include dust and mixed fragments. The form matters for how you use it — tears for heating, powder for blending. See [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Myrrh vs frankincense

> **Direct answer:** Both are gum-resins from the same plant family (Burseraceae), but from different genera. Myrrh (*Commiphora*) is bitter, balsamic, and earthy; frankincense (*Boswellia*) is fresh, citrus, and creamy. The two are classically paired.

| | Myrrh (没药) | Frankincense (乳香) |
|---|---|---|
| Genus | *Commiphora* | *Boswellia* |
| Aroma | Bitter, balsamic, earthy | Fresh, citrus, creamy |
| Character | Dark, heavy, grounding | Bright, clean, lifting |
| Traditional pairing | Paired with frankincense | Paired with myrrh |

The two are complements, not substitutes: frankincense brightens and lifts, myrrh deepens and grounds. See [frankincense](/ingredients/frankincense/) and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is described in traditional systems (Chinese, Ayurvedic, and others) for oral health, wounds, and inflammation, and there is some modern research — e.g., on mouthwash use — but broader medicinal claims remain traditional or preliminary, not established clinical fact.

- **Traditional Use** — myrrh is used for oral and ritual purposes across multiple traditions, and appears in traditional materia medica paired with frankincense.
- **Historical Record** — myrrh is one of the oldest traded resins, documented in Chinese materia medica and in Western religious and perfumery tradition.
- **Scientific Evidence** — there is some modern research (e.g. on mouthwash use), but most broader medicinal claims remain **traditional or preliminary**, not established clinical fact.

**Important:** we describe myrrh's traditional and incense use; we do **not** make medical claims.

## Botanical note (disambiguation)

> **Direct answer:** "Myrrh" can be confused with other *Commiphora* resins (bdellium, guggul, opopanax), and "myrrh beads" (from an unrelated African tree) or the herb sweet cicely (*Myrrhis odorata*) are different things entirely. In an incense context, "myrrh" means the resin of *Commiphora*.

## Sustainability & sourcing

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh is tapped from wild and semi-wild *Commiphora* trees, so long-term supply depends on the health of those stands. Sourcing should confirm the species and origin, as with frankincense.

Like frankincense, myrrh comes from wild or semi-managed trees in arid regions rather than plantations, so its long-term supply depends on the health of those stands. For a buyer, the practical questions are the same as for other resins: **which species, which origin, and what form** — with documentation where available. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Myrrh goes into incense (loose resin and blends), perfumery (myrrh oil), and some oral-care and personal-care products. The resin, the powder, and the oil serve different markets.

- **Incense** — loose tears for heating, and resin powder blended into sticks, cones, and pastes.
- **Perfumery** — myrrh essential oil as a bitter-balsamic, resinous base note.
- **Oral and personal care** — myrrh appears in some mouthwash and oral-care formulations, reflecting its traditional oral use.

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** Buyers should ask for species and origin, look for whole, clean, reddish-brown tears over dust, and confirm the form — tears, powder, or oil — since each behaves differently in a blend.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

- **Species and origin** — *Commiphora myrrha* and which region; a real seller can name them.
- **Tear quality** — whole, clean, reddish-brown tears over dust and mixed fragments.
- **Form** — tears for heating, powder for blending, oil for fragrance; they are not interchangeable.
- **Aroma** — warm, bitter-balsamic, with no stale or smoky off-notes.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Do not consume resin based on traditional-medicine claims without professional guidance. See our [safety guide](/safety/).

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, myrrh is a bitter-balsamic depth note used in small proportion, and the working variables are species, tear grade, and form consistency — not a generic "myrrh" label. Because it is heavy, dosing is the practical constraint.

Myrrh's manufacturing role is a **grounding depth note**, almost never a lead. Its bitterness and weight mean a little goes a long way, and overuse makes a blend feel heavy or medicinal.

- **"Myrrh" is a family, not one material.** Other *Commiphora* resins (opopanax, guggul, bdellium) read differently, so a formula built on *C. myrrha* will not reproduce if the source switches to a related resin under the same loose name.
- **Resin powder is sticky and heat-sensitive.** Compared with wood powder, resin powder behaves differently under grinding and heat, so particle size and how it is combined with a binder affect burn — and the right settings **depend on formulation**. There is no universal figure; a buyer should sample against their own production.

In our view, the commercial value is a **documented, consistent species-and-grade supply**, used in a controlled proportion, rather than a generic "myrrh" label.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between myrrh and frankincense?** Both are aromatic gum-resins from trees in the Burseraceae family, but they come from different genera and smell quite different. Myrrh is the resin of *Commiphora* trees, and it reads darker, bitter, and balsamic with a slightly medicinal edge. Frankincense is the resin of *Boswellia* trees, and it reads fresher, citrus, and creamy. In use they are classically paired — in Chinese materia medica, 没药 (myrrh) is traditionally matched with 乳香 (frankincense), and in incense the two are blended so frankincense brightens and balances myrrh's heavier, more earthy character. They are related materials, not the same resin.

**What does myrrh smell like?** Myrrh has a warm, balsamic, and earthy aroma with a bitter, slightly medicinal edge — darker and heavier than frankincense. It reads resinous and grounding rather than bright or citrus, with a depth that lingers. This bitter-balsamic character is why myrrh is often blended with frankincense, which adds a fresher, brighter counterpoint and prevents the myrrh from feeling too heavy or medicinal on its own. In perfumery and incense, myrrh sits as a base or middle note, lending warmth and an earthy richness to wood-and-resin blends. The exact profile varies by *Commiphora* species and grade.

**How do you burn myrrh?** Myrrh resin is traditionally warmed on a lit charcoal disc or, more gently, on an electric incense heater. To burn it on charcoal, place the disc in sand or ash on a heatproof surface, light it until it glows, then set small pieces of myrrh resin on top — the resin melts and releases fragrant smoke. The electric-heater method is slower and lower in smoke, which many people prefer indoors. Use small pieces at a time, since a little myrrh goes a long way and it can burn harshly if overheated. It is often blended with frankincense, which brightens the scent and balances myrrh's bitter, earthy edge.

**Is myrrh a wood?** No — myrrh is a dried gum-resin collected from the bark of *Commiphora* trees, not the wood itself. The tree is tapped by cutting the bark, and the resin that exudes is what becomes myrrh, hardening into reddish-brown tears or lumps. This is the same general process as frankincense, though the two resins come from different genera. The word "myrrh" in an incense context always refers to this resin, not to wood, and it should not be confused with unrelated materials such as "myrrh beads" from an African tree or the herb sweet cicely (*Myrrhis odorata*).

**Is myrrh used in Chinese incense?** Yes — myrrh, 没药, is a classic resin in Chinese blended incense (合香) and traditional materia medica, where it is classically paired with frankincense (乳香). The two resins are commonly mentioned together, with myrrh contributing a bitter-balsamic depth and frankincense adding a brighter, fresher note. Myrrh's warm, earthy character makes it a useful base or supporting note in wood-and-resin blends. Beyond incense, myrrh has a long record of use in perfume and traditional medicine across multiple traditions, though most medicinal claims remain traditional or preliminary rather than established clinical fact.

**What is the difference between myrrh and opopanax?** Both are resins from *Commiphora* trees, but they are distinct materials with different scent profiles. Opopanax, sometimes called "sweet myrrh," has a sweeter, more balsamic-sweet and slightly powdery character, while myrrh proper is more bitter, earthy, and medicinal. The two come from different *Commiphora* species, and they play different roles in perfumery: opopanax leans sweet and ambery, while myrrh leans dark and resinous. Because both are called myrrh in some older texts, the terms are easy to confuse, but in a modern incense or perfume context they are treated as related yet separate resins.
