## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 薰衣草 (lavender) has a fresh, clean floral scent with a pronounced herbal, camphoraceous edge — the soft, sweet-floral lift of linalool and linalyl acetate over a green, slightly medicinal base. It is one of the most recognizable modern fragrance notes, bridging fresh herb and soft flower.

薰衣草 (lavender) has a fresh, clean floral scent with a pronounced herbal, camphoraceous edge — the soft, sweet-floral lift of linalool and linalyl acetate over a green, slightly medicinal base. It is one of the most recognizable modern fragrance notes, bridging fresh herb and soft flower.

The herb-flower bridge is lavender's signature: it is not purely floral (like rose or jasmine) nor purely herbal (like rosemary) but sits between — a soft, clean floral over a green, camphoraceous herb. This duality is what made it the workhorse of modern aromatherapy and home fragrance, and it is what distinguishes it from both its floral and its herbal neighbors.

## The "clothes-scenting herb" naming point (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The name 薰衣草 is literal and revealing: 薰 "to scent/fumigate," 衣 "clothes," 草 "herb" — "the herb that scents clothes." It records lavender's Western use of perfuming linen drawers and sachets, not any classical Chinese standing.

The Chinese name 薰衣草 (xūnyīcǎo) is worth unpacking, because it encodes the plant's *actual* Western use rather than a Chinese pedigree:

- **薰 (xūn)** — to scent, fumigate, or perfume.
- **衣 (yī)** — clothes.
- **草 (cǎo)** — herb/grass.

So 薰衣草 means literally **"the herb that scents clothes"** — a descriptive, functional name that records lavender's long Western use in sachets and linen drawers. This is a revealing contrast with the classical 香材, whose names are rooted in Chinese history and tradition: lavender's name tells you it is a *foreign, functional* plant, not a native one. The name itself is the provenance note.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Lavandula angustifolia*, English lavender, is a small woody shrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to the western Mediterranean. Its flowers are the fragrant part, distilled for lavender essential oil or dried for sachets; *L. angustifolia* is the "true" or English lavender among the *Lavandula* species.

*Lavandula angustifolia*, English lavender, is a small woody shrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to the western Mediterranean. Its flowers are the fragrant part, distilled for lavender essential oil or dried for sachets. The genus *Lavandula* includes several lavandin and spike-lavender relatives, but *L. angustifolia* is the "true" or English lavender. GBIF records the species.

The mint-family placement (Lamiaceae) is significant: lavender shares its family with rosemary, mint, sage, and basil, which is why its scent has that green, herbal, camphoraceous edge beneath the floral. It is a *mint-family shrub*, not a true floral like rose (Rosaceae) or jasmine (Oleaceae).

## The lavender / rosemary distinction (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Lavender and rosemary are both mint-family (Lamiaceae) herbs with a camphoraceous edge, but lavender is soft, sweet, and floral, while rosemary is greener and more savory. They are different genera — *Lavandula* vs *Salvia* — and are not interchangeable.

The two mint-family "camphoraceous" herbs are easily confused:

- **薰衣草 (lavender)** — *Lavandula angustifolia*, soft, sweet, floral, with a clean camphoraceous edge. This entry.
- **迷迭香 (rosemary)** — *Salvia rosmarinus*, greener, more savory, piney-camphoraceous. Its own entry.

Both are Lamiaceae herbs with a camphoraceous edge — hence the shared register — but lavender is sweet and floral, rosemary savory and green. They are different genera and not interchangeable. See [rosemary](/ingredients/rosemary/).

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Lavender's fragrant material is both the dried flower (for sachets and incense) and the distilled essential oil (for perfumery and aromatherapy). The oil is steam-distilled from the flowering tops; the dried flower is a separate, simpler product.

The processing is a **flower-drying / oil-distillation** split:

1. **Drying** — flowering tops are dried for sachets and, in some incense, as dried flowers.
2. **Distillation** — the essential oil is steam-distilled from the fresh flowering tops.
3. **Use** — the oil goes to perfumery and aromatherapy; the dried flowers go to sachets and home fragrance.

The two products — dried flower and essential oil — have different uses: the oil is the concentrated fragrance material, the dried flower the simpler, milder sachet material.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Lavender quality is a matter of species and freshness: *L. angustifolia* (English lavender) is the prized "true" lavender, with a sweeter, more floral oil than the harsher lavandin hybrids. Freshness and the linalool/linalyl-acetate character are the practical signals.

The quality factors:

1. **Species** — the decisive factor; *L. angustifolia* (English lavender) is prized over the harsher, higher-yielding lavandin (*L. × intermedia*).
2. **Freshness** — the fresh-floral note is volatile; a fresh oil or flower is brighter.
3. **Chemical character** — the linalool / linalyl acetate balance shapes the sweet-floral quality.

The *angustifolia* vs lavandin distinction is the real quality point in lavender: "true" lavender has a sweeter, more refined oil, while lavandin is cheaper and more camphoraceous.
