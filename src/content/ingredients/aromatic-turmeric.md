> **Direct answer:** Aromatic turmeric (郁金, yùjīn) is the dried rhizome of *Curcuma aromatica*, a ginger-family (Zingiberaceae) perennial native to South and Southeast Asia and cultivated in southern China. It is a warm, camphoraceous, earthy rhizome sometimes called "wild turmeric," and its name (郁金) literally means "aromatic gold." It is one of three closely related *Curcuma* rhizomes that are routinely confused.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 郁金 (yùjīn) literally means "aromatic gold" — 郁 (fragrant/lush) + 金 (gold). But the same two characters also name the tulip (郁金香, "aromatic-gold fragrance"), and this collision is a classic Chinese nomenclature trap: a rhizome and a flower share a name but are unrelated.

The name 郁金 (yùjīn) is a praise-name: 郁 "fragrant, lush" + 金 "gold" — "aromatic gold," a name of honor for an aromatic rhizome. But here is the trap: the very same characters 郁金 also appear in 郁金香 (yùjīnxiāng), the tulip — literally "aromatic-gold fragrance." So 郁金 can name:

- **郁金 (the rhizome)** — *Curcuma aromatica*, the "wild turmeric." This entry.
- **郁金香 (the tulip)** — the flower *Tulipa*, named for its fragrance, not related to the rhizome at all.

The two senses meet only in the characters, not in botany. A label that says 郁金 names a rhizome; a label that says 郁金香 names a flower.

> **Direct answer:** Two confusions sit inside 郁金. First, 郁金香 (tulip) is not 郁金 (the rhizome) — the shared characters name a flower and a rhizome that are unrelated. Second, 郁金 is one of three closely related *Curcuma* rhizomes — 姜黄 (turmeric), 郁金 (aromatic turmeric), 莪术 (zedoary) — that historical sources have not always kept apart.

Two separate confusions live in 郁金, and both matter:

- **The tulip collision.** 郁金香 (the tulip, *Tulipa*) and 郁金 (the rhizome, *Curcuma aromatica*) are unrelated plants that share two characters. - **The *Curcuma* three-way confusion.** 郁金 is also routinely confused with its genus-mates:

  - **莪术** (*Curcuma zedoaria* / *C. phaeocaulis*) — the zedoary group, the most medicinal of the three.

The confusion arises because all three are *Curcuma* rhizomes with warm, earthy, camphoraceous characters, and historical sources have not always kept them apart. **This page describes 郁金 = *Curcuma aromatica* = aromatic (wild) turmeric.** When a source says 姜黄 or 莪术, it is describing a different material — and when it says 郁金香, it may be describing the tulip. ## The imported oil record (香乘考据)

> **Direct answer:** The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 郁金油 (aromatic-turmeric oil) and 郁金香, and notes that "in India people anoint the body with various aromatics, namely sandalwood and 郁金" (印度之人身塗諸香所謂旃檀鬱金也) — an imported aromatic-oil use, distinct from the culinary turmeric and from the tulip.

The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) preserves a distinctive record of 郁金 as an *imported aromatic*, not a domestic cooking spice:

- **郁金油 (aromatic-turmeric oil)** — the text records 郁金油, the aromatic oil of the rhizome, as a fragrance material.
- **The body-anointing note** — the text observes that "in India people anoint the body with various aromatics, namely sandalwood and 郁金" (印度之人身塗諸香所謂旃檀鬱金也), placing 郁金 beside sandalwood as an anointing aromatic.

This is a meaningful distinction: it records 郁金 in its *aromatic* register — an oil and an anointing material — rather than as the culinary coloring spice that turmeric (姜黄) became. The imported, fragrant-oil identity is what separates 郁金 from its genus-mates in the classical record.
