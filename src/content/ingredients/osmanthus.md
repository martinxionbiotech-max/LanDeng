**Definition:** Osmanthus — *guìhuā* (桂花) in Chinese — is the small golden flower of *Osmanthus fragrans*, prized for a sweet, apricot- and honey-like fragrance. It is a classic Chinese fragrance, used in tea, desserts, and incense.

## Chinese name and terminology (the 桂 character)

> **Direct answer:** The Chinese name 桂花 (guìhuā) shares the 桂 character with unrelated plants: 月桂叶 (bay leaf, *Laurus nobilis*) and 桂皮 (cinnamon, *Cinnamomum cassia*). In classical texts, 月桂 often means osmanthus — not the bay laurel — so the 桂 character needs careful reading.

The character 桂 ("guì") appears in several unrelated plant names, which is a real source of confusion:

- **桂花 (osmanthus)** — *Osmanthus fragrans* (Oleaceae), the sweet apricot-honey flower. This entry.
- **月桂叶 (bay leaf)** — *Laurus nobilis* (Lauraceae), the Mediterranean bay laurel, a herbal leaf. See [bay-leaf](/ingredients/bay-leaf/).
- **桂皮 (cinnamon/cassia)** — *Cinnamomum cassia* (Lauraceae), a bark. See [cinnamon](/ingredients/cinnamon/).

The classical trap is 月桂: in old Chinese texts 月桂 usually denotes the osmanthus, not the bay laurel. When a source says 月桂子 (osmanthus fruit), it is describing osmanthus. The shared 桂 character connects three unrelated plants, and Chinese names — 桂花, 月桂叶, 桂皮 — keep them apart.

## Where does osmanthus come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Osmanthus fragrans* is native to East Asia — from the Himalaya through southern China to Taiwan and southern Japan — and is widely cultivated there for its autumn bloom and fragrance.

*Osmanthus fragrans* is native to East Asia, ranging from the Himalaya through southern and central China to Taiwan and southern Japan. It has been cultivated for centuries as a garden and fragrance plant, and its strong autumn bloom made it a cultural landmark — the flower that marks mid-autumn (中秋) in the Chinese calendar. Because it is a cultivated ornamental and fragrance crop rather than a wild-harvested material, supply is stable and the flower is economical.

## Varieties (金桂 / 银桂 / 丹桂)

> **Direct answer:** *Osmanthus fragrans* has several color variants: 金桂 (gold, richest and most fragrant), 银桂 (silver, lighter), 丹桂 (red, less fragrant), and 四季桂 (four-seasons, blooms repeatedly). The golden 金桂 type is the reference for fragrance and tea.

*Osmanthus fragrans* has several color variants, each with a slightly different character:

- **金桂 (gold osmanthus)** — golden-yellow, the richest and most fragrant.
- **银桂 (silver osmanthus)** — pale/white, lighter scent.
- **丹桂 (red osmanthus)** — orange-red, less fragrant.
- **四季桂 (four-seasons osmanthus)** — a less fragrant variety that blooms repeatedly.

In fragrance and tea, the golden (金桂) type is the reference. The color names appear in Chinese sources and product labels, so they are useful to recognize.

## How is osmanthus processed?

> **Direct answer:** Osmanthus is processed lightly: the flowers are picked and dried whole for tea and desserts, or made into osmanthus sugar or syrup. For perfumery, a solvent extraction yields osmanthus absolute, a costly natural floral extract.

Osmanthus processing is simple and mostly preserves the flower:

- **Drying** — the small flowers are picked and dried whole for tea (桂花茶), desserts, and blending.
- **Sugar and syrup** — fresh flowers are layered with sugar or steeped in syrup to make 桂花糖 (osmanthus sugar) and syrup, staples of Chinese desserts.
- **Absolute** — for perfumery, solvent extraction yields osmanthus absolute, a costly natural extract because the tiny flowers yield little.

## What determines osmanthus quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality rests on the color variant (金桂 grades highest), freshness, and aroma intensity. The golden 金桂 type is the fragrance reference; fresh, fragrant, whole flowers beat faded or stem-heavy material.

The main quality factors:

1. **Color variant** — 金桂 (gold) is the most fragrant and the reference; 丹桂 (red) is less so.
2. **Freshness** — well-dried, fragrant flowers beat stale or faded ones.
3. **Cleanliness** — freedom from stems, leaves, and debris.
4. **Aroma intensity** — the sweet apricot-honey scent is the practical test.

Osmanthus is not graded by a formal standardized system; these are trade distinctions, and the color names on the label indicate the likely strength and character.

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The two risks are naming — the 桂 character shared with bay leaf and cinnamon — and, for the absolute, substitution: genuine osmanthus absolute is expensive, and synthetic or extended "osmanthus" notes are common. Dried-flower color variants should also match the label.

Osmanthus's risks are modest but real:

- **Naming** — 桂花 (osmanthus) shares the 桂 character with 月桂叶 (bay leaf) and 桂皮 (cinnamon); a classical text saying 月桂 usually means osmanthus, and the three are unrelated plants.
- **Absolute substitution** — genuine osmanthus absolute is costly, so synthetic osmanthus notes (built on ionones and related molecules) are common. This is legitimate when labeled, a problem only when sold as natural absolute.
- **Color-variant accuracy** — a label claiming 金桂 (gold) should not deliver a lower-fragrance 丹桂 (red).
