> **Direct answer:** Paicao (排草, páicǎo) is the dried herb of *Lysimachia capillipes*, a small perennial of the primrose family (Primulaceae) native to southern China. It is an aromatic herb with a sweet, hay-like, faintly woody scent, used in Chinese incense as a supporting herbal note in 合香 (blended incense). It belongs to the same genus as linglingxiang (零陵香), *Lysimachia foenum-graecum*.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Paicao is herbal and sweet, with a hay-like, faintly woody character typical of the *Lysimachia* aromatic herbs — a fresh, green-sweet scent that recalls coumarin-tonka notes without being heavy. It is a supporting herb: it fills the herbal register of a blend, supporting and softening brighter or spicier ingredients rather than leading with its own strong note.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Lysimachia capillipes* is a perennial herb of the primrose family (Primulaceae). Flora of China describes a plant 40–60 cm tall, strongly curry-scented when dry, with ovate to lanceolate leaves 1.5–7 cm and small yellow flowers, growing in mixed forests and on rocky limestone slopes across southern and eastern China.

Flora of China describes *Lysimachia capillipes* precisely: a **perennial herb 40–60 cm tall, strongly curry-scented when dry**, with stems two to many, erect, angular or winged, branched from the middle; leaves alternate, ovate to lanceolate, **1.5–7 × 1–3 cm**, entire or obscurely undulate at the margin; flowers **solitary and axillary**, the corolla **yellow, 6–8 mm**; capsule globose, 3–4 mm. It grows in mixed forests, streamsides, on acidic soils and rocky limestone slopes at **300–2,000 m**, distributed across Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, southern Henan, Hunan, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, eastern Yunnan, and Zhejiang, and the Philippines. See [Lysimachia capillipes in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200016984) and [Lysimachia capillipes on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/4005914).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 排草 (also 排香草, classical 排草香) names the herb, but the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) gives the key classical note: 排草 was described as a root — "白色状如细柳根" (white, like fine willow roots) — gathered from 交址 (Jiaozhi) and cultivated in 岭南 (Lingnan). The classical description of the part used (a root) is worth holding against the modern whole-herb identification.

The name 排草 (also written 排香草; the classical form is 排草香) names the herb, and the Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) gives the definitive classical description:

> "排草出交址，今岭南亦或莳之，草根也，白色，状如细柳根，人多伪杂之。淮海志云：排草香状如白茅香，芬烈如茅香。白茅香、排草香、瓶香、耕香当是一类。"

— 排草 comes from 交址 (Jiaozhi, northern Vietnam), and is now also cultivated in 岭南 (Lingnan); it is a **root**, white, shaped like fine willow roots, and **often adulterated** ("人多伪杂之"). The *Huaihai Zhi* says 排草香 resembles 白茅香 and is as strongly fragrant as 茅香, and groups 白茅香, 排草香, 瓶香, and 耕香 as one class.

This is the important nuance: the classical 排草香 was described as a **root** (草根), whereas the modern botanical identification *Lysimachia capillipes* is used as the whole herb. The discrepancy is a provenance note, not a settled botanical conclusion — worth holding against any claim that a given "排草" is exactly the classical material.

The shared profile reflects their common genus, not the same plant.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** 排草 is a supporting aromatic herb in classical 合香 formulas, contributing a sweet, hay-like body beneath woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* records its use in formulas ("广排草须一两"), where it functions as a blender that fills the herbal register rather than leading.

排草's role in incense is as a *supporting herbal note*: a sweet, hay-like body that fills the herbal register of a 合香 blend beneath woods and resins. The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records it in actual formulas — for example "甲香制过一钱五分，广排草须一两，花露一两" (murex processed, one-and-a-half parts; Guangdong paicao, one part; flower dew, one part) — using the qualified name **广排草** ("Guangdong paicao"), which marks the material by its origin the way 广零陵香 marks the Lingnan 零陵香. It is a blender, not a lead; its sweet-green character supports a formula rather than carrying one.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 排草 is handled as a dried herb. The *Xiangcheng* records one processing step in a formula — "排草酒浸半日，炒干" (paicao soaked in wine half a day, then dry-fried) — a classical treatment that both extracted and modified the herb before blending. Otherwise, processing is the usual drying question.

The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records a specific classical processing step for 排草 in one formula: **"排草酒浸半日，炒干"** — the herb soaked in wine for half a day and then dry-fried — a treatment that extracted and modified the herb before it entered the blend. Outside that formula, 排草 is handled like other leafy aromatic herbs: gathered and dried to preserve its sweet, hay-like character. The note is volatile, so drying temperature and freshness govern how much of it survives; the whole dried herb (or, classically, the root) is the material of use.

## What determines paicao quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on aroma — a sweet, hay-like, faintly woody note from a clean, well-dried herb — plus species clarity and part-of-plant. The *Xiangcheng* adds a caution worth repeating: 排草 was "人多伪杂之" (often adulterated), so identity and purity are the first checks.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Identity** — *Lysimachia capillipes*, not its close relative 零陵香 (*L. foenum-graecum*).
2. **Aroma** — sweet, hay-like, faintly woody on gentle warming; a fresh sweet-green scent beats a faded one.
3. **Condition and purity** — clean, well-dried herb; the *Xiangcheng* records that 排草 was often adulterated ("人多伪杂之").
4. **Part and origin** — the classical 排草香 was a root from 交址; the modern whole-herb identification should be held against that provenance note.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder; identity, aroma, and purity are the practical signals.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** 排草 is a supporting herb whose commercial risk is twofold: it shares its sweet-hay profile with a close relative (零陵香), and the classical record describes a root while the modern identification is a whole herb. A buyer should treat "排草" as requiring both a species name and a part-of-plant statement.

From a manufacturing perspective, 排草 illustrates two overlapping sourcing risks. Two points follow:

- **Shared profile is not shared identity.** 排草 (*Lysimachia capillipes*) and 零陵香 (*Lysimachia foenum-graecum*) are the same genus, both curry-scented when dry, both sweet-hay blenders — but they are distinct materials with distinct entries. In a repeatable blend, "a *Lysimachia* sweet herb" is not a specification; the species must carry it. The two should not be substituted on the strength of a similar aroma.
- **Part-of-plant has drifted too.** The classical 排草香 was a root from 交址 ("草根也，白色，状如细柳根"); the modern botanical identification is the whole herb. Neither is "wrong," but they are not necessarily the same commodity, and a buyer who assumes the label resolves the part is assuming more than the name guarantees. The *Xiangcheng*'s "人多伪杂之" is a standing warning that 排草 was adulterated even classically.

In our view, 排草 is a useful sweet-herb supporting note — buy it by species name, state the part (whole herb vs root), check purity against a documented history of adulteration, and treat it as a blender rather than a lead 香材.
