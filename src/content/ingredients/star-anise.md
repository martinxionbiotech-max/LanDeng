**Technical answer:** Star anise — *bājiǎo* (八角) in Chinese — is the star-shaped fruit of *Illicium verum*, an evergreen tree of southern China and Vietnam. It is one of the defining sweet-spice aromas of Chinese cooking, and a traditional-medicine item, but it is **not** a classical Chinese incense material: it does not appear in the Song- and Ming-dynasty fragrance manuals. Its presence in incense is a modern culinary crossover.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Star anise smells sweet and licorice-like — warm, spicy, and intensely aromatic — driven largely by anethole, the same molecule behind anise and fennel. It is a "sweet-spice" note in the family of cinnamon and clove, used in modern incense for a warm, food-like sweetness.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Star anise is the dried, star-shaped fruit of *Illicium verum*, an evergreen tree in the family Schisandraceae. It is unrelated to anise (*Pimpinella anisum*, carrot family) and fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*, carrot family), despite sharing the anethole sweetness and the 茴香 name element.

*Illicium verum* is an evergreen tree in the family Schisandraceae, native to southern China and Vietnam. The part used is the **dried fruit**: an eight-pointed, star-shaped cluster of seed-bearing follicles, each carrying the intense anethole aroma. Two botanical points matter:

- **Not related to anise or fennel.** Star anise is a Schisandraceae tree; anise (*Pimpinella anisum*) and fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*) are both carrot-family (Apiaceae) herbs. The three converge on the same molecule (anethole) and the same 茴 name element, but they are unrelated plants.
- **A look-alike to avoid.** *Illicium anisatum* (Japanese star anise) is a near-identical species whose fruit is toxic — the key safety distinction, covered below.

## Chinese name and terminology (八角 / 茴香)

> **Direct answer:** Chinese calls star anise 八角 (bājiǎo, "eight corners"), 八角茴香, or 大茴香 ("big fennel"). The 茴香 element is shared with fennel (小茴香, "small fennel") and anise (茴芹), three unrelated plants that all carry the anethole sweetness.

The 茴 name element connects three unrelated plants, which is a real source of confusion:

- **八角 / 大茴香 (star anise)** — *Illicium verum* (Schisandraceae), the star-shaped tree fruit. This entry.
- **小茴香 (fennel)** — *Foeniculum vulgare* (Apiaceae), the seed-like fruit of a carrot-family herb. See [fennel](/ingredients/fennel/).
- **茴芹 (anise)** — *Pimpinella anisum* (Apiaceae), the small Mediterranean seed-fruit. See [anise](/ingredients/anise/).

The shared 茴香 name and shared anethole sweetness explain why the three are confused. When a classical recipe lists 茴香, it is usually read as fennel; 大茴香 specifically denotes star anise.

## Where does star anise come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Illicium verum* is native to southern China — chiefly Guangxi and Yunnan — and northern Vietnam. It is a cultivated evergreen tree, and its dried fruit has been a regional spice for centuries.

Star anise is a tree of southern China and northern Vietnam, with Guangxi and Yunnan the classic producing regions. It is cultivated rather than wild-collected, so supply is stable, and the fruit is harvested when mature and dried whole into the familiar star shape. The same region's culinary tradition made star anise a cornerstone of Chinese braising and five-spice — which is why its aroma reads as familiar and food-adjacent.

## A safety note that matters (two "star anise")

> **Direct answer:** There are two "star anise": Chinese star anise (*Illicium verum*, the safe culinary spice) and Japanese star anise (*Illicium anisatum*), a near-identical look-alike whose fruit is toxic and must not be eaten or burned for fragrance. This species distinction is the key safety point.

This is the one point about star anise that is genuinely safety-relevant, and it is a real distinction, not a pedantic one:

- **Chinese star anise** — *Illicium verum*, the safe culinary spice.
- **Japanese star anise** — *Illicium anisatum* (syn. *I. religiosum*), a look-alike whose fruits are **toxic** and must not be eaten or burned for fragrance.

The two are nearly identical in appearance and have been involved in adulteration incidents. For a material that is primarily a food spice, this substitution risk is the key thing to know.

## How is star anise processed?

> **Direct answer:** Star anise is processed simply: the mature fruit is picked and dried whole, preserving its star shape. It is then sold whole or ground, or distilled for star anise oil, a separate concentrated product.

Star anise processing is minimal:

- **Harvest** — the mature fruit is picked and sun-dried whole, keeping the eight-pointed star shape.
- **Grading by appearance** — whole, intact, well-formed stars are preferred; broken or faded fruit grades lower.
- **Grinding** — dried fruit is ground to powder for blending and spice use.
- **Distillation** — steam distillation yields star anise oil, a concentrated anethole product (also the source of extracted shikimic acid, below).

## What determines star anise quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality rests on the fruit being whole, star-shaped, and intensely aromatic — the scent is the main indicator — and, above all, on the species being *Illicium verum* rather than the toxic *Illicium anisatum*.

The main quality factors:

1. **Species** — *Illicium verum* (edible, fragrant) vs *Illicium anisatum* (toxic); the critical distinction.
2. **Fruit integrity** — whole, well-formed stars beat broken or faded fruit.
3. **Aroma intensity** — a strong, sweet, licorice-anise scent indicates freshness and oil content.
4. **Cleanliness** — freedom from debris and mold.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter; burn in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Do **not** confuse star anise with the toxic Japanese star anise (*Illicium anisatum*). Keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice. ## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, star anise is a cheap, potent sweet-spice accent whose value is controlled warmth, not rarity — but it carries a species-specific safety requirement that most aromatics do not. The key variables are species and consistency, and the dosing depends on formulation.

Star anise's commercial logic is familiar — abundant and strong, valued for consistent, economical sweetness — but it carries a distinctive constraint:

- **The species check is non-negotiable.** Because *Illicium verum* and the toxic *Illicium anisatum* are near-identical in appearance, "star anise" must be pinned to the species, not the common name. This is a safety matter, not a grading nicety.
- **It is a flavor crossover, not a 香材.** Star anise's warmth is culinary in origin, so it reads food-like in a blend. Whether that is desirable depends entirely on the formula — it suits modern gourmand and spice-forward blends, and is out of place in an authentically classical wood-and-resin composition.
- **Dosing is the constraint.** Like clove and cinnamon, star anise is easy to overuse; the useful range is a small proportion. The right amount **depends on formulation**, so no single figure transfers between recipes.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **species-confirmed *Illicium verum*, bought whole and consistent**, used as a controlled sweet-spice accent — and never accepted on the strength of the name "star anise" alone.
