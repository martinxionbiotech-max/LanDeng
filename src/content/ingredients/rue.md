> **Direct answer:** Rue (芸香, yúnxiāng) is the dried leafy herb of *Ruta graveolens*, a small evergreen shrub of the rue family (Rutaceae) native to the Mediterranean and western Asia, long cultivated in China. It is a strongly bitter, herbaceous aromatic with a green, faintly woody, penetrating scent, used in Chinese incense as a powerful accent and in the classical "book herb" (芸草) tradition. Its Chinese name is also applied to a group of aromatic book-preserving herbs, and it must be distinguished from the "兰"-named fragrant herbs such as 佩兰.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** In Chinese, 芸香 has a broad application: it names rue (*Ruta graveolens*), and it also names a group of aromatic herbs placed among books to repel insects — the "book herb" tradition that gives a library its classical names 芸阁 and 芸台. The two senses are related but distinct, and a label that says only 芸香 must be read with the herb context in mind.

The name 芸香 carries two overlapping senses in Chinese:

- **芸香 (rue)** — *Ruta graveolens*, the bitter Mediterranean herb. This entry.
- **芸香 (the "book herb")** — a broader, functional sense: aromatic herbs placed among books and scrolls to deter insects, so that a library or study is poetically called 芸阁 (yún'gé) or 芸台 (yúntái).

The two senses are related — both are strong, protective green aromatics — but they are not the same claim. A label that says only 芸香 identifies a function and a family of herbs more than a single species.

## The 芸香 / 佩兰 "兰"-name distinction (read this)

> **Direct answer:** 芸香 (rue) and 佩兰 (eupatorium) are different plants from different families, but both belong to the classical roster of Chinese fragrant herbs, and the character 兰 ("orchid") in 佩兰 invites confusion with true orchids. 芸香 is a Rutaceae rue with a bitter, penetrating scent; 佩兰 is an Asteraceae herb whose name means "worn orchid." Neither is an orchid.

A recurring difficulty in classical Chinese aromatic nomenclature is that the character 兰 ("orchid") names many fragrant plants that are **not** orchids, and 芸香 sits beside those names without being one of them:

- **芸香 (rue)** — *Ruta graveolens*, Rutaceae; bitter, herbaceous, penetrating. This entry.
- **佩兰 (eupatorium)** — *Eupatorium fortunei*, Asteraceae; fresh, herbaceous, mildly sweet, literally "worn orchid" (see [eupatorium](/ingredients/eupatorium/)).
- **泽兰** — *Eupatorium japonicum*, a related but distinct herb (see [eupatorium-japonicum](/ingredients/eupatorium-japonicum/)).

The point for a buyer or reader: "兰" is a name of honor in Chinese fragrant-herb culture, not a botanical family. 芸香 is a rue, 佩兰 is a eupatorium, and neither is an orchid — yet all three share the classical fragrant-herb register, which is precisely where the confusion lives.

## Rue in the classical record (香乘考据)

> **Direct answer:** The Ming *Xiangcheng* (香乘) records 芸香 through a chain of early texts — the *Shuowen* ("芸香草也，似苜蓿"), the *Erya Yi* (其叶生於仲春), and Zheng Xuan's note that people cultivate it — and devotes entries to 芸香 planted before palace halls and to the "芸香室" (library), the origin of the classical library name. It also records the practice of laying 芸香 leaves under a mat to drive off fleas.

The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) gathers the classical 芸香 record from several early sources:

- **《说文》 (Shuowen)** — "芸香草也，似苜蓿" (the yun herb, resembling medic/alfalfa).
- **《尔雅翼》 (Erya Yi)** — "仲春之月，芸始生……秋复生，叶间微白如粉" (the yun begins to grow in the second spring month; it regrows in autumn, its leaves faintly white).
- **郑玄 (Zheng Xuan)** — "芸香草，世人种之" (the yun herb is cultivated by people).
- **宫殿植芸香 (palace plantings)** — 显阳殿前芸香一株, 徽音殿前芸香二株, 含英殿前……太极殿前芸香四畦, 式干殿前芸香八畦 — the herb planted in measured rows before the palace halls.
- **芸香室** — the "yun-fragrance room," from the 陈子昂集 (Chen Zi'ang's collection), the origin of the library's poetic name.
- **芸香去虱** — "采芸香叶置席下能去蚤虱" (laying yun leaves under a mat drives off fleas and lice), from the *Xu Bowuzhi*.

These are the documentary roots of the "book herb" tradition: the 芸台 and 芸阁 library names come from the practice of placing 芸香 among books and scrolls to protect them from insects.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Rue is used as a powerful, bitter-green accent — a penetrating, protective note blended sparingly into 合香 rather than burned alone. Its value is intensity and a "protective" green character, not sweetness, which is why it is a small-dose ingredient and why its classical fame attaches to scenting and book-preserving rather than to a pleasant burn.

Rue's role in incense is as a *powerful accent*: a bitter, penetrating green note used sparingly in 合香 for its intensity and protective character. It is not a pleasant base, and it is not burned alone — its value is the sharp, green, "warding" quality it lends a blend in small doses. The same intensity underlies its better-documented classical uses in scenting garments and protecting books and scrolls from insects, where the goal was deterrence rather than fragrance.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Rue is used as the dried leafy herb, gathered and dried to preserve its bitter-green aromatic intensity. Processing is a drying question: the strong, penetrating note fades with age, so a well-dried, recently gathered herb is the useful grade, and freshness is the practical concern.

As a leafy aromatic, rue is handled by drying rather than tapping or distilling. The dried herb — gathered and dried to preserve its bitter-green intensity — is the material of use. The practical concern is freshness: the strong, penetrating note fades with age, so a recently gathered, well-dried herb is the useful grade, and a faded batch has lost the intensity that is the whole point of the ingredient. This contrasts with resin materials, which harden and hold their character for far longer.

## What determines rue quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on intensity and freshness — a clean, well-dried rue that retains its bitter, penetrating green character. The note fades with age, so a flat or musty batch is a spent batch. There is no standardized grading ladder; aroma intensity on gentle warming is the practical signal.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Intensity** — the bitter, penetrating green character is the value; a strong, sharp batch beats a faded one.
2. **Freshness** — the note fades with age and poor storage.
3. **Condition** — clean, well-dried herb, free of mold and excess stem.
4. **Identity** — rue (*Ruta graveolens*) rather than an unspecified "book herb" or a "兰"-named fragrant herb.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder for rue; aroma intensity on gentle warming is the practical check.
