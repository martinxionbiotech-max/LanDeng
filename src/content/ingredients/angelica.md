**Technical answer:** Angelica — *báizhǐ* (白芷) in Chinese — is the dried root of *Angelica dahurica* (family Apiaceae), an aromatic herb with a distinctive herbal, slightly sweet-bitter scent. It is a classic ingredient in Chinese 合香 (blended incense) and in traditional materia medica.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Angelica root has a warm, herbal, aromatic scent with a slightly sweet-bitter edge and a subtle spicy undertone. It is a "root" aroma — earthy and dry rather than floral — reading more like a seasoning than a flower.

Angelica root has a warm, herbal, aromatic scent with a slightly sweet-bitter edge and a subtle spicy undertone. It is a "root" aroma — earthy and dry rather than floral — and it reads more like a seasoning than a flower.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** 白芷 is *Angelica dahurica*, a stout perennial herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae). Flora of China records the plant as 1–2.5 m tall, with a cylindrical brown root 3–5 cm thick that is "strongly aromatic" — the aromatic root is the part used.

*Angelica dahurica* is a stout perennial of the Apiaceae (carrot family). Flora of China records the plant as **1–2.5 m tall**, with a **cylindrical, brown root 3–5 cm thick that is "strongly aromatic"** — a useful botanical anchor, because the aromatic root is exactly the material of value. It flowers July–August and fruits August–September. See [Angelica dahurica in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200015358).

The part used is the dried root — harvested, cleaned, dried, then sliced or ground. This is a **root** material, structurally distinct from the woods and resins it is often blended with.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 白芷 (báizhǐ) is the Chinese name for *Angelica dahurica*; the shared English name "angelica" also covers 当归 (dong quai, *Angelica sinensis*) and European angelica (*Angelica archangelica*) — same genus, different species, different uses.

The English name "angelica" hides a botanical split. Three distinct plants share it:

- **白芷 (báizhǐ)** — *Angelica dahurica*, the aromatic root of Chinese 合香. This entry.
- **当归 (dāngguī, dong quai)** — *Angelica sinensis*, the "women's herb" of materia medica, a different species. See [angelica-sinensis](/ingredients/angelica-sinensis/).
- **European angelica** — *Angelica archangelica*, used in European liqueurs and perfumery.

In an incense context, 白芷 (*A. dahurica*) is the relevant one.

## Where does angelica come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Angelica dahurica* is a north and east Asian plant; Flora of China records it in Hebei, Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, Shaanxi, and northern Taiwan, at forest margins, valley grasslands, and streamsides from 500–1,000 m, with a range into Japan, Korea, and Siberia.

Flora of China places *Angelica dahurica* in the forest margins, valley grasslands, and streamsides of northern and eastern China — Hebei, Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, Shaanxi, and northern Taiwan — at elevations of 500–1,000 m, extending into Japan, Korea, and Siberia. This is a cool-temperate aromatic root, not a tropical material, which is a useful distinction from the warm-climate resins and woods that dominate much of incense.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Angelica root (白芷) is used in Chinese 合香 as a classic supporting body note — its warm, herbal, earthy character adds depth and body beneath the woods and resins that anchor a formula, rather than leading with a bright or sweet top note.

白芷 is a supporting ingredient in blended incense. Its warm, herbal, earthy root character supplies **body and depth** — it grounds and fills out a formula from below, complementing the woods and resins rather than competing with them. This is the classic role of the aromatic roots in 合香: they give a blend its layered, herbal register, which is why 白芷 appears alongside other roots (菖蒲, 藿香, and others) as a standing member of the classical incense palette.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Angelica root is harvested, cleaned, dried, and then sliced or ground. No extraction step is involved in the root form; only an essential oil or extract would require distillation or solvent extraction.

The root is **harvested, cleaned, and dried**, then either sliced (for use as root slices) or **ground to powder** (for blending into incense). No extraction step is involved in the dried-root form. For powder buyers, particle size and moisture are the practical variables — but the exact target depends on the formula, binder, and production line (see Our Industry View below). The grinding and forming mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process.

## What determines angelica quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on the root's aromatic intensity, cleanliness, and freedom from rot — a fragrant, clean, well-dried root beats a stale or woody one. The aromatic compounds concentrate in the root, so a thick, sound, aromatic root is the benchmark.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Aromatic intensity** — the root should be fragrant, herbal, and sweet-bitter when warmed; this is the decisive variable.
2. **Cleanliness and soundness** — freedom from rot, mold, and foreign matter.
3. **Root thickness and maturity** — a sound, mature root (recalling the botanical "strongly aromatic" root) reads richer than thin or woody material.
4. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

There is **no standardized grading system**; the buyer evaluates the physical condition and aroma of the lot.

## Angelica vs dong quai (当归)

> **Direct answer:** 白芷 (*Angelica dahurica*) and 当归 (*Angelica sinensis*, dong quai) are the same genus, different species. 白芷 is the aromatic root of incense and blended fragrance; 当归 is the materia-medica "women's herb." The shared English name "angelica" hides the difference.

| | 白芷 (Angelica) | 当归 (Dong quai) |
|---|---|---|
| Botanical source | *Angelica dahurica* | *Angelica sinensis* |
| Chinese name | 白芷 (báizhǐ) | 当归 (dāngguī) |
| Role | Aromatic root of 合香 (incense) | Materia medica "women's herb"; sachet (香囊) root |
| Aroma | Warm, herbal, sweet-bitter, spicy | Warm, sweet, fuller, less sharp |

The two are complements, not substitutes, in the Chinese tradition. See [angelica-sinensis](/ingredients/angelica-sinensis/) and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The main risk is misidentification, not adulteration: "angelica" can mean 白芷, dong quai, or European angelica — three different species. The botanical name on the label resolves which is meant.

Angelica is not a heavily faked, expensive aromatic in the agarwood sense; its risk is the **shared common name**. A buyer who wants the warm, herbal 白芷 for incense should not unknowingly receive 当归 (dong quai) or European angelica — related plants with different aromas and roles. The botanical name on the label resolves the matter. See the [incense substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) for the general screening logic.

白芷 is a long-cultivated aromatic and medicinal root, and there is no CITES-style or wild-harvest scarcity concern of the kind attached to spikenard or agarwood. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Angelica root is used in traditional materia medica, in blended incense, and in perfumery as a warm herbal base note. Its commercial weight is led by materia medica and incense blending rather than any single flagship product.

Angelica reaches the market through distinct channels:

- **Materia medica** — 白芷 is a standard dried root of the Chinese herbal trade.
- **Blended incense** — 白芷 is a supporting body note in 合香 formulas.
- **Perfumery** — the warm, herbal root note is used in some fragrance blends.

The three channels value the root somewhat differently: the materia-medica trade prizes clean, intact root slices, while the incense buyer cares more about aroma on gentle heat and how the powder behaves in a formula.

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** Buyers should confirm the species (*Angelica dahurica*, not dong quai or European angelica), then judge the root's aroma, soundness, and cleanliness — and, for powder, test particle size and moisture against the formula.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

- **Species** — confirm *Angelica dahurica* (白芷) rather than *A. sinensis* (当归) or *A. archangelica*; a real seller names the species.
- **Aroma** — the root should be warm, herbal, and sweet-bitter when gently warmed.
- **Soundness and cleanliness** — freedom from rot, mold, and foreign matter.
- **Powder consistency** — confirm particle size and moisture, and test a sample against your formula.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, 白芷 is a supporting body note, not a lead. Its value is in grounding a blend, and that role depends on consistency — of aroma, particle size, moisture, and batch-to-batch variation — more than on any rare or premium grading.

From a manufacturing perspective, 白芷 is a **body-note ingredient whose value is consistency, not rarity**. It will not carry a formula the way sandalwood or agarwood can; its job is to deepen and ground a blend, which is exactly the role most vulnerable to batch-to-batch drift — a slightly stale or differently ground lot changes the blend's character without announcing itself.

Two points follow:

- **It is a supporting note, so consistency is the specification.** A formula that relies on 白芷 for body needs the *same* warm, herbal register every batch. A single fragrant lot proves little; what matters is that the next lot smells and behaves like the last.
- **Particle size and moisture affect behavior, not just aroma.** In powder, particle size changes how the material packs, draws air, and burns; moisture changes how it grinds and how the finished stick dries. The right target **depends on formulation** — the binder, the other ingredients, and the production line — so a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **consistent, well-documented 白芷 with a confirmed species and a steady aromatic profile**, used as a supporting body note — not a rare root, but one that must be reorderable to the same specification.

## Summary

Angelica (白芷, *báizhǐ*) is the dried, strongly aromatic root of *Angelica dahurica*, a stout Apiaceae herb of northern and eastern China. It is a classic supporting body note in Chinese 合香, valued for a warm, herbal, sweet-bitter root character that grounds a blend from below. Its quality rests on aromatic intensity, soundness, and consistency rather than a formal grade, and its naming risk is confusion with dong quai (当归, *Angelica sinensis*) — same genus, different species. In incense it is a supporting note, not a lead; commercially, species verification and batch consistency outweigh rarity.

It is an aromatic root rather than a wood, resin, or flower, which gives it its earthy, herbal character. In Chinese 合香 (blended incense), the dried root is typically powdered and combined with other aromatics rather than burned as a whole piece.
