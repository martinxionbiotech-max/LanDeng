> **Direct answer:** Banksia rose (木香花, mùxiānghuā) is the flower of *Rosa banksiae*, a climbing rose of the rose family (Rosaceae) native to central and western China. It is a sweet, honeyed, lightly green floral used in Chinese incense and recorded as a flower material in the classical compendium 香乘. Its name is easily confused with 木香 (costus), an entirely different aromatic root.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** *Rosa banksiae* is an evergreen climbing rose of the rose family (Rosaceae). Flora of China describes a shrub climbing to 6 m, with leaflets in 3s to 5s and small flowers 1.5–2.5 cm across in clusters of 4–15, white or yellow, flowering April–May. Notably, the double or semi-double forms are not fragrant, while the single-flowered forms may be fragrant — a botanical detail that bears directly on what makes "木香花" an incense material at all.

Flora of China describes *Rosa banksiae* precisely: **evergreen climbing shrubs to 6 m tall**, with old branches bearing large rigid prickles; leaves with 3–5 (rarely 7) leaflets, elliptic-ovate to oblong-lanceolate, 2–5 × 0.8–1.8 cm, leathery; flowers **4–15 in simple umbels or corymbs, 1.5–2.5 cm across**, petals 5, semi-double, or double, **white or yellow**, "fragrant or not"; hip orange or black-brown, globose or ovoid, 5–7 mm; flowering **April–May**, fruiting August–October. Crucially, Flora of China keys the forms by fragrance: **"flowers double or semi-double, not fragrant; flowers single, fragrant or not."** The double white and yellow forms — the ones most cultivated, including the classic yellow "Lady Banks' rose" — are **not fragrant**; only the single-flowered form may be. This is a botanical fact with direct commercial consequence for a material sold as an incense flower. See [Rosa banksiae in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200011215) and [Rosa banksiae on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/3004247).

## Where does banksia rose come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Rosa banksiae* is native to central and western China — Gansu, Guizhou, Henan, Hubei, Jiangsu, Sichuan, and Yunnan — growing in thickets, scrub, valleys, and stream sides at 500–2,200 m, and widely cultivated. It was later exported to Western gardens as the "Lady Banks' rose."

*Rosa banksiae* is native to central and western China — **Gansu, Guizhou, Henan, Hubei, Jiangsu, Sichuan, and Yunnan** — in thickets, scrub, valleys, stream sides, and roadsides at **500–2,200 m**, and is widely cultivated in China. It was later exported to Western gardens as the "Lady Banks' rose." Its native, wild, single-flowered forms are the ones that may be fragrant, while the cultivated double forms are the ornamental, scentless ones — a geographic-plus-form distinction that matters for anyone sourcing it as an aromatic.

## What determines banksia rose quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on two things at once: the flower form (single-flowered = potentially fragrant; double = scentless) and freshness of drying. A clean, well-dried, fragrant single-flowered material with a sweet, honeyed note is the good grade; musty or faded blooms are lower. The 木香花/木香 root distinction is the identity check.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Flower form** — single-flowered (fragrant) vs double (scentless); the single form is the aromatic material.
2. **Aroma** — sweet, honeyed, lightly green; a clean note beats a faded or musty one.
3. **Condition** — clean, well-dried blooms.
4. **Identity** — the flower (*Rosa banksiae*), not the root 木香 (*Saussurea costus*).

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder, but "single-flowered, clean, fresh, and correctly identified" is the durable signal.
