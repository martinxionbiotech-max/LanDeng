**Technical answer:** 甘草 — *gāncǎo* in Chinese — is the dried root and rhizome of *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, a leguminous perennial of the pea family (Fabaceae) native to northern China, Mongolia, and Siberia. It is a deeply sweet root — the glycyrrhizin it contains is many times sweeter than sugar — and in Chinese incense its role is not to be burned for a dominant scent but to act as a **sweetening, harmonizing binder** in 合香 (blended incense) formulas, where a small amount rounds and softens the other aromatics.

## Aroma profile

Licorice root is sweet and woody, with an earthy, slightly bitter undertone — more a deep, dry sweetness than a sugary one. Its defining trait is the intense sweetness of glycyrrhizin, which persists long after the root has dried. In incense it does not lead: it reads as a *softening, harmonizing note* that rounds sharp or pungent aromatics and lends a smooth, mellow finish. Burnt alone it is unremarkable; blended in small proportion it is a quiet workhorse of classical formulas.

## A role of sweetness, not scent (read this)

甘草 enters classical incense not for its own fragrance but for what it does to a formula. The Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) treats 甘草 as one of the routine preparation ingredients alongside 藿香 (agastache) and 零陵香 — its sections on material preparation instruct that ingredients "such as 藿香, 甘草, 零陵香" (凡藿香甘草零陵之類) be picked clean of stems and weeds, sun-dried, and crumbled before use. Formulas in the same text list 甘草末 (powdered licorice) in small measured amounts — often a few *qian* against *liang* of the principal aromatics — confirming its role as a minor sweetening addition, not a principal scent.

## Botanical source and origin

*Glycyrrhiza uralensis* is a leguminous perennial of the pea family (Fabaceae), native to the steppes and semi-arid regions of northern China, Mongolia, and Siberia. The part used is the dried root and rhizome, which concentrates glycyrrhizin — the compound responsible for the characteristic sweetness. It is one of several licorice species; *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* is the Chinese licorice, distinct from the Mediterranean *Glycyrrhiza glabra*, though the two share the sweetening principle.

## In Chinese tradition

- **合香 harmonizer:** 甘草 is a classical *sweetener and harmonizer* in blended incense, added in small amounts to round and soften sharper aromatics.
- **Materia medica:** 甘草 is one of the most-used herbs in traditional Chinese medicine, classically described for "harmonizing the hundred herbs" (调和诸药) — a *harmonizing* role that carries over into its incense use. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **Not a solo incense material:** 甘草 is not burned alone for fragrance; it appears in formulas as a supporting ingredient.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The sweetening and harmonizing use in incense is documented in the classical manuals. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Fabaceae placement, and the root's long use as a minor sweetening binder in 合香.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 甘草 is *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, the Chinese licorice; distinguish it from the Mediterranean *Glycyrrhiza glabra* — different species, same sweetening principle.
- Expect a deep, dry sweetness rather than a floral or spicy scent; the root is a supporting ingredient, not a principal aromatic.
- In incense it is used in small, measured amounts — a sweetening harmonizer, not a base to build a formula around.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.
