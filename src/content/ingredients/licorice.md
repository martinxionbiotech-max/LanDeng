> **Direct answer:** Licorice (甘草, gāncǎo) is the dried root and rhizome of *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, a leguminous perennial of the pea family (Fabaceae) native to northern China, Mongolia, and Siberia. It is a deeply sweet root — its glycyrrhizin is many times sweeter than sugar — and in Chinese incense its role is not to be burned for a dominant scent but to act as a sweetening, harmonizing binder in 合香 formulas.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Licorice root is sweet and woody with an earthy, slightly bitter undertone — a deep, dry sweetness from glycyrrhizin, not a sugary one. In incense it does not lead; it reads as a softening, harmonizing note that rounds sharp aromatics and lends a smooth, mellow finish.

Licorice root is sweet and woody, with an earthy, slightly bitter undertone — more a deep, dry sweetness than a sugary one. Its defining trait is the intense sweetness of glycyrrhizin, which persists long after the root has dried. In incense it does not lead: it reads as a *softening, harmonizing note* that rounds sharp or pungent aromatics and lends a smooth, mellow finish. Burnt alone it is unremarkable; blended in small proportion it is a quiet workhorse of classical formulas. The sweetness is the key — it is a *taste-like* sweetness carried into a fragrance context, which is why licorice functions as a blender rather than a scent.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 甘草 means "sweet herb" (甘 = sweet, 草 = herb), a literal description of its defining quality. The name encodes the root's role — it is valued for sweetness, not fragrance — which is exactly how it functions in incense: a sweetener, not a scent.

The name 甘草 (gāncǎo) is plain and descriptive: 甘 "sweet" + 草 "herb" — "the sweet herb." That literalness is itself informative. Unlike aromatic materials named for their scent (沉香 "sinking fragrance") or their origin (川芎 "Sichuan lovage"), licorice is named for its *taste*. This tells you its role before you ever smell it: licorice enters a formula for sweetness and harmonizing, not for aromatic leadership. The name is a functional label, and it maps directly onto how the root behaves in a blend.

## A role of sweetness, not scent (read this)

> **Direct answer:** 甘草 enters classical incense not for its own fragrance but for what it does to a formula — it sweetens and harmonizes. The Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) treats it as a routine preparation ingredient, added in small measured amounts (a few *qian* against *liang* of principal aromatics), confirming its role as a minor sweetening addition, not a principal scent.

甘草 enters classical incense not for its own fragrance but for what it does to a formula. The Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) treats 甘草 as one of the routine preparation ingredients alongside 藿香 (agastache) and 零陵香 — its sections on material preparation instruct that ingredients "such as 藿香, 甘草, 零陵香" (凡藿香甘草零陵之類) be picked clean of stems and weeds, sun-dried, and crumbled before use. Formulas in the same text list 甘草末 (powdered licorice) in small measured amounts — often a few *qian* against *liang* of the principal aromatics — confirming its role as a minor sweetening addition, not a principal scent.

This is a documented structural role: licorice is a *harmonizer*, dosed like a seasoning rather than a substance. The pattern is worth internalizing for buyers — a material listed in small amounts against large amounts of the principal aromatics is a supporting ingredient by definition.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Licorice is used in incense as a sweetening, harmonizing binder — added in small amounts to round and soften sharper aromatics and to lend a smooth, mellow finish. It is a structural ingredient whose function is to modify the blend, not to supply its own fragrance.

Licorice's role in 合香 is fundamentally *relational* rather than aromatic. It is added to sweeten and harmonize — to take the sharp edges off pungent or resinous materials and bind the composition into a smoother whole. This is the same "harmonizing" function it holds in materia medica, carried over into fragrance. It is not burned alone, and it is not a principal scent; it is the quiet ingredient that makes the louder ones work together. For how such harmonizing and blending logic operates across a formula, see [hexiang (合香) blending](/blog/hexiang-blending-system/).

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Licorice is processed by digging the root and rhizome, cleaning, and drying, then either sold whole/sliced or ground to powder (甘草末) for formula use. The classical manuals instruct that the dried material be picked clean of stems and weeds, sun-dried, and crumbled before use.

The processing is simple but was codified in the classical manuals: the root is dug, cleaned, and dried, then prepared for use. The *Xiangcheng* (香乘) specifies that 甘草 be picked clean of stems and weeds, sun-dried, and crumbled — a preparation note that confirms it was handled as a routine, minor ingredient rather than a precious one. For a modern formulator, the operative form is the powder (甘草末), and its particle size and moisture are the practical variables; as with any botanical powder, the correct target depends on the formula, binder, and line. ## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality in licorice turns on species (Chinese *G. uralensis* vs Mediterranean *G. glabra*), the depth of the dry sweetness (glycyrrhizin content), cleanliness, and dryness. It is a supporting ingredient, so consistency and correct species matter more than aromatic intensity.

For a sourcing buyer, the checks are:

1. **Species** — confirm *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* (Chinese licorice), not *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (Mediterranean); they share the sweetening principle but are different crops.
2. **Sweetness** — the deep, dry sweetness of glycyrrhizin should be evident; it is the material's defining trait.
3. **Cleanliness and dryness** — free of stems, weeds, soil, and excess moisture.
4. **Consistency** — because licorice is dosed in small amounts as a harmonizer, batch-to-batch consistency matters more than any single high note.

Licorice is a supporting ingredient, so the buyer's concern is less "is this the most fragrant root" and more "is this the right species, consistently prepared."

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Licorice is a clean illustration of a principle the encyclopedia stresses: in a blend, the ingredient whose role is relational — sweetening, harmonizing, rounding — is dosed like a seasoning, not a substance. Its value is in what it does to the other materials, not in its own aroma, which is why consistency beats intensity for this kind of supporting ingredient.

From a manufacturing perspective, licorice is a useful corrective to the assumption that an incense ingredient must be valued for its *own* fragrance. Three points follow:

- **Function over fragrance.** Licorice is the clearest example of a material whose job is to modify other materials. Its sweetness is a *taste-like* quality carried into a fragrance context, and its harmonizing role is relational — it makes pungent and resinous materials sit more smoothly together. A formulator reaches for licorice the way a cook reaches for a pinch of sugar: to balance, not to feature.
- **Dosing as a structural signal.** The classical manuals list 甘草末 in small *qian* against *liang* of the principal aromatics. That ratio is itself information: when a material is consistently dosed small against large, it is a supporting ingredient by definition, and a buyer should treat it as one — specify it for consistency, not for headline intensity.
- **Species is the real spec.** Because Chinese and Mediterranean licorice share both the English name and the sweetening compound, the meaningful commercial distinction is species. A buyer who says only "licorice" has not yet specified anything; a buyer who says *Glycyrrhiza uralensis* has. This is the same "pin the name" discipline the encyclopedia applies to every material with a shifting or shared name.

In our view, licorice's commercial reality is that of a **consistent, correctly-specied harmonizer** — a small-amount ingredient whose reliability comes from the right species and steady preparation, not from aromatic intensity. Its value is structural, and that is precisely why it is under-appreciated: the most useful blending ingredients are often the ones you barely smell.
