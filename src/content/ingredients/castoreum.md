**Technical answer:** 海狸香 — *hǎilíxiāng* in Chinese — is castoreum, the historical scent secretion of beavers (*Castor fiber* in Eurasia, *Castor canadensis* in North America), drawn from the castor sacs near the base of the tail. It is a **Western classical perfumery fixative, not a Chinese incense material**, and it carries an important caveat: the beaver is a protected species in much of its range, and modern "castoreum" is almost entirely a synthetic reconstruction. This page treats it strictly as historical context.

## Aroma profile

Castoreum is animalic and leathery, smoky and sweet, with a rich, musky-tobacco character — a deep, warm, "animal" note used in Western perfumery to add warmth and a leather-like tenacity to a fragrance. It is a fixative, not a lead scent: it anchors and deepens other notes rather than standing on its own. Its historical value was that warm, leathery base, now reproduced synthetically.

## Historical context only, and a protection caveat (read this)

海狸香 must be framed precisely:

- **Western classical, not Chinese:** castoreum is a European perfumery material, absent from the Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) and the Chinese incense tradition. Its Chinese name 海狸香 is a modern descriptive term, not a classical ingredient.
- **Historical use only:** castoreum was used in classical and 19th–20th-century Western perfumery as a fixative. It is **not** a contemporary or recommended material.
- **Protection:** the Eurasian beaver *Castor fiber* is a legally protected species across much of Europe and Asia, and its conservation status ranges from Near Threatened to Critically Endangered in some subspecies. Harvesting castoreum from protected beavers is not a legitimate modern practice.
- **Synthetic today:** virtually all "castoreum" used in modern fragrance and flavor is a synthetic reconstruction of the scent, not the animal secretion.

**This page describes 海狸香 = castoreum as historical context only.** It is documented for what it was, not recommended as a material, and we state the protection and synthetic-replacement facts plainly.

## Source and origin

Castoreum is a secretion from the castor sacs of beavers — the Eurasian beaver *Castor fiber* and the North American beaver *Castor canadensis* — used by the animals for scent-marking. In Western perfumery it was historically obtained by harvesting the sacs, a practice now largely obsolete given the beaver's protected status and the availability of synthetic substitutes. Its aromatic character — animalic, leathery, smoky, sweet — is what modern perfumers reproduce synthetically.

## In Western tradition (historical)

- **Perfumery fixative:** castoreum was a classical Western perfumery fixative, prized for its warm, leathery, animalic depth.
- **Not Chinese incense:** castoreum has no role in the Chinese incense tradition or the 香乘; its Chinese name is a modern descriptive term.
- **Synthetic reconstruction:** modern "castoreum" is a synthetic accord, not the animal material.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The historical Western perfumery use is documented. We make **no medical claims**, and we explicitly note that castoreum is historical context only, not a recommended material, given the beaver's protected status.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is 海狸香 (castoreum)?** The historical scent secretion of beavers — the Eurasian beaver *Castor fiber* and the North American beaver *Castor canadensis* — drawn from the castor sacs near the base of the tail. It was used in Western perfumery as a fixative, and its Chinese name is a modern descriptive term.

**Is castoreum a Chinese incense material?** No. Castoreum is a Western classical perfumery material, absent from the Ming-dynasty *Xiangcheng* (香乘) and the Chinese incense tradition. Its Chinese name 海狸香 ("beaver scent") is modern and descriptive, not a classical ingredient.

**What does castoreum smell like?** Animalic and leathery, smoky and sweet, with a rich, musky-tobacco character. It is a fixative rather than a lead scent — it anchors and deepens other notes with a warm, leathery base.

**Is castoreum still used today?** Only as a synthetic reconstruction. Virtually all modern "castoreum" in fragrance and flavor is a synthetic accord reproducing the animalic-leathery scent. The animal material is essentially obsolete, and the Eurasian beaver is a protected species in much of its range.

**Is the beaver protected?** Yes. The Eurasian beaver *Castor fiber* is legally protected across much of Europe and Asia, with a conservation status ranging from Near Threatened to Critically Endangered in some subspecies. This is why legitimate modern fragrance uses the synthetic, not the animal secretion.

**Is castoreum the same as musk or civet?** No, though all three are historical animal fixatives with warm, animalic depth. Castoreum comes from beavers, musk from the musk deer (*Moschus*), and civet from civets. Each has its own entry, and all three are now largely replaced by synthetic substitutes.
