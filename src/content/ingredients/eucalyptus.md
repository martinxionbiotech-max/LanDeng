> **Direct answer:** Eucalyptus (桉叶, ānyè) is here the leaf of *Eucalyptus globulus*, the blue gum, a tall evergreen tree of the myrtle family (Myrtaceae) native to Australia. Its leaves carry a sharp, camphoraceous, fresh, slightly medicinal scent from a eucalyptol-rich essential oil. It is a modern crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 桉叶 (eucalyptus) is sharp, clean, and camphoraceous, with a fresh, slightly medicinal, cooling character — the crisp, penetrating smell of crushed eucalyptus leaf. It is dominated by the compound eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), which gives it its distinctive fresh-clearing quality, and it reads as a clean, invigorating green note rather than a sweet one.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Eucalyptus globulus*, the blue gum or Tasmanian blue gum, is a large evergreen of the myrtle family (Myrtaceae), with smooth, exfoliating grayish-blue bark, young leaves opposite and sessile, and mature lanceolate, falcate leaves 15–30 × 1–2 cm. Flora of China records it as cultivated in Fujian, Guangxi, Guizhou, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, Yunnan, and Zhejiang — native to southeastern Australia and Tasmania.

*Eucalyptus globulus*, the blue gum or Tasmanian blue gum, is a large evergreen tree of the myrtle family (Myrtaceae), native to southeastern Australia and Tasmania. Flora of China records it as a **large tree with smooth, exfoliating grayish-blue bark**, **young leaves opposite and sessile, ovate, glaucescent**, and **mature leaves lanceolate, 15–30 × 1–2 cm, falcate, leathery**, with both surfaces glandular. It is recorded as **cultivated in Fujian, Guangxi, Guizhou, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Taiwan, Yunnan, and Zhejiang**, native to southeastern Australia and Tasmania. The fragrant part is the leaf, whose oil glands hold the volatile essential oil. It is one of many *Eucalyptus* species; *E. globulus* is the classic "eucalyptus" of aromatherapy. See [Eucalyptus globulus in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200014782).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 桉叶 (ānyè) means "eucalyptus leaf" — 桉 "eucalyptus" + 叶 "leaf" — while the tree is 蓝桉 (lán'ān, "blue gum"). The name is modern and part-specific: it names the leaf, the fragrant part, rather than the tree's timber. This matters because eucalyptus is grown in China chiefly for timber, and the leaf is a secondary aromatic use.

The Chinese terminology is modern and part-precise: 桉 (ān) is the eucalyptus (the genus *Eucalyptus*), and 叶 (yè) is the leaf, so 桉叶 names **the leaf specifically**. The tree itself is 蓝桉 (lán'ān), "blue gum," after its blue-gray bark. This part-precision is the practical point: eucalyptus in China is grown primarily as **plantation timber**, and the fragrant leaf — the aromatherapy material — is a secondary use. A buyer who specifies 桉叶 is asking for the leaf (and its oil), not the wood, and should confirm which *Eucalyptus* species is meant, since the genus is large and the leaf oils differ by species.

## The camphoraceous family (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Eucalyptus belongs to a "camphoraceous" chemistry family it shares with camphor (樟脑) and borneol (龙脑) — sharp, cool, medicinal-fresh notes. But they are different plants with different sources: eucalyptus is a eucalyptol-bearing Australian leaf, camphor a distilled crystal from the camphor laurel, borneol a crystal from *Dryobalanops* or *Blumea*. The shared adjective hides a real botanical split.

The word "camphoraceous" names a *chemistry family*, not a plant, and it hides a real split:

- **桉叶 (eucalyptus)** — *Eucalyptus globulus*, an Australian leaf whose note is dominated by **eucalyptol (1,8-cineole)**. This entry.
- **樟脑 (camphor)** — *Cinnamomum camphora*, the camphor laurel, whose **camphor crystal** is steam-distilled from the wood.
- **龙脑 (borneol)** — *Dryobalanops aromatica* or *Blumea balsamifera*, a **borneol crystal** from a different plant.

All three read "camphoraceous," but they are different materials with different sources and chemistries. The shared adjective is a false-friend, exactly like the camphor-vs-borneol confusion within the camphor entry itself. ## How is eucalyptus used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Eucalyptus is not a classical incense material, so in incense it appears only as a modern fragrance note — usually an essential oil or an accord reproducing the fresh, camphoraceous character — rather than as a burned leaf. Its sharp, clean lift is a top note and a blending accent, not a base.

Because eucalyptus has no classical Chinese incense role, its incense use is entirely modern and borrowed from aromatherapy. Where it does appear, it is as a **clean, camphoraceous top note**, typically delivered as an essential oil or a fragrance accord rather than as dried leaf to be burned. Its eucalyptol-fresh character lifts and clears heavier blends — a role closer to camphor than to a floral or sweet note. The practical point for a formulator is that eucalyptus's charm is volatile and top-note in nature: it brightens a blend but does not anchor it, and it is easily lost under heat or buried by heavier materials.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Eucalyptus is processed by harvesting the leaf and steam-distilling its essential oil; the leaf itself can also be dried. The oil is the commercial aromatic product, and the leaf's high eucalyptol content is what makes it a potent, single-note oil rather than a subtle one.

The processing of eucalyptus is oil-oriented:

1. **Harvest** — the leaves are harvested.
2. **Distillation** — the leaves are steam-distilled to recover the eucalyptol-rich essential oil.
3. **Dried leaf (optional)** — the leaf can also be dried for direct use.

The essential oil is the commercial aromatic product, and because the leaf is strongly eucalyptol-dominant, it is a **potent, single-note oil** rather than a subtle or complex one — which is why it is dosed sparingly and blended rather than used neat.

## Eucalyptus vs camphor vs borneol

> **Direct answer:** These are three "camphoraceous" aromatics from different plants. Eucalyptus is a eucalyptol-rich Australian leaf; camphor (樟脑) is a distilled crystal from the camphor laurel; borneol (龙脑) is a crystal from *Dryobalanops* or *Blumea*. They share a cool, sharp character but are distinct materials with distinct sources.
