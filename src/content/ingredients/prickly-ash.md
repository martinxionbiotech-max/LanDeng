> **Direct answer:** Prickly ash (蜀椒, shǔjiāo) is the dried fruit husk of *Zanthoxylum bungeanum*, a prickly-ash tree of the rue family (Rutaceae) native to central and western China. It is the source of Sichuan pepper (花椒), with a citrusy, woody aroma and the distinctive numbing sensation of hydroxy-α-sanshool. In classical Chinese incense it is the "椒" that the *Xiangcheng* (香乘) counts among the earliest aromatics of Chinese antiquity.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 蜀椒 is spicy and citrusy with a woody undertone and a distinctive numbing, tingling quality — the trademark of the *Zanthoxylum* genus, which sets it apart from true pepper. The aroma is bright and warm rather than hot, and the numbing sensation is a mouth-feel, not a scent.

蜀椒 is spicy and citrusy with a woody undertone and a distinctive numbing, tingling quality — the trademark of the *Zanthoxylum* genus, which sets it apart from true pepper. The aroma is bright and warm rather than hot, and the numbing sensation is a mouth-feel, not a scent; in incense the husk contributes a warm, citrus-spicy lift. It is one of the oldest aromatics recorded in Chinese culture.

The numbing quality is the defining trait and the one that most needs pinning down: hydroxy-α-sanshool, the compound behind it, produces a tingling, vibrating sensation on the tongue — a *mouth-feel*, not a heat and not a scent. This is why 蜀椒 cannot be reduced to "hot": its spice character is bright, citrusy, and numbing rather than burning, and this register is unique to the *Zanthoxylum* genus and entirely absent from true pepper (*Piper nigrum*).

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Zanthoxylum bungeanum* is a prickly-ash tree or shrub of the rue family (Rutaceae), native to central and western China and long cultivated there. The part used is the dried fruit husk, which carries the aromatic oil and the numbing hydroxy-α-sanshool. It is the classical 蜀椒 and the principal source of 花椒 (Sichuan pepper).

*Zanthoxylum bungeanum* is a prickly-ash tree or shrub of the rue family (Rutaceae), native to central and western China and long cultivated there. The part used is the dried fruit husk, which carries the aromatic oil and the numbing hydroxy-α-sanshool. Flora of China records it as a deciduous tree 3–7 m tall, with prickles on the stems and branchlets and purplish-red fruit follicles — the "prickly ash" of the English common name. It is the classical 蜀椒, and the broader 花椒 (Sichuan pepper) of cooking is largely this species. Note the distinction from true pepper (*Piper nigrum*), which belongs to a different family (Piperaceae).

The everyday culinary name 花椒 ("flower pepper") records the fruit husk's flower-like fragrance. The single character 椒 is the classical term, and it refers to this native tree, not to the imported black pepper (胡椒).

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** 蜀椒 is produced by harvesting the ripe fruit husks and drying them; the seeds are usually sifted out, leaving the aromatic husk. Drying concentrates the citrus-spicy oil, and the husk is the part of value — the seeds contribute little aroma and are typically removed.

The ripe fruit husks are harvested and dried, producing the reddish-brown husk that is the traded form. This husk-vs-seed distinction is a practical point: the "Sichuan pepper" of commerce is the husk, not the whole fruit.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** 蜀椒 quality turns on the freshness and intensity of its citrus-spicy aroma and its numbing character. Good husks are bright, reddish, and strongly citrus-spicy with a clean numbing quality; dull, dark, or stale husks have lost their volatile oil and their bite.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **aroma intensity** (a bright, fresh, citrus-spicy scent, not a flat or stale note), **numbing character** (a clean, strong tingling quality from hydroxy-α-sanshool), and **color/freshness** (bright, reddish husks rather than dull, dark, or crumbled ones). The volatile oils and the numbing compound both degrade with age, so freshness is the primary quality driver — a stale Sichuan pepper has lost both its citrus lift and its numbing bite.
