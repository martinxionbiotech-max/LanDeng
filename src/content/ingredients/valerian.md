> **Direct answer:** Valerian (缬草, xiécǎo) is the root of *Valeriana officinalis*, a perennial herb of the valerian family native to Eurasia, including China. Its dried root has a strong, earthy, musky, woody scent, and it is a classical European herbal root and a modern perfumery base note. It is a native Chinese species recorded in Flora of China — but not a classical *Bencao Gangmu* herb.

## Aroma profile

缬草 has a deep, earthy, musky, woody-root scent with a slightly animalic, "wet forest-floor" edge — the dense, tenacious smell of a cured root rather than a flower or leaf. In perfumery it works as a fixative and a dark base note, anchoring lighter florals and green notes. It is heavier and more rooty-musty than the soft, sweet-spicy spikenard (甘松, *Nardostachys jatamansi*).

## A provenance note (read this)

缬草 (valerian) sits in an unusual position — **native to China, but not a classical 香材**:

- *Valeriana officinalis* is a native Chinese species; Flora of China records it under 缬草, and it grows across Eurasia.
- Its classical materia-medica record in China is thin: a search of the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) and its supplement (本草纲目拾遗) did not turn up 缬草 as a named entry. The plant's documented Chinese materia-medica use is largely **modern**, not classical.
- Its stronghold as a fragrance and herbal root is **European** — valerian is a Western herbal and perfumery root, not a member of the classical Chinese 香材 canon.

This page records it honestly: a native species with a real Chinese name, but **no classical Chinese incense or materia-medica origin is claimed.**

## Botanical source and origin

*Valeriana officinalis* is a tall perennial herb of the valerian family, native to Europe and temperate Asia and widespread in China; Flora of China records the species (缬草). The part used is the dried root and rhizome, which develop the characteristic earthy-musky scent as they cure and dry. The same genus (*Valeriana*) holds many species, and the name 缬草 is the Flora of China Chinese name for *V. officinalis*.

## In modern use

- **Perfumery:** valerian root is a dark, earthy, musky base note and a natural fixative in some modern fragrances.
- **Herbalism:** valerian root is a long-standing European herbal; in China its materia-medica use is modern rather than classical.
- **Not classical Chinese incense:** valerian is absent from the 香材 canon; any Chinese use is a modern crossover.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Valerian has **no classical Chinese incense record**, and its Chinese materia-medica documentation is modern rather than part of the 本草纲目 canon. We make **no medical claims**. The documented facts are botanical identity, the Valerianaceae placement, and its modern perfumery and herbal role.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 缬草 (valerian) is the dried root of *Valeriana officinalis*; expect a strong, earthy, musky, woody scent.
- It is a heavy base note, best blended rather than burned alone; the cured root smells earthier than the fresh one.
- Distinguish it from spikenard (甘松, *Nardostachys jatamansi*) and vetiver (*Chrysopogon zizanioides*) — all earthy roots, but different species and scents.

## FAQ

**What is 缬草 (valerian)?** The dried root of *Valeriana officinalis*, a perennial herb of the valerian family native to Eurasia and China. It has a strong, earthy, musky, woody-root scent and is a European herbal root and modern perfumery base note, not a classical Chinese 香材.

**Is 缬草 a classical Chinese incense ingredient?** No. Although *Valeriana officinalis* is a native Chinese species recorded in Flora of China, it does not appear as a named entry in the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) or its supplement. Its documented Chinese use is modern, and its stronghold as a fragrance and herbal root is European.

**What does valerian smell like?** Deep, earthy, musky, and woody-root, with a slightly animalic, wet-forest-floor edge. It is a heavy, tenacious base note — more rooty and musty than the soft, sweet-spicy spikenard (甘松).

**Is valerian the same as spikenard?** No. Valerian is *Valeriana officinalis* (Valerianaceae); spikenard is *Nardostachys jatamansi* (Caprifoliaceae). Both are earthy roots, but they are different species with different scents, and spikenard has a genuine classical Chinese record while valerian does not.

**How is valerian used in fragrance?** As a dark, earthy, musky base note and a natural fixative in some modern perfumes, usually in small amounts to anchor lighter notes. It is a modern crossover into fragrance, not a classical incense ingredient.
