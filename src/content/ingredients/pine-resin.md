# Pine Resin (松香 / Song Xiang)

**Direct answer:** Pine resin — *sōngxiāng* (松香) in Chinese — is the hardened oleoresin of pine trees (*Pinus* spp.), the same material that, refined, is known as **rosin** or **colophony**. It has a fresh, piney, woody-balsamic aroma and is used in incense, varnish, and as a traditional aromatic and adhesive.

## Aroma profile

Pine resin has a fresh, forest-like pine aroma over a warm, woody-balsamic base — brighter and more "green" than the sweet resins like benzoin, and less deep than agarwood. It is the scent of conifer woods, and it reads clean and outdoorsy rather than heavy.

## What it is (rosin vs raw resin)

Pine resin is the sticky oleoresin that exudes when pine bark is cut. It can be used raw, or refined:

- **Raw resin** — the hardened, semi-translucent gum as collected.
- **Rosin / colophony** — the refined solid left after the volatile turpentine fraction is distilled off. It is the familiar amber solid used in violin bows, varnish, and some incense.

In incense, the term "pine resin" usually points at the raw aromatic gum; "rosin" points at the refined solid. The two are stages of the same material.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Incense:** pine resin is a traditional aromatic in Chinese 合香, adding a fresh, woody-resinous note.
- **Practical uses:** rosin has long been used as an adhesive, a sealant, and in varnishes and fluxes — a workhorse material beyond fragrance.
- **Symbolism:** pine (松) carries strong cultural associations with longevity and steadfastness in Chinese tradition.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Pine resin appears in traditional uses across cultures. We describe its aromatic and practical roles; we do **not** make medical claims. Refined rosin is a common industrial material, which is a matter of chemistry, not health benefit.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter; burn in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling concentrated smoke. Pine resin is flammable — store it away from flame and heat. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**Is pine resin the same as rosin?** Rosin (colophony) is refined pine resin — the solid left after the volatile turpentine fraction is removed. They are stages of the same oleoresin, not different materials.

**Does pine resin smell like pine needles?** Related but not identical — pine resin is woody-balsamic and resinous, while "pine needle" scent is greener and more conifer-fresh. The resin is the base note; the needles give the brighter top.

## People Also Ask (Long-Tail)

**What is pine resin used for in incense?** It adds a fresh, woody-balsamic, conifer note to blends, and is also used as a traditional binder/adhesive in some preparations. It is usually a supporting note, not a solo scent.

**Is pine resin the same as frankincense?** No. Pine resin comes from *Pinus* trees (Pinaceae) and is piney and woody-balsamic; frankincense comes from *Boswellia* trees (Burseraceae) and is citrus-resinous. Different plants, different resins.

**How do you burn pine resin?** Like other resins — on a lit charcoal disc (in sand or ash on a heatproof surface) or more gently on an electric incense heater. Use small pieces; it melts and releases fragrant smoke.
