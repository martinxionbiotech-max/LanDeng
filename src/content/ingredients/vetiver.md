# Vetiver (岩兰草 / Yan Lan Cao)

**Definition:** Vetiver — *yánláncǎo* (岩兰草) in Chinese — is a tropical grass whose roots yield a deep, earthy, woody, slightly smoky essential oil, used primarily as a base note in perfumery and incense.

## Key facts

| | |
|---|---|
| Chinese | 岩兰草 (yánláncǎo) |

## Aroma profile

Vetiver has a deep, earthy, "rooty" aroma with woody and slightly smoky warmth. It is a classic **base note** — heavy and long-lasting — used to anchor lighter notes in a blend. It is sometimes called "oil of tranquillity" for its grounding character (an aromatherapy description, not a medical claim).

The scent is genuinely "underground": damp earth, dry roots, and a faint smoky-green sweetness. It is the reference "earthy" note, darker than patchouli's musty-spice and smoother than cedar's dry wood. Because it is tenacious, a small amount of vetiver can carry a blend for hours.

## Botanical note

Vetiver is a grass (family Poaceae). The name has been revised from *Vetiveria zizanioides* to *Chrysopogon zizanioides*. It is also known as "khus" in India. Unlike woods or resins, the fragrant part is the **root**, steam-distilled.

The roots are famously deep and dense (they are also used for erosion control and woven into screens and mats — a separate, non-fragrance use). The essential oil is distilled from the washed, dried roots.

## Origins & grades

Vetiver oil is produced across the tropics, and origin meaningfully changes the aroma:

- **India (khus)** — the classic, earthy-woody profile; the historical reference.
- **Haiti** — lighter, cleaner, often described as more floral/sweet.
- **Java (Indonesia)** — smoky, dry, and woody.
- **Réunion (Bourbon)** — prized, floral-earthy; historically the benchmark for quality.

The origin note matters because "vetiver" on a label can be several different scent profiles. In incense, the earthy Indian/Java types are the most common.

## In incense & perfumery

Vetiver is almost always a **base note**: it sits under brighter top notes and gives a blend its depth and staying power. In incense it is usually paired with sandalwood or cedar (woody warmth) or brightened with citrus. It is rarely burned alone — its job is to anchor, not to lead.

## Vetiver vs patchouli

Both are earthy base notes, but they differ:

| | Vetiver (岩兰草) | Patchouli (广藿香) |
|---|---|---|
| Plant part | Grass root | Mint-family leaf |
| Character | Earthy, smoky-green, smooth | Earthy, musty-sweet, spicy |
| Role | Grounding base | Anchoring base |

Vetiver is smoother and "drier"; patchouli is mustier and spicier. They are often used together to build a deep, earthy base under woods and florals.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

Vetiver is described in aromatherapy as "calming" and "grounding." We treat these as **aromatic/experiential descriptions, not medical claims.** We do not claim vetiver treats any condition.

## Safety

Incense/essential-oil combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke.

## People Also Ask (Long-Tail)

**What does vetiver smell like?** Deep, earthy, "rooty," and woody with slightly smoky warmth — a heavy base note, sometimes called "oil of tranquillity" (an aromatherapy description, not a medical claim). The scent reads as damp earth and dry roots with a faint smoky-green sweetness, darker than patchouli and smoother than cedar. Because it is tenacious, a small amount of vetiver can carry an entire blend for hours.

**Where does vetiver come from?** Major producing regions include India, Haiti, and Indonesia (Java). It is a tropical grass whose roots are steam-distilled. The origin meaningfully changes the aroma — Indian (khus) vetiver is the classic earthy-woody profile, Haitian is lighter and cleaner, and Java vetiver is smokier and drier. So "vetiver" on a label can be several different scent profiles depending on where the grass was grown and distilled.

**What does vetiver pair with?** It anchors heavier blends — classically with sandalwood, cedar, and citrus. It works as a base note rather than a solo star. Vetiver sits under brighter top notes and gives a blend its depth and staying power, so it is almost never burned alone. Its earthy warmth pairs naturally with woods and is often brightened with a citrus top note to lift the heavier base.

**Is vetiver a wood?** No — it is a grass (Poaceae); the fragrant part is the steam-distilled root. The roots are famously deep and dense — they are also used for erosion control and woven into screens and mats — but in fragrance, only the steam-distilled root oil matters. This is a key botanical distinction: vetiver's woody, earthy aroma comes from a grass root, not from any tree or wood.

**Does vetiver oil differ by origin?** Yes — Indian (khus) vetiver is the classic earthy-woody profile, Haitian is lighter and cleaner, and Java vetiver is smokier and drier. Origin changes the aroma meaningfully, so "vetiver" on a label can be several different scent profiles; in incense, the earthy Indian and Java types are the most common. A fourth, Réunion (Bourbon) vetiver, is prized for its floral-earthy quality and is historically the benchmark.

**What is khus?** The Indian name for vetiver. Khus is used in Indian perfumery and in cooling drinks and sherbets in South Asia — a separate, culinary use of the same grass. The word is common on Indian vetiver products and simply refers to the same *Chrysopogon zizanioides* plant. In Indian perfumery, khus oil is the classic earthy-woody reference profile, and khus sherbet is a well-known summer drink.
