**Definition:** Vetiver — *yánláncǎo* (岩兰草) in Chinese — is a tropical grass whose roots yield a deep, earthy, woody, slightly smoky essential oil, used primarily as a base note in perfumery and incense. The fragrant part is the **root**, steam-distilled — not a wood, and not the grass's leaves.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver has a deep, earthy, "rooty" aroma with woody and slightly smoky warmth — the reference "earthy" note, darker than patchouli and smoother than cedar. It is a heavy, long-lasting base note used to anchor lighter notes.

Vetiver has a deep, earthy, "rooty" aroma with woody and slightly smoky warmth. It is a classic **base note** — heavy and long-lasting — used to anchor lighter notes in a blend. It is sometimes called "oil of tranquillity" for its grounding character (an aromatherapy description, not a medical claim).

The scent is genuinely "underground": damp earth, dry roots, and a faint smoky-green sweetness. It is the reference "earthy" note, darker than patchouli's musty-spice and smoother than cedar's dry wood. Because it is tenacious, a small amount of vetiver can carry a blend for hours.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver is a grass in the family Poaceae. The accepted name is *Chrysopogon zizanioides* (the older name *Vetiveria zizanioides* is a synonym), and the fragrant part is the deep, dense root, steam-distilled — unlike woods or resins, which use heartwood or exudate.

Vetiver is a grass (family Poaceae). The name has been revised from *Vetiveria zizanioides* to *Chrysopogon zizanioides*. It is also known as "khus" in India. Unlike woods or resins, the fragrant part is the **root**, steam-distilled. See [*Chrysopogon zizanioides* in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=242312768).

The roots are famously deep and dense (they are also used for erosion control and woven into screens and mats — a separate, non-fragrance use). The essential oil is distilled from the washed, dried roots.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 岩兰草 (yánláncǎo) is the Chinese name for vetiver, and "khus" is the Indian name for the same grass. Both point to *Chrysopogon zizanioides*; the names vary by region, but the material is the same root-distilled grass.

- **岩兰草 (yánláncǎo)** — the Chinese name, where 草 marks it as a grass rather than a wood or resin.
- **Khus (खस)** — the Indian name, common on Indian vetiver products and in South Asian perfumery and cooling drinks.
- **Vetiver** — the international trade name, from the Tamil word for the grass.

The name alone does not identify the origin or grade; "vetiver" on a label can be several different scent profiles.

## Where does vetiver come from?

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver is a tropical grass native to India and now cultivated across the tropics. The major producing regions are India, Haiti, and Indonesia (Java), with Réunion (Bourbon) historically the quality benchmark — and origin meaningfully changes the aroma.

Vetiver is a tropical grass native to India, now planted across the tropics for its root and for erosion control. The oil is produced across these regions, and origin meaningfully changes the aroma:

- **India (khus)** — the classic, earthy-woody profile; the historical reference.
- **Haiti** — lighter, cleaner, often described as more floral/sweet.
- **Java (Indonesia)** — smoky, dry, and woody.
- **Réunion (Bourbon)** — prized, floral-earthy; historically the benchmark for quality.

The origin note matters because "vetiver" on a label can be several different scent profiles. In incense, the earthy Indian/Java types are the most common.

## Why is vetiver used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver is used in incense as a base note — it sits under brighter top notes and gives a blend its depth and staying power. It is almost always blended (with sandalwood, cedar, or citrus) rather than burned alone.

Vetiver is almost always a **base note**: it sits under brighter top notes and gives a blend its depth and staying power. In incense it is usually paired with sandalwood or cedar (woody warmth) or brightened with citrus. It is rarely burned alone — its job is to anchor, not to lead. ## How is vetiver processed?

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver oil is made by washing and drying the grass's deep roots, then steam-distilling them. The root is the fragrant part; the grass's leaves are not used for the oil.

The processing is root-first:

- **Harvest** — the deep, dense root system is dug up; this is labor-intensive because the roots run deep.
- **Wash and dry** — the roots are washed and dried before distillation.
- **Distill** — the dried roots are steam-distilled to yield the deep, earthy essential oil.

The same roots have a separate, non-fragrance life — woven into screens, mats, and used for erosion control — but the fragrance material is the distilled root.

## What determines vetiver quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is driven chiefly by origin, which sets the aroma profile — Indian (khus) is earthy-woody, Haitian is lighter and cleaner, Java is smoky and dry, and Réunion (Bourbon) is the prized floral-earthy benchmark. Root quality and distillation care also matter.

The quality factors:

1. **Origin** — the single biggest driver; different regions yield different profiles.
2. **Root quality** — mature, clean, well-dried roots.
3. **Distillation** — careful distillation preserves the deep, earthy character.
4. **Aroma balance** — a full, warm, earthy profile with no thin or harsh off-notes.

These are trade distinctions, not a single standardized grading system.

## Common forms

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver is sold chiefly as essential oil, and to a lesser extent as dried root (whole or for sachets and screens). The oil is the perfumery and incense-blending form; the dried root has fragrance and non-fragrance uses.

| Form | Typical use |
|---|---|
| Essential oil | Perfumery base note; incense blending (the dominant form) |
| Dried roots | Woven into screens/mats; sachets; erosion control |
| Powder | Ground root (less common than the oil) |

The distinction is functional: the oil is a concentrated base note, while the dried root is a bulk botanical with both fragrance and non-fragrance uses. See [incense powder & resin](/blog/incense-powder-resin/).

## Vetiver vs patchouli

> **Direct answer:** Both are earthy base notes, but vetiver is a grass root and patchouli a mint-family leaf. Vetiver is smokier and smoother; patchouli is mustier and spicier. They are often used together to build a deep, earthy base.

| | Vetiver (岩兰草) | Patchouli (广藿香) |
|---|---|---|
| Plant part | Grass root | Mint-family leaf |
| Character | Earthy, smoky-green, smooth | Earthy, musty-sweet, spicy |
| Role | Grounding base | Anchoring base |

Vetiver is smoother and "drier"; patchouli is mustier and spicier. They are often used together to build a deep, earthy base under woods and florals. See [patchouli](/ingredients/patchouli/) and the [material comparison matrix](/blog/incense-material-comparison-matrix/).

Vetiver is planted, not wild-harvested, and its deep root system makes it a recognized erosion-control and soil-conservation crop — a genuine sustainability benefit that runs alongside its fragrance use. For a buyer, the practical question is **which origin**, because the label "vetiver" can be several different scent profiles depending on where the grass was grown and distilled. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Vetiver oil is a major perfumery base note and fixative, and it also goes into incense blends and scented products. Separately, the dried root is used in erosion control, screens, and mats — a non-fragrance use of the same grass.

- **Perfumery** — vetiver oil is a staple base note and fixative across fragrance families.
- **Incense** — the oil is blended into sticks and cones as a grounding base.
- **Culinary (regional)** — khus roots flavor cooling drinks and sherbets in South Asia.
- **Non-fragrance** — the grass's roots are used for erosion control and woven into screens and mats.

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** Buyers should ask for the origin, since it sets the aroma — Indian (khus) earthy-woody, Haitian lighter, Java smoky, Réunion prized — and confirm the form (oil vs dried root). A sample against the formula matters more than a generic "vetiver" label.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

- **Origin** — India, Haiti, Java, or Réunion; each is a different profile, and a real seller names it.
- **Form** — essential oil versus dried root; they are not interchangeable.
- **Aroma** — a full, earthy, woody profile with no thin or harsh off-notes.
- **Consistency** — because origin shifts the scent, a repeatable source matters more than a one-off lot.

## Safety

Incense/essential-oil combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space; avoid inhaling concentrated smoke.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, vetiver's value is a reliable, grounding base note — and the working variable is origin, because "vetiver" is several different scent profiles, not one. A formula built on one origin will not reproduce if the source switches silently.

Vetiver's commercial logic is **origin-first**: the name hides several different aromas, and which one a formula is built on matters more than the word "vetiver."

- **Origin is the identity.** Indian khus, Haitian, and Java vetiver will read differently in the same blend, so a formula built on one will not smell the same if the source switches. The buyer's first question is not "is this vetiver" but "which vetiver."
- **It is a base, not a lead.** Vetiver's job is to anchor and extend, so it is used in a supporting proportion. The right dose **depends on formulation** — what it is meant to ground and how long the dry-down should last — so no single percentage transfers between recipes.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **named origin, bought consistently**, used as a grounding base rather than a showcase note.

## Summary

Vetiver (岩兰草) is the steam-distilled root of the tropical grass *Chrysopogon zizanioides*, prized as a deep, earthy, woody, slightly smoky base note in perfumery and incense. Origin is the key quality variable — Indian (khus), Haitian, Java, and Réunion vetiver are different scent profiles — and the material is almost always blended as an anchor rather than burned alone. It is a cultivated, renewable crop whose roots also serve erosion control; "calming"/"grounding" descriptions are aromatic, not medical claims.
