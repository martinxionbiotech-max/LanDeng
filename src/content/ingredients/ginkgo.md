> **Direct answer:** Ginkgo (银杏, yínxìng) is the seed of *Ginkgo biloba*, a "living fossil" tree that is the sole surviving species of its division, native to China. The seed, called 白果 (báiguǒ, "white fruit"), is a mild, nutty, faintly bitter food seed. The *Bencao Gangmu* records it under 白果 and 鸭脚子, noting it entered tribute in the Song dynasty.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Ginkgo nut is nutty and mild, with a faint sweetness and a slightly bitter, starchy edge — a quiet, food-like scent closer to a roasted chestnut or bean than to any spice or flower. The scent is subtle and secondary to its culinary role, so it is eaten far more than it is used for fragrance.

Ginkgo nut is nutty and mild, with a faint sweetness and a slightly bitter, starchy edge — a quiet, food-like scent closer to a roasted chestnut or bean than to any spice or flower. The scent is subtle and secondary to its culinary role; the seed is eaten (roasted, boiled, or in savory and sweet dishes) far more than it is used for fragrance. In aromatics its value is negligible — a gentle food note, not a lead or supporting incense material. This page records it honestly as a food seed, not as a 香材.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Ginkgo biloba* is a deciduous tree to 40 m tall and the only living member of the division Ginkgophyta — a "living fossil" whose leaves turn bright yellow in autumn. Its seed is 2.5–3.5 × 1.6–2.2 cm, with a fleshy outer coat that has a rancid odor when ripe and a white inner shell. It is perhaps native to northwestern Zhejiang (Tianmu Shan) and now widely cultivated.

*Ginkgo biloba* is a deciduous tree and the only living member of the division Ginkgophyta — a "living fossil" whose lineage predates the flowering plants and once ranged worldwide. Flora of China records it as a **tree to 40 m tall, trunk to 4 m diameter**, with fan-shaped leaves that **turn bright yellow in autumn**, and a **seed 2.5–3.5 × 1.6–2.2 cm** whose outer coat (sarcotesta) is **yellow or orange-yellow with a rancid odor when ripe**, enclosing a **white shell (sclerotesta) with 2–3 longitudinal ridges**. It is recorded as **perhaps native in northwestern Zhejiang (Tianmu Shan)**, now widely and long cultivated across China and beyond. The seed (白果) is enclosed in that fleshy, malodorous outer coat, which is removed to reach the edible kernel. See [Ginkgo biloba in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200005235).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 银杏 (yínxìng, "silver apricot") is the tree's name; the seed is 白果 (báiguǒ, "white fruit"); and 鸭脚子 ("duck-foot seed") is an older name from the leaf's shape. The *Bencao Gangmu* explains the Song-dynasty tribute origin of 银杏. The seed and the leaf are different parts with different reputations.

The Chinese terminology separates the tree, the seed, and the leaf:

- **银杏 (yínxìng)** — "silver apricot," the tree's name, from the seed's silvery-white shell and apricot-like shape.
- **白果 (báiguǒ)** — "white fruit," the seed — the food and materia-medica part.
- **鸭脚子 (yājiǎozǐ)** — "duck-foot seed," an older name from the fan-shaped leaf.

The *Bencao Gangmu* records 银杏 as 白果 and 鸭脚子, explaining the Song-dynasty tribute origin of the name 银杏. A naming caution for modern buyers: the **seed (白果)** and the **leaf (银杏叶, *Ginkgo biloba* leaf)** are different parts with different uses and very different reputations — the leaf is famous for its extract, the seed is a food — and they should not be conflated.

## The "living fossil" framing (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Ginkgo is botanically famous — the sole survivor of its entire division, a lineage older than the flowering plants — but that fame is a botanical fact, not an aromatic one. Its seed is a food, not an incense material, and this page keeps the two claims separate: the fame is real, the incense role is not.

Ginkgo's botanical fame deserves a precise framing, because it is the most common source of inflation around this material:

- **The fame is botanical.** *Ginkgo biloba* is the only living member of the division Ginkgophyta, a lineage that predates the flowering plants and once ranged worldwide. Every other species in its group is extinct. That is a genuine, well-documented fact.
- **The incense role is not real.** Despite that fame, the ginkgo *seed* is a mild, food-like nut with negligible aromatic value, and it is not a classical 香乘 ingredient. The leaf's modern fame is for its extract, not for burning.

The point for this encyclopedia: **botanical celebrity and aromatic value are not the same thing.** Ginkgo is catalogued for its real identity and its real (culinary, materia-medica) record, with the incense absence stated plainly rather than inflated.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Food (Historical Record):** the seed 白果 is eaten roasted or boiled, in both sweet and savory dishes — the documented primary use.
- **Materia medica (Traditional Use):** 白果 appears in traditional Chinese medicine. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **A naming record (Historical Record):** the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) records 银杏 as 白果 and 鸭脚子, explaining the Song-dynasty tribute origin of the name 银杏 ("silver apricot").

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Ginkgo seed is processed by removing the fleshy, malodorous outer coat to reach the kernel, which is then roasted, boiled, or otherwise cooked. There is no incense-processing record; processing is culinary, and the outer coat must be handled for its rancid odor.

The processing of 白果 is culinary:

1. **Dehusk** — the fleshy, malodorous outer coat (sarcotesta) is removed to reach the white shell and kernel.
2. **Shell** — the hard white shell is cracked to extract the kernel.
3. **Cook** — the kernel is roasted, boiled, or otherwise cooked.

The practical point is that the **outer coat carries a rancid odor** (Flora of China notes it directly), which is why it is removed before use — a processing step that separates the edible kernel from the unpleasant husk. There is no classical incense-processing record.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is judged as a food seed — a clean, sound kernel, well-filled and properly husked, with a mild, nutty character. There is no incense-grading standard, because ginkgo is not an incense material.

The quality factors are culinary:

1. **Kernel soundness** — a clean, well-filled kernel without damage.
2. **Proper husking** — the malodorous outer coat fully removed.
3. **Freshness** — no rancidity or off-notes.

Because 白果 is a food seed, it is graded as food, not as an aromatic — the same honest positioning applied to torreya and other food seeds in this database.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## Ginkgo nut vs torreya vs cypress seed

> **Direct answer:** Three seeds sit in this database with three different roles: ginkgo (白果) is a Ginkgoaceae food seed; torreya (香榧) is a Taxaceae culinary nut; cypress seed (柏子) is a Cupressaceae seed that *is* a genuine incense material (柏子香). The seed category thus spans food and incense, and the family tells you which is which.

| Seed | Botanical source | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Ginkgo nut (白果) | *Ginkgo biloba* (Ginkgoaceae) | Food seed; 白果 in materia medica |
| Torreya (香榧) | *Torreya grandis* (Taxaceae) | Culinary nut; 榧实 in materia medica |
| Cypress seed (柏子) | *Platycladus orientalis* (Cupressaceae) | Incense seed of 柏子香 |

The seed category in this encyclopedia deliberately spans food and incense, and the **plant family is the key to the role**: the Ginkgoaceae and Taxaceae seeds are food, while the Cupressaceae seed 柏子 is the incense material.
