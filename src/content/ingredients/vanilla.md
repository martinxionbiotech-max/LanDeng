> **Direct answer:** Vanilla (香草荚, xiāngcǎo jiá) is the vanilla bean, the cured seed pod of the vanilla orchid, *Vanilla planifolia*, a climbing orchid (Orchidaceae) native to Mexico and Central America. Its sweet, creamy, warm scent develops only after the green pods are cured. Vanilla is a modern crossover into Chinese flavor and fragrance — it has no classical Chinese incense origin, and none is claimed.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla is sweet, creamy, and warm, with a soft, almost caramel gourmand character — the familiar scent of baked goods and fine fragrance. The aroma is not present in the green pod; it develops through curing, when enzymatic action releases vanillin. This Western-tropical profile is a modern addition to Chinese desserts, perfumery, and some incense, not a classical 香材.

## The curing story (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla's scent does not exist on the vine. The green pod is nearly scentless; it is the curing process — scalding, sweating, and drying over weeks — that releases vanillin through enzymatic action and produces the sweet, creamy aroma. This makes vanilla a manufactured aroma, not a harvested one.

Vanilla's defining fact is that **its aroma is produced, not harvested**:

- The green pod, when picked, is nearly scentless — vanillin is not present in its free form.
- **Curing** — a sequence of scalding (to stop the pod's growth), sweating (to trigger enzymatic breakdown of the precursor glucovanillin), and slow drying — releases vanillin and develops the sweet, creamy scent over weeks.
- The result is that vanilla's aroma is a **curing product**, closer in spirit to aged chenpi (陈皮) than to a raw flower or leaf.

This matters commercially: the difference between a good and a poor vanilla bean is largely a matter of **curing quality**, not just origin — the same principle that governs 陈皮's aging.

## Why is vanilla used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla appears in incense only as a modern, Western-style sweet base note — usually vanilla extract, absolute, or synthetic vanillin — not as a burned pod from the classical tradition. Its sweet, warm, gourmand character anchors and rounds modern blends, a role it shares with other sweet fixatives.

Vanilla's incense use is entirely modern and borrowed from perfumery. Where it appears, it is as a **sweet, warm, gourmand base note** — typically delivered as vanilla absolute, extract, or synthetic vanillin, not as a burned pod. Its role is to anchor and round: the sweet, creamy depth sits at the base of a blend, fixing and softening lighter notes, in the same way it functions in fine fragrance. This is a **Western-perfumery role**, not a classical Chinese one, and it is why vanilla is often grouped with the sweet balsamic and coumarin-noted fixatives — benzoin, peru balsam, and the coumarin-sweet 零陵香.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla is processed by harvesting the green pod and curing it — scalding, sweating, and drying over weeks — which releases vanillin. The cured pod is then used whole, or its flavor is extracted into alcohol or turned into an absolute. The aroma is a product of curing, not of the raw pod.

The processing of vanilla is the source of its aroma:

1. **Harvest** — the green, nearly scentless pods are picked.
2. **Scald** — the pods are scalded to stop growth.
3. **Sweat** — the pods are sweated, triggering the enzymatic release of vanillin from glucovanillin.
4. **Dry** — the pods are slowly dried over weeks.

From there the cured pod is used whole, or extracted (into alcohol for extract, or into an absolute for perfumery). The key point is that the aroma is a **curing product** — the green pod and the cured bean are almost different materials.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on origin, curing, and vanillin development — a good bean is moist, plump, and richly vanillin-scented, with a clean, sweet, creamy character and no off-notes. Curing quality is as decisive as origin, because the aroma is produced by curing.

The quality factors:

1. **Origin** — the classic benchmark origins (e.g., Madagascar "Bourbon" vanilla) carry a reputation for character.
2. **Curing** — the pod's vanillin development and moisture; a plump, fragrant, well-cured bean beats a dry or poorly cured one.
3. **Vanillin content** — the richer the vanillin (and related compounds), the deeper the sweet, creamy character.
4. **Condition** — freedom from mold, off-notes, and excessive dryness.

Because the aroma is a curing product, **curing quality rivals origin** as the decisive factor.

## Vanilla vs the sweet fixatives

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla, benzoin, peru balsam, and linglingxiang are all sweet, warm fixatives — but different materials with different sweet notes. Vanilla's sweetness is vanillin; benzoin's is a balsamic resin; peru balsam's is a rich balsam; linglingxiang's is a hay-like coumarin note. They share a "sweet base" role, not a chemistry.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Vanilla's lesson is that a scent can be a manufactured product, not a harvested one — the pod's aroma is created by curing, and its "sweet" character is a molecule (vanillin) that can be synthesized. For a buyer, this means origin is only half the spec: curing quality and the natural-versus-synthetic vanillin split are the real variables, and a modern crossover should be labeled a modern crossover.

From a sourcing perspective, vanilla carries two lessons that matter for any aromatic:

- **The aroma is manufactured, not harvested.** The green pod is nearly scentless; vanillin is released by curing. This makes vanilla closer in spirit to an *aged* product (like 陈皮) than to a raw flower — and it means curing quality is a first-order variable, not an afterthought.
- **The note is a molecule.** Vanilla's "sweet, creamy" character is vanillin, and vanillin can be synthesized cheaply. The natural-versus-synthetic split is therefore a real commercial question — the same pattern as camphor and borneol — and "vanilla" on a label does not say which one is meant.
- **The sweet fixative is a role, not an identity.** Vanilla shares the "sweet base note" function with benzoin, peru balsam, and the coumarin-sweet linglingxiang, but the sweet notes differ (vanillin vs coumarin vs balsam). A formulator chooses among them for a role, not for a single chemistry.

In our view, the useful takeaway is procedural: **specify the form and the natural-versus-synthetic vanillin, treat curing as a quality variable alongside origin, and label a modern crossover as modern** — and vanilla becomes fully specifiable instead of a vague "sweet note."
