> **Direct answer:** Bergamot (佛手柑, fóshǒugān) is the bergamot orange, *Citrus × bergamia*, a cultivated citrus (Rutaceae) grown chiefly in southern Italy for its fragrant rind. Its peel yields bergamot essential oil, with a bright, citrus-floral, faintly bitter scent. It is a modern essential-oil material, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient, distinct from finger citron (佛手) and citron (香橼).

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Bergamot has a bright, sparkling citrus scent with a distinct floral-green, slightly bitter, almost tea-like edge — more complex and less sweet than lemon, with a delicate neroli-like lift. It is a classic top note in modern perfumery and the signature flavor of Earl Grey tea.

The register is worth pinning against lemon and citron: bergamot is less purely sweet than lemon, and its distinctive value is the *green-floral-bitter* edge — a faint, almost tea-like bitterness that gives the oil its lift and its complexity. This bitter-green undertone is what separates bergamot from the sweeter, simpler citrus notes, and it is why bergamot reads as "elegant" rather than merely "citrusy" in fine fragrance.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus × bergamia*, the bergamot orange, is a cultivated citrus (Rutaceae) grown chiefly in Calabria, southern Italy, for its fragrant rind. It is a modern hybrid — botanists generally treat it as a sour-orange × lemon cross, and modern taxonomy places it under *Citrus × limon*. The rind is cold-pressed to yield bergamot essential oil.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Bergamot oil is produced by cold-pressing the fresh rind of the bergamot orange — a mechanical expression, not a distillation — to yield the bright, citrus-floral essential oil. The oil is the commercial product; the fruit itself is rarely used.

The bergamot rind is cold-pressed to yield the essential oil — a mechanical expression of the peel's oil sacs, not a steam distillation, which is what preserves the oil's fresh, bright top-note character. The oil is the commercial product, and the fruit itself is rarely eaten or used; bergamot is grown almost entirely for its rind oil. This processing detail matters: cold-pressed citrus oils retain the volatile, fresh, green-floral notes that define bergamot, whereas distillation would coarsen or flatten them.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Bergamot oil quality turns on the freshness and character of the cold-pressed oil — a bright, citrus-floral, faintly bitter top note with a clean, not harsh or oxidized, edge. The rind's quality and the cold-press method are the primary drivers, and freshness matters because the top note is volatile.

For a buyer, the practical checks are: **freshness** (a bright, fresh, citrus-floral character, not a flat or oxidized note), **bitter-green balance** (the distinctive faintly bitter, tea-like edge, present but not harsh), and **purity** (cold-pressed bergamot oil, not a synthetic or diluted substitute). Because bergamot's value is as a volatile top note, freshness is the primary quality driver — aged oil loses the bright, sparkling lift that is its whole point.
