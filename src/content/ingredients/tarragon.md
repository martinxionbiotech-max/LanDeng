**Direct answer:** Tarragon — *lónghāo* (龙蒿) in Chinese — is the herb *Artemisia dracunculus*, a perennial member of the *Artemisia* (mugwort) genus, family Asteraceae. Its leaves carry a fresh, herbal, anise-like, slightly sweet scent, familiar from French cooking and, more recently, modern fragrance. It is a **modern culinary and fragrance crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient**, and it must be distinguished from its close relatives 艾草 (mugwort, *Artemisia argyi*) and 青蒿 (sweet wormwood, *Artemisia annua*).

## Aroma profile

龙蒿 (tarragon) has a fresh, green, herbaceous scent with a distinctive **anise-like** sweetness — the licorice-tarragon character of the culinary herb — over a faint bitter-green *Artemisia* base. It is lighter and sweeter than mugwort (艾草), and more anise-sweet than the camphoraceous 青蒿. The anise character comes chiefly from the compound **estragole** (methyl chavicol).

## A provenance note (read this)

龙蒿 (tarragon) is a **modern culinary and fragrance crossover**, not a classical Chinese 香材:

- *Artemisia dracunculus* is a widely distributed northern-temperate species, recorded by Flora of China (including within China's range); the plant itself is not foreign to China. But **"tarragon" as a named culinary herb is a European tradition** — the French-tarragon cultivar — and its use as a fragrance note is modern.
- The classical Chinese incense canon uses other *Artemisia* species — chiefly 艾草 (*A. argyi*) and 青蒿 (*A. annua*) — not tarragon as a distinct 香材.
- 龙蒿 is the Chinese name for the species, but it is **not a lead 香材** in the classical tradition.

This page records it honestly as a modern culinary and fragrance herb — **no classical Chinese incense origin is claimed, and none is fabricated.**

## The Artemisia trio (read this)

The *Artemisia* genus supplies three distinct herbs in this encyclopedia, and they must not be confused:

- **艾草 (mugwort)** — *Artemisia argyi* (and *A. vulgaris*), the perennial mugwort of moxibustion and 艾 incense. Its own entry.
- **青蒿 (sweet wormwood)** — *Artemisia annua*, an annual with a camphoraceous scent, a classical materia-medica herb. Its own entry.
- **龙蒿 (tarragon)** — *Artemisia dracunculus*, a perennial with an anise-like scent, a modern culinary and fragrance herb. This entry.

All three are *Artemisia* herbs sharing a green-herbal family character, but they are different species with different aromas and roles.

## Botanical source and origin

*Artemisia dracunculus* is a perennial herb in the Asteraceae (daisy) family, native across the northern temperate zone and recorded by Flora of China within China. The fragrant part is the aerial herb — the narrow, linear leaves. Two culinary forms are distinguished: **French tarragon** (sterile, anise-sweet, propagated by cuttings) and **Russian tarragon** (seeded, coarser, less aromatic). The fragrance material is the French form.

## In modern use

- **Cooking:** tarragon is a classic French culinary herb — a key component of fines herbes and béarnaise sauce.
- **Fragrance:** tarragon appears in modern perfumery as a green, anise-herbal note, and in modern herbal blends.
- **Not classical Chinese incense:** tarragon has no place in the classical 香材 canon; its named use is modern and European-influenced.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

There is **no classical Chinese incense record** for 龙蒿 (tarragon) as a distinct 香材; its position is modern. We make **no medical claims**. The documented facts are botanical identity, the Asteraceae placement, and its modern culinary and fragrance role.

## FAQ

**What is 龙蒿 (tarragon)?** The herb *Artemisia dracunculus*, a perennial member of the *Artemisia* (mugwort) genus, family Asteraceae. Its leaves carry a fresh, herbal, anise-like, slightly sweet scent, familiar from French cooking and, more recently, modern fragrance. It is a modern culinary and fragrance crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient.

**Is 龙蒿 the same as 艾草 (mugwort)?** No — related but distinct species in the same genus (*Artemisia*). 龙蒿 is *Artemisia dracunculus*, a perennial with an anise-like scent used as a modern culinary and fragrance herb; 艾草 is *Artemisia argyi* (and *A. vulgaris*), the perennial mugwort of moxibustion and 艾 incense. They share a green-herbal family character but are different plants.

**How is tarragon different from 青蒿 (sweet wormwood)?** Both are *Artemisia* herbs, but 龙蒿 is *Artemisia dracunculus* — anise-like and used as a modern culinary herb — while 青蒿 is *Artemisia annua* — camphoraceous and a classical materia-medica herb. Different species, different aromas, different roles.

**What does tarragon smell like?** Fresh, green, and herbaceous, with a distinctive anise-like (licorice-tarragon) sweetness over a faint bitter-green *Artemisia* base. It is lighter and sweeter than mugwort and more anise-sweet than sweet wormwood. The anise character comes chiefly from estragole.

**How is tarragon used?** Primarily as a classic French culinary herb, and in modern perfumery as a green, anise-herbal note. It is a modern, European-influenced use, not a classical Chinese incense material, and it is usually blended rather than burned alone.
