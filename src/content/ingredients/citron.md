> **Direct answer:** Citron (香橼, xiāngyuán) is *Citrus medica*, one of the oldest cultivated citrus, in the rue family (Rutaceae). It is a thick-rinded, intensely fragrant fruit grown for its aromatic rind rather than its juice, used in Chinese culture as a 清供 (scholar's-table offering) and room-fragrance fruit. It is the parent species of the finger citron (佛手), and the two must be kept distinct.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 香橼 is citrus and floral, with a sweet, lemony, slightly bitter rind scent that is more perfumed and complex than an orange, and more reserved than the finger citron's exuberant floral lift. The fragrance lives in the thick, oil-rich rind, not the scant juice.

香橼 is citrus and floral, with a sweet, lemony, slightly bitter rind scent that is more perfumed and complex than an orange, and more reserved than the finger citron's exuberant floral lift. The fragrance lives in the thick, oil-rich rind, not the scant juice. In a room it reads as a *clear, elegant citrus*, experienced by proximity and evaporation rather than by burning.

Like the finger citron, the citron is a "fragrance of presence" material — its rind slowly releases volatile oil, scenting a room by evaporation. But where the finger citron's floral lift is exuberant, the citron's is more reserved, with a slightly bitter, perfumed-complex edge that reads as more mature and less sweet.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus medica* is one of the most ancient cultivated citrus, native to the foothills of the Himalaya and long grown across China, Southeast Asia, and the Mediterranean. It is also one of the ancestral species — alongside pomelo and mandarin — from which much of the cultivated citrus family descends.

*Citrus medica* is one of the most ancient cultivated citrus, native to the foothills of the Himalaya and long grown across China, Southeast Asia, and the Mediterranean. Its fruit is large, thick-rinded, and intensely aromatic, with relatively little pulp. Flora of China records the species (including the fingered form *sarcodactylis*); the citron is also the ancestor — alongside pomelo and mandarin — of much of the cultivated citrus family.

This ancestral status is the citron's quiet distinction: it is one of the "parent" species of the whole citrus genus, which means a large part of the citrus family — lemons, limes, oranges, grapefruits — descends, in hybridization terms, from the citron. Yet in Chinese fragrance it remained a humble 清供 fruit, not a grand incense material — a reminder that botanical importance and aromatic importance are different things.

## The citrus-family context (read this)

- **佛手 (finger citron)** — *Citrus medica* var. *sarcodactylis*, its fingered variety.

The citron is the botanical anchor: the finger citron is its variety, and the lemon descends partly from it. But the names cross in confusing ways (佛手柑 ≠ 佛手), so a buyer must specify the species, not rely on the name.

## Why is the citron used as a room fragrance?

> **Direct answer:** The citron scents a room by evaporation from its thick, oil-rich rind — a "fragrance of presence" requiring no burning. It is displayed as a 清供 offering, where its clear, elegant citrus scent and its auspicious form are the point.

The citron's role is the same as the finger citron's: **presence, not combustion.** Its thick, oil-rich rind slowly releases volatile fragrance, scenting a room for days simply by sitting on a scholar's table. It is a 清供 (offering) fruit, displayed for its fragrance and its form — and, like the finger citron, it is a non-combustible fragrance material, smelled in place rather than burned. The difference from the finger citron is subtle but real: the citron's scent is more reserved and slightly bitter, where the finger citron's is more exuberantly floral.

## How is it used / processed?

> **Direct answer:** The citron needs no processing for its primary use — it is displayed whole, and its rind scents the room by evaporation. Where a preserved form is wanted, the rind may be candied or dried, but the fragrance use is the fresh, whole fruit.

The processing is minimal, because the primary use is the whole fresh fruit:

1. **Display whole** — the fresh fruit is placed on a table or offering stand, scenting the room by evaporation.
2. **Drying (optional)** — the rind may be dried, with loss of volatile fragrance.
3. **Candying (optional)** — the thick rind may be candied as a food.

There is no distillation or combustion step for the classical use; the citron's fragrance is released by evaporation, and its processing is essentially "display it."

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality is a matter of rind condition and fragrance: a fresh, intact, thick-rinded fruit with a clear, elegant, slightly bitter citrus scent. The oil-rich rind is the value, not the scant juice, and there is no standardized grading ladder.

The quality factors:

1. **Fragrance intensity** — the strength and clarity of the citrus-floral rind scent; the rind's oil is the value.
2. **Freshness** — the volatile fragrance fades as the fruit dries; a fresh fruit is more fragrant.
3. **Rind condition** — an intact, thick, oil-rich rind without damage or rot.

There is no standardized grading system; fragrance, freshness, and rind condition are the practical signals.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** The citron is the botanical "root" of the Chinese citrus-fragrance family — the ancestral species behind the finger citron and, in hybridization, the lemon — yet it remained a humble 清供 fruit, not an incense material. That gap between botanical importance and aromatic importance is its real lesson for sourcing.

From a manufacturing and sourcing perspective, the citron illustrates something subtle: **botanical importance and aromatic importance do not always align.** Three points follow:

- **An ancestor that stayed modest.** The citron is one of the parent species of the entire citrus genus, so a large share of the world's citrus — lemons, limes, oranges, grapefruits — carries its lineage. Yet in Chinese fragrance it remained a 清供 display fruit, never entering the burned-incense canon (香乘). This is a useful corrective to the assumption that "ancient" or "foundational" means "commercially central."
- **The fragrance-of-presence logic.** Like the finger citron, the citron scents a room by evaporation, not combustion — a category modern incense commerce barely serves. Its value is volatile rind oil, and freshness is the whole specification, because that oil fades with time.
- **The name is the trap.** The citron anchors a naming tangle: its variety is 佛手 (finger citron), and the bergamot's name 佛手柑 borrows 佛手. A buyer who does not pin down the species can easily end up with the wrong citrus. Disambiguation, not quality, is the practical risk.

In our view, the professional framing for the citron is a **fresh, correctly identified, ancestral 清供 fruit** — a material whose lesson is that pedigree is not the same as commercial value, and whose chief risk is a name shared across three different citrus.
