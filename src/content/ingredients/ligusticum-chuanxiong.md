> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong (川芎, chuānxiōng) is the aromatic rhizome of *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, an herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae) cultivated in Sichuan. It has a strong, herbal, spicy, slightly bitter aromatic-root character and is one of the best-known aromatic roots of Chinese materia medica. It is a different species from, and must be distinguished from, 藁本 (*Ligusticum sinense*).

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong smells like a pungent, penetrating lovage root — herbal and spicy with a warm, slightly bitter edge — more medicinal and sharp than the sweeter, softer angelica note. It anchors a blend with an earthy, aromatic middle rather than a floral or resinous top.

川芎 has a strong, penetrating, herbal-spicy root aroma with a slightly bitter, warm edge — the pungent-sweet smell characteristic of the lovage/carrot-family roots. It is more medicinal and sharp than the softer sweet-herbal notes of angelica (当归), and it anchors a blend with an earthy, aromatic middle rather than a floral or resinous top. The aroma is concentrated in the rhizome, where the essential oil sits in the tissue; the scent reads "rooty" and medicinal, not green or floral, which is why it behaves as a middle-to-base aromatic rather than a bright opening note.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong is the rhizome of *Ligusticum chuanxiong* (Apiaceae), a Sichuan-cultivated aromatic of the lovage group. Modern botanical databases treat the name as a synonym of *Conioselinum anthriscoides* — a nomenclatural shift that matters for sourcing documentation, because older and Chinese materia-medica sources still use *Ligusticum chuanxiong*.

*Ligusticum chuanxiong* is an aromatic perennial of the carrot family (Apiaceae), cultivated in Sichuan as the source of 川芎. Modern botanical databases treat the name as a synonym of *Conioselinum anthriscoides*; Flora of China records the lovage group under *Ligusticum*, where 藁本 (*L. sinense*) sits alongside other species. The Sichuan name 川芎 ("Sichuan lovage") reflects its place of cultivation.

This nomenclatural instability is a real sourcing issue, not an academic footnote. A buyer or formulator who looks up the material in one database finds *Ligusticum chuanxiong*, in another *Conioselinum anthriscoides*, and in a Chinese materia-medica manual simply 川芎. All three point to the same cultivated Sichuan root, but the divergence means the *Chinese drug name* — 川芎, not 藁本 — is the most reliable specifier across sources. ## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** The name 川芎 means "Sichuan lovage" (川 = Sichuan, 芎 short for 芎藭, the classical lovage name). The *Bencao Gangmu* records the origin logic directly — "出蜀中者，為川芎" ("the one produced in Sichuan is called chuanxiong") — so the name is a provenance marker that distinguishes the Sichuan root from lovage grown elsewhere.

The classical name for the lovage group is 芎藭 (xiōngqióng), under which the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), 草之三, records 川芎 as the Sichuan form: "出蜀中者，為川芎" — the one produced in Sichuan (蜀) is called 川芎. The name therefore encodes provenance, not just plant identity: it tells you *where* the root was grown, which in classical pharmacology was part of the identity claim. This is the same naming logic seen in other place-tagged Chinese aromatics, and it is why "chuanxiong" and "lovage" are not interchangeable in a sourcing conversation — the former carries the Sichuan-cultivation claim.

## Where does chuanxiong come from?

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong is a cultivated crop of Sichuan, in southwestern China, where it is grown as a field herb rather than wild-harvested. Its name records this geographic anchoring — it is a Sichuan agricultural aromatic, not a foraged wild root.

川芎 is fundamentally a cultivated material. Unlike many aromatic roots that are wild-harvested, 川芎 is grown as an agricultural crop in Sichuan, where the regional name and the cultivation practice are linked. This has a concrete implication for sourcing: it is a *crop* with agricultural seasonality and regional supply, not a wild-harvested botanical with the variability and conservation concerns that wild collection carries. The Sichuan identity is therefore simultaneously a provenance claim, a quality signal (the regional material being the named standard), and a documentation point — a buyer should be able to confirm the Sichuan origin rather than accepting an undifferentiated "lovage root."

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong is used in incense not as a lead scent but as a medicinal-aromatic bridge — its pungent, spicy root character adds an earthy, herbal middle that supports sweeter or woodier aromatics. Its primary classical standing is in materia medica, so its incense role is secondary and structural rather than a defining 香材.

In the incense context, 川芎 occupies an interesting intermediate position. It is **primarily a materia-medica root**, not a lead 香材 (incense material) in the way agarwood or sandalwood are. But its strong aromatic character — pungent, herbal, spicy — gives it a genuine supporting role: it can lend an earthy, aromatic middle that anchors a blend and bridges sharper or sweeter materials. The key distinction to hold is that its incense use is *derived from* its medicinal-aromatic character, not from a classical incense-manual prominence. It is a root you might add for its herbal spine, not one you would build a formula around. ## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong is processed by digging the rhizome, cleaning, and drying — the aromatic value being concentrated in the rhizome rather than the aerial herb. There is no distillation step for the root form; only its essential oil (if required) would be extracted separately.

The processing of 川芎 is straightforward: the rhizome is dug, cleaned of soil, and dried, concentrating the essential oil that carries the pungent, spicy aroma. In its dried whole or sliced form it is a root commodity; ground, it becomes a powder that can be blended. Because its incense role is as a supporting aromatic, the practical processing question for a formulator is particle size and dryness — but, as with most botanical powders, the correct target depends on the formula, binder, and production line rather than any universal figure. ## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality in chuanxiong is driven by aroma intensity — a good root should smell strongly pungent, herbal, and spicy — plus cleanliness, dryness, and freedom from the wrong species (藁本). Provenance (Sichuan) and the correct drug name are the identification checks that sit above the sensory ones.

For a sourcing buyer, the quality checks are:

1. **Aroma intensity** — the pungent, spicy character should be strong and immediate; a weak-smelling root is low-grade.
2. **Species and name** — confirm it is 川芎 (*Ligusticum chuanxiong*), not 藁本 (*Ligusticum sinense*); the drug name is the reliable specifier.
3. **Provenance** — the name itself claims Sichuan origin; a credible supplier should be able to state it.
4. **Cleanliness and dryness** — free of soil, mold, and excess moisture, since these affect both storage and powder behavior.

None of these is a laboratory test; they are sensory and documentary screening. The single most useful check is the aroma, because in a material defined by its pungent root scent, the scent *is* the grade.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** Chuanxiong is a useful case study in a sourcing principle the encyclopedia returns to repeatedly: when a plant has a shifting botanical name and a shared genus, the *Chinese drug name* — not the Latin synonym — is the reliable specifier. Provenance and the drug name do more identification work than the modern taxonomy does.

From a sourcing perspective, 川芎 illustrates a recurring problem in aromatic roots: **nomenclatural drift.** The same cultivated Sichuan root appears as *Ligusticum chuanxiong* in some databases, *Conioselinum anthriscoides* in others, and 川芎 in Chinese materia medica — while 藁本 (*Ligusticum sinense*) sits confusingly close in the same genus and is also drifting toward the same synonym. For a buyer or formulator, the lesson is practical:

- **Pin the drug name, not the Latin synonym.** Because the taxonomy is in motion, "川芎" is a more stable specifier than any single botanical name. It also carries the provenance claim (Sichuan) that the Latin name does not.
- **Aroma is the grade, because the material is an aromatic root.** In a commodity defined by its pungent, herbal-spicy scent, the intensity of that scent is the single most informative quality signal — a weak-smelling root fails the one test that matters.
- **Its incense role is structural, not leading.** Chuanxiong is a supporting aromatic whose value in a blend is an earthy, herbal middle; it is not a material to build a formula around, and buyers should treat it accordingly — as an accent, not a centerpiece.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot for a material like this is a **well-documented Sichuan 川芎 with a strong, consistent aroma and a clear drug name**, sourced and specified as 川芎 rather than as an undifferentiated lovage root — because the provenance and the name are precisely what separate it from its close relative 藁本.
