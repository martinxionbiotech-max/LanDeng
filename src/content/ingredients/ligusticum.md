**Technical answer:** Ligusticum — *gǎoběn* (藁本) in Chinese — is the dried root and rhizome of *Ligusticum sinense*, an aromatic herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae) native to China. It is a classical Chinese incense ingredient with a herbal, slightly spicy, aromatic root character, best known in the incense record as one of the named ingredients of the 寿阳公主梅花香 (Shouyang princess's plum incense) formula, where it supplies a herbal middle tone alongside spikenard and angelica.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Ligusticum root is herbal and aromatic, with a slightly spicy, warming, faintly woody character typical of the carrot-family aromatic roots. It is a middle note that sits in the herbal register of a blend, supporting and grounding brighter or sweeter ingredients rather than leading.

In classical formulas it is roasted or dried and ground, and it contributes body rather than a leading scent.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 is *Ligusticum sinense*, an herbaceous Apiaceae plant. Flora of China records it as 0.5–1 m tall with a thick rootstock "apparently swollen at nodes," growing in forests and scrub from 500–2,700 m across central and western China.

*Ligusticum sinense* is an herbaceous plant of the Apiaceae family, native to China. Flora of China records the plant as **0.5–1 m tall**, with a **thick rootstock "apparently swollen at nodes"** — the rootstock and rhizome being exactly the material of value. It grows in forests, montane scrub, grassy slopes, streamsides, and moist roadsides, from 500–2,700 m, across Gansu, Guizhou, Henan, Hubei, Jiangxi, Nei Mongol, Shaanxi, Sichuan, and Yunnan, flowering July–August. See [Ligusticum sinense in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200015664).

The part used is the root and rhizome, harvested, cleaned, and dried. The genus *Ligusticum* groups aromatic "lovage-like" herbs of the carrot family, several of which are used as spices and incense materials.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 (gǎoběn) is the Chinese name for *Ligusticum sinense*; it is distinct from 川芎 (chuānxiōng, *Ligusticum chuanxiong*), its close relative in the same genus. The two are separate Chinese drug names kept apart by materia medica, despite modern taxonomic reshuffling.

The name 藁本 belongs to one member of a closely related group. Two terms matter:

- **藁本 (gǎoběn)** — *Ligusticum sinense*, the Chinese lovage. This entry.
- **川芎 (chuānxiōng, chuanxiong)** — *Ligusticum chuanxiong* (syn. *Conioselinum anthriscoides*), the Sichuan lovage, a related but distinct root. See [ligusticum-chuanxiong](/ingredients/ligusticum-chuanxiong/).

Chinese materia medica keeps the two separate, and modern taxonomy has shifted both toward *Conioselinum anthriscoides* — which is precisely why the Chinese drug name (藁本 vs 川芎) matters more than the shifting scientific label.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Ligusticum (藁本) is used in incense as a herbal middle note — it supplies a herbal, slightly medicinal middle register beneath the plum and wood notes of classical formulas, most famously the 寿阳公主梅花香 (Shouyang princess's plum incense).

Ligusticum's incense role is best understood through its classical formula appearances. In the plum-incense accord it sits with spikenard (甘松) and angelica (白芷) as the herbal, slightly medicinal **middle register** beneath the plum and wood notes — it supplies the blend's herbal body and depth rather than leading with a bright or sweet note. It is a supporting ingredient, used for its grounding herbal character. See the [Shouyang princess plum recipe](/blog/shouyang-princess-plum-recipe/).

## What determines ligusticum quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on the root's aromatic intensity and soundness — a clean, dry root with a herbal, slightly spicy scent when warmed beats a stale or woody one. The aromatic compounds concentrate in the root and rhizome, so a sound, fragrant root is the benchmark.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Aromatic intensity** — the root should be herbal, aromatic, and slightly spicy when warmed; this is the decisive variable.
2. **Soundness and cleanliness** — freedom from rot, mold, and foreign matter.
3. **Root maturity** — a sound, mature rootstock (recalling the botanical "swollen at nodes" rootstock) reads richer than thin material.
4. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

There is **no standardized grading system**; the buyer evaluates the physical condition and aroma of the lot.

## Ligusticum vs chuanxiong (川芎)

> **Direct answer:** 藁本 (*Ligusticum sinense*) and 川芎 (*Ligusticum chuanxiong*) are closely related but distinct roots in the same genus. 藁本 is the herbal middle-note root of classical incense formulas; 川芎 is the materia-medica rhizome of Sichuan. ## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The main risk is misidentification under the "lovage" or "ligusticum" name: 藁本 (*Ligusticum sinense*) is distinct from true lovage (*Levisticum officinale*) and from chuanxiong (*L. chuanxiong*). The species or Chinese drug name on the label resolves which is meant.

Ligusticum is not a heavily faked, expensive aromatic; its risk is a **naming and identification matter**. Two confusions recur:

- **"Lovage"** — the English name "lovage" is sometimes applied to 藁本, but true lovage is *Levisticum officinale*, a different plant.
- **Chuanxiong** — 藁本 and 川芎 are closely related roots that modern taxonomy has both shifted toward *Conioselinum anthriscoides*, so a label naming only the genus may not distinguish them.

The Chinese drug name (藁本 vs 川芎) or the full botanical name resolves the matter. See the [incense substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) for the general screening logic.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, 藁本 is a niche middle-note ingredient whose value is authentic classical character, not volume. Its role depends on consistency — of aroma, particle size, moisture, and batch-to-batch variation — more than on any rare or premium grading.

From a manufacturing perspective, 藁本 is a **niche ingredient whose value is authenticity, not scale**. It will not carry a formula the way sandalwood or agarwood can; its job is to supply a herbal middle register that reproduces a classical accord — which is exactly the role most sensitive to batch drift, since a slightly stale or differently ground lot changes the blend's herbal character without announcing itself.

Two points follow:

- **It is a supporting note, so consistency is the specification.** A formula that relies on 藁本 for its herbal middle needs the *same* register every batch. A single fragrant lot proves little; what matters is that the next lot smells and behaves like the last.
- **Particle size and moisture affect behavior, not just aroma.** In powder, particle size changes how the material packs, draws air, and burns; moisture changes how it grinds and how the finished stick dries. The right target **depends on formulation** — the binder, the other ingredients, and the production line — so a figure that works in one factory does not transfer to another.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **consistent, correctly identified 藁本 with a steady herbal profile**, used as a supporting middle note — a niche root whose value is that it makes a classical formula reproducible.
