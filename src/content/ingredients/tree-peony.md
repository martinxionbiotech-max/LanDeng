> **Direct answer:** Tree peony (牡丹, mǔdān) is the woody-stemmed peony, *Paeonia suffruticosa*, the shrub peony of the peony family (Paeoniaceae) native to China. It is the celebrated "king of flowers" (花王) of Chinese horticulture, with a soft, rosy, sweet floral scent. It is a different species from, and must be distinguished from, the herbaceous peony (芍药, *Paeonia lactiflora*).

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Paeonia suffruticosa* is a woody-stemmed shrub peony (Paeoniaceae), native to China and the ancestor of the vast garden tree-peony cultivars. Unlike the herbaceous 芍药 (*P. lactiflora*), it produces a persistent woody stem that survives winter.

*Paeonia suffruticosa* is a woody-stemmed shrub peony (Paeoniaceae), native to China and the ancestor of the vast garden tree-peony cultivars. Unlike the herbaceous 芍药 (*P. lactiflora*), it produces a persistent woody stem that survives winter. Flora of China records the species; the same genus (*Paeonia*) holds both the shrub (牡丹) and herbaceous (芍药) peonies.

The woody stem is the whole distinction: it is why the plant is called the *tree* peony, and why the Tang name 木芍药 ("woody peony") marks it as the shrub relative of the herbaceous 芍药. The name records the botanical fact.

## Why is tree peony used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Tree peony is not a lead incense ingredient — its fame is ornamental and cultural. It can lend a soft, rosy, green floral accent, but the classical incense florals are jasmine, osmanthus, and rose. The tree peony's root bark (牡丹皮) is a separate materia-medica part.

The tree peony's incense role is a *quiet accent*, not a lead note. Its soft, rosy, sweet-green scent is gentle and elegant, but its fame — "king of flowers" — is a horticultural and cultural distinction, not an aromatic one. In the classical incense canon, the prominent florals are jasmine (茉莉), osmanthus (桂花), and rose (玫瑰); the tree peony appears as a delicate floral accent where it appears at all. Its root bark (牡丹皮) is a separate materia-medica part, distinct from the flower.

This is the honest framing: the tree peony is a flower whose value is beauty, not scent, and its incense use is correspondingly minor.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** The tree peony flower needs no processing for its ornamental use — it is grown and appreciated in the garden. Where the flower is used aromatically, it is a light accent, and the distinct materia-medica part is the root bark (牡丹皮), which is a separate product.

The processing depends on the part:

1. **Ornamental flower** — grown and appreciated in the garden; no processing for its primary cultural role.
2. **Floral accent** — where the flower is used aromatically, it is a light, delicate accent.
3. **Root bark (牡丹皮)** — the materia-medica part, harvested and dried separately; its own entry.

The flower and the root bark are two different products with different processing; the celebrated flower is essentially unprocessed, while the bark is a harvested and dried materia-medica material.

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** The tree peony is the clearest case of "cultural weight vs aromatic value": it is the "king of flowers," with enormous cultural fame, yet almost no incense role. This is a useful corrective — fame does not equal fragrance value, and the tree peony's commercial reality is split between an ornamental flower and a materia-medica root bark.

From a manufacturing and sourcing perspective, the tree peony illustrates a principle that is easy to miss in an aromatic canon: **cultural fame and aromatic value are different currencies.** Three points follow:

- **The "king of flowers" has almost no incense role.** 牡丹 is among the most culturally celebrated flowers in Chinese tradition — the 花王 of art and poetry — yet it is not a prominent incense floral. The classical incense florals are jasmine, osmanthus, and rose. This is a useful correction to the assumption that a famous flower must be an important incense material: the tree peony's fame is horticultural, not aromatic.
- **One plant, two very different products.** The tree peony supplies an ornamental flower (valued for beauty) and a materia-medica root bark (牡丹皮, valued in a different, traditional-medical register). These are not two grades of one product but two products entirely — and conflating them is the main sourcing error.
- **The name records the botany.** The Tang alias 木芍药 ("woody peony") is not a poetic flourish but a precise botanical marker — the shrub peony, distinguished from the herbaceous 芍药 by its persistent woody stem. The name itself is the disambiguation, which is a useful pattern for buyers.

In our view, the professional framing for the tree peony is a **correctly part- and species-identified plant** — the ornamental flower (牡丹) distinguished from the herbaceous peony (芍药) and from its own root bark (牡丹皮) — and a reminder that in botanicals, fame and fragrance value do not always travel together.
