## What is nutmeg?

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg (肉豆蔻, *ròudòukòu*) is the dried seed of *Myristica fragrans*, an evergreen tree of the nutmeg family (Myristicaceae) native to the Moluccas of Indonesia. It is a warm, spicy, sweet spice whose lacy red covering — mace (肉豆蔻衣) — is a separate spice from the same fruit.

Nutmeg is a **seed spice with a double identity**: one fruit of *Myristica fragrans* yields two distinct spices — the seed (nutmeg, 肉豆蔻) and the lacy red aril that wraps it (mace, 肉豆蔻衣). It is familiar from baking and braising, and in Chinese incense it appears as a warm, food-adjacent spice accent in some 合香 (blended incense) contexts, rather than as a classical defining material. Its Chinese name shares the 豆蔻 element with cardamom, which is a different plant — a naming trap worth reading.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg is the seed of *Myristica fragrans*, a member of the nutmeg family (Myristicaceae) — a distinct family from the ginger family (Zingiberaceae) that produces cardamom and sand ginger. The tree is dioecious, native to the Moluccas, and now cultivated across the tropics.

*Myristica fragrans* is an evergreen tree of the **nutmeg family (Myristicaceae)**, native to the Moluccas — the historical "Spice Islands" — of Indonesia, and now cultivated across the tropics. It is a distinct family from the ginger family (Zingiberaceae), a point that matters because several Chinese spice names (cardamom 白豆蔻, sand ginger 山柰) sit in Zingiberaceae while nutmeg does not. The tree yields two spices from one fruit: the **seed** (nutmeg, 肉豆蔻) and the **lacy red aril** around it (mace, 肉豆蔻衣). See [Myristica fragrans on GBIF](https://www.gbif.org/species/5406817).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 肉豆蔻 (ròudòukòu, "fleshy cardamom") is the seed; mace is 肉豆蔻衣 ("nutmeg coat"). The shared 豆蔻 ("cardamom") element in Chinese spice names reflects historical grouping, not the same plant — nutmeg (肉豆蔻) is not cardamom (白豆蔻).

The Chinese terms:

- **肉豆蔻 (ròudòukòu)** — nutmeg, the seed; 肉 "fleshy" distinguishes it from other 豆蔻-named spices.
- **肉豆蔻衣 (ròudòukòu yī)** — mace, the aril; literally "nutmeg coat."

The shared 豆蔻 in Chinese spice names is a historical grouping, not a botanical one: nutmeg (肉豆蔻, Myristicaceae) and cardamom (白豆蔻, *Amomum kravanh*, Zingiberaceae) are different spices in different families.

## Where does nutmeg come from?

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg is native to the Moluccas (the historical "Spice Islands") of Indonesia and is now cultivated across the tropics. It entered Chinese cooking and materia medica as an imported aromatic after centuries of global spice trade.

*Myristica fragrans* is native to the **Moluccas of Indonesia**, the historical "Spice Islands" whose nutmeg monopoly drove centuries of colonial spice trade. It is now cultivated across the tropics — Indonesia, Grenada, India, and elsewhere. Nutmeg reached China as an **imported aromatic**, entering both cooking and materia medica, which is why its Chinese name reflects a borrowed, exotic spice rather than a native plant.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg is warm, spicy, and sweet, with a woody, slightly penetrating edge from its essential oil. It is a "sweet-spice" note in the family of clove and cardamom, and in incense it adds a warm, food-adjacent spiciness as an accent rather than a base.

Nutmeg is warm, spicy, and sweet, with a woody, slightly penetrating edge from its essential oil. It is a "sweet-spice" note in the family of clove and cardamom. In incense it adds a warm, food-adjacent spiciness — an accent rather than a base. The closely related mace (the red aril around the seed) has a similar but more delicate character.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg is used in incense as a warm, sweet spice accent that adds food-adjacent warmth to a 合香 blend, usually alongside clove and cardamom, rather than as a lead or defining material.

Nutmeg's incense role follows its culinary character: a warm, sweet, slightly woody spice note that lends a comforting, food-adjacent warmth. It is a **supporting accent** in 合香 (blended incense) — used alongside clove and cardamom to build a sweet-spice layer — rather than a classical defining material burned on its own. Because its scent fades after grinding, the whole seed (grated fresh) is the practical form for a blend.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Nutmeg is used as the dried seed, either whole (grated fresh) or pre-ground. The seed loses scent once ground, so whole nutmeg is preferred, and in incense it is usually ground or grated into a blend, not distilled.

The processing is simple and seed-first:

- **Harvest and dry** — the fruit is harvested and the seed (with its aril removed for mace) is dried.
- **Whole vs ground** — the seed is sold whole or pre-ground; because it loses aroma once ground, whole is the quality form.
- **Blend** — in incense, nutmeg is grated or ground into a blend as a warm spice accent.

The one practical rule is **grind fresh**: nutmeg's essential oil is volatile, so the whole seed retains scent far longer than pre-ground powder.

## What determines nutmeg quality?

> **Direct answer:** Whole nutmeg should be dense, oily, and strongly aromatic; the seed loses scent once ground, so whole is preferred. The strength of aroma when freshly broken or grated is the main quality indicator.

The quality factors:

1. **Whole vs ground** — whole seed is preferred; pre-ground loses aroma quickly.
2. **Oil content** — a dense, oily, strongly aromatic seed is the marker of good material.
3. **Aroma** — a clean warm-spicy-sweet character with no mustiness.

There is no standardized grading system; the freshly broken aroma is the working test.
