> **Direct answer:** Tsaoko (草果, cǎoguǒ) is the dried fruit of *Amomum tsao-ko* (recently reclassified as *Lanxangia tsao-ko* in some databases), a ginger-family herb (Zingiberaceae) native to the mountains of Yunnan and Guangxi. It is a smoky, spicy, camphoraceous aromatic best known as the "black cardamom" of southwestern Chinese cooking, used in incense as a 香药 (aromatic-medicine) spice accent.

## Aroma profile

草果 is smoky and spicy, with a camphoraceous, resinous warmth and a distinctly earthy-smoky depth that sets it apart from the brighter, sweeter white cardamom. Its smokiness comes through especially in the dried whole fruit, which is usually cracked or bruised before use. In a blend it reads as a *dark, warming spice*, adding body and a smouldering undertone rather than sweet lift.

## Botanical source and origin

*Amomum tsao-ko* is a ginger-family (Zingiberaceae) herb native to the mountains of southern China (Yunnan, Guangxi) and adjacent northern Southeast Asia. The part used is the dried, ribbed, red-brown fruit, whose dark seeds carry the aroma. Flora of China records it as *Amomum tsaoko*; GBIF has recently transferred the name to *Lanxangia tsao-ko*. The same ginger family includes 白豆蔻 (*Amomum kravanh*), 砂仁 (*Amomum villosum*), and 高良姜 (galangal).

## In Chinese tradition

- **Culinary spice:** 草果 is a signature spice of southwestern Chinese cooking — the dark, smoky "black cardamom" of braised and stewed dishes.
- **香药 (aromatic medicine):** as a 香药 it bridges spice and traditional medicine.
- **Not a 香乘 ingredient:** 草果 does not appear in the classical incense compendium 香乘; its documented role is culinary and medicinal rather than as a canonical incense ingredient.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The culinary and 香药 use is well documented. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Zingiberaceae placement, and the smoky camphoraceous aromatic use.

## Quality and sourcing notes

Practical points for buyers:

- 草果 is the dried whole fruit; the aroma is in the dark seeds, so well-filled, intact fruits are preferred.
- Expect a dark, smoky, camphoraceous spice — distinct from the sweeter 白豆蔻 and the more medicinal 砂仁.
- In incense it is a dark warming accent, usually blended rather than burned alone, and is not a classical 香乘 ingredient.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice.

## FAQ

**What is 草果 (tsaoko)?** The dried fruit of *Amomum tsao-ko* (Flora of China: *Amomum tsaoko*; recently reclassified as *Lanxangia tsao-ko*), a ginger-family herb (Zingiberaceae) native to the mountains of Yunnan and Guangxi. It is a smoky, spicy, camphoraceous aromatic best known as the "black cardamom" of southwestern Chinese cooking, and used in incense as a 香药 spice accent.

**Is 草果 the same as black cardamom?** In the Chinese context, yes — 草果 is often called "black cardamom" in English, but it is not the same as the Indian black cardamom (*Amomum subulatum*). 草果 is *Amomum tsao-ko*, a distinct species with a smoky, camphoraceous character. It is also distinct from white/round cardamom (白豆蔻, *Amomum kravanh*).

**What does 草果 smell like?** Smoky and spicy, with a camphoraceous, resinous warmth and an earthy-smoky depth that sets it apart from brighter, sweeter cardamom. The smokiness comes through especially in the dried whole fruit, usually cracked or bruised before use. In a blend it reads as a dark, warming spice that adds body rather than sweet lift.

**How is 草果 used in Chinese cooking?** It is a signature spice of southwestern Chinese cuisine — the dark, smoky "black cardamom" of braised and stewed dishes, where whole fruits are cracked or bruised to release their aromatic seeds. Its smoky warmth is central to many Sichuan and Yunnan braises and stews, and it also serves as a 香药 (aromatic medicine).

**Is 草果 a classical incense ingredient?** Not in the 香乘 canon — 草果 does not appear in the classical incense compendium 香乘. Its documented role is culinary and medicinal (香药) rather than as a canonical incense ingredient. Its use in incense is a modern application of its smoky aromatic character.
