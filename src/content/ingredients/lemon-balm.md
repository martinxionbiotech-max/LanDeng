> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm (香蜂草, xiāngfēngcǎo) is the herb *Melissa officinalis*, a perennial of the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to southern Europe and the Mediterranean. Its leaves carry a soft, lemony, fresh, green scent. It is a modern crossover, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 香蜂草 (lemon balm) is soft and lemony, with a fresh, green, herbaceous character and a gentle sweetness — the delicate lemon scent of the crushed leaf, quieter and more herbal than the bright zest of actual lemon. Its lemony character comes from the compounds citral and citronellal, shared with other lemon-scented herbs.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Melissa officinalis*, lemon balm, is a perennial herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae), with erect, pyramidally branched stems, ovate leaves, and creamy-white flowers, flowering June–August. Flora of China records it (under 香蜂花) as cultivated in China, native to the Mediterranean and southwestern Asia.

*Melissa officinalis*, lemon balm, is a perennial herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to southern Europe and the Mediterranean. Flora of China records it (under the name 香蜂花) as a plant with **erect, pyramidally branched, pubescent stems**, **ovate leaves 1–5 (–6.5) × 0.8–4 (–5) cm**, and a **creamy-white corolla 1.2–1.3 cm**, flowering **June–August**; it is recorded as **cultivated in China**, native to southwestern Asia and Europe. Flora of China adds that it is **"used for flavoring salads, soups, and liqueurs; the oil is employed in perfumery; commonly known as balm tea."** The fragrant part is the leaf, used fresh or dried. It shares the mint family with 薄荷 (*Mentha haplocalyx*) and 留兰香 (*Mentha spicata*), but is a distinct genus (*Melissa*). See [Melissa officinalis in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=200019809).

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 香蜂草 (xiāngfēngcǎo) means "fragrant bee herb" — 香 "fragrant" + 蜂 "bee" + 草 "herb" — a name that records the plant's bee-attracting flowers and its scent; the Flora of China name is 香蜂花 ("fragrant bee flower"). The name is modern, and the "lemon" in "lemon balm" is an English descriptor, not a botanical claim.

The Chinese name 香蜂草 is descriptive and modern: 香 (xiāng, "fragrant") + 蜂 (fēng, "bee") + 草 (cǎo, "herb") — "fragrant bee herb," after the plant's bee-attracting flowers. The Flora of China name 香蜂花 (xiāngfēnghuā, "fragrant bee flower") points the same way. The English name "lemon balm" is the descriptor that needs care: the "lemon" is a **scent character** (from citral/citronellal), not a botanical relationship — lemon balm is a mint-family herb, not a citrus. The genus name *Melissa* is itself the Greek word for "honey bee," completing the same bee association.

## The lemon-scented convergence (read this)

> **Direct answer:** The lemon scent is not a citrus monopoly. Lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*, Lamiaceae), lemon (*Citrus × limon*, Rutaceae), and lemongrass (a grass, Poaceae) all smell "lemony" through citral/citronellal-type chemistry — three different plant families converging on one aromatic family. "Lemon-scented" names a molecule, not a relationship.

The "lemon" note is one of the clearest cases of aromatic convergence in this encyclopedia, and it matters for sourcing:

- **香蜂草 (lemon balm)** — *Melissa officinalis*, a mint-family (Lamiaceae) herb; lemony via citral/citronellal. This entry.
- **柠檬 (lemon)** — *Citrus × limon*, a rue-family (Rutaceae) citrus; lemony via limonene/citral from the rind.
- **Lemongrass** — *Cymbopogon*, a grass (Poaceae); lemony via citral.

Three different plant families converge on the same "lemony" register through shared chemistry. The practical consequence: **"lemon-scented" is a molecule, not a species** — a buyer who orders "something lemony" is ordering a register, and could receive a mint herb, a citrus, or a grass depending on what the seller means. ## How is lemon balm used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm is not a classical incense material, so in incense it appears only as a modern fragrance note — a soft, green-lemony accent, usually as an essential oil or accord rather than a burned herb. Its quiet, fresh character is a blending accent, not a base or a lead.

Because lemon balm has no classical Chinese incense role, its incense use is entirely modern and borrowed from perfumery. Where it does appear, it is as a **soft, green-lemony accent** — typically delivered as an essential oil or a fragrance accord rather than as dried herb to be burned. Its citral/citronellal freshness lifts greener, lighter blends, a role closer to lemon or a green herb than to a heavy wood. The practical point for a formulator is that lemon balm's charm is quiet and top-note in nature: it brightens a blend but does not anchor it, and it is easily lost under heat or buried by heavier materials.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm is processed by harvesting the leaves and using them fresh or dried, or by distilling or extracting the essential oil for fragrance. The leaf is the fragrant part, and its soft lemony note is volatile, so drying and freshness govern how much character survives.

The processing of lemon balm is straightforward:

1. **Harvest** — the leaves are harvested.
2. **Use fresh or dried** — for tea and herbal use, the leaf is used fresh or dried.
3. **Distill / extract** — for fragrance, the leaf is distilled or extracted for its essential oil.

The fragrant part is the leaf, and its soft, lemony note is volatile — so **drying temperature and freshness** govern how much character survives, a point shared with other leafy herbs.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on a fresh, clean, softly lemony leaf with a good citral/citronellal character, and — for fragrance — a well-distilled oil. Because lemon balm is quiet, freshness matters more than with stronger materials, and there is no standardized grading ladder.

The quality factors:

1. **Freshness** — the soft lemony note is volatile and fades with age.
2. **Aroma** — a fresh, green, softly lemony character with no mustiness.
3. **Cleanliness** — clean, sound leaf material.
4. **Oil quality (for fragrance)** — a well-distilled oil with a clean citral/citronellal profile.

There is no standardized commercial grading ladder; freshness and the soft lemony-green character are the practical signals.

## Lemon balm vs lemon vs mint

> **Direct answer:** Lemon balm (香蜂草) is a mint-family herb that smells lemony through citral/citronellal; lemon (柠檬) is a rue-family citrus whose rind yields a bright, zesty oil; mint (薄荷) is a *Mentha* herb with a menthol-camphoraceous note. Three different plants, three different families, one shared "lemon" descriptor that can mislead.
