## Aroma profile

Silk tree flower has a delicate, sweet, slightly powdery floral scent — a soft, light perfume that suits its airy, pink, thread-like blossoms. It is gentler and more diffuse than the richer white florals like jasmine or osmanthus, reading as a quiet garden note. Its aromatic value is modest; the tree's fame rests more on its cultural meaning and its place in materia medica than on any strong fragrance.

## A cultural note (read this)

合欢 is famous in Chinese culture for its meaning, not its incense role:

- The name 合欢 means "collective joy" (also "conjoining joy"), and the tree is a symbol of harmony and reunion.
- The *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目), tree section (木之二), records 合欢 with the alternative names 合昏 and 夜合 (its leaves close at night), and quotes the classical line "合欢蠲忿，萱草忘忧" — 合欢 dispels anger, the daylily forgets sorrow.
- This is a **cultural and materia-medica association**, not a documented burned-incense use; the flower is not a staple of the 香乘 (Xiang Cheng) canon.

This page records 合欢花 honestly as a cultural and materia-medica flower, **without inventing a classical incense role.**

## Botanical source and origin

*Albizia julibrissin*, the silk tree or mimosa tree, is a leguminous tree (Fabaceae) native to a broad swath of Asia, widely planted for its feathery foliage and its pink, thread-like summer blossoms. Its leaves fold together at night, giving the classical names 合昏 and 夜合 ("night-closing"). Flora of China records the species.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Cultural symbol:** 合欢 is a symbol of harmony and joyful reunion; its name means "collective joy."
- **Materia medica:** 合欢 appears in traditional Chinese medicine. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**
- **The classical line:** the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草纲目) quotes "合欢蠲忿，萱草忘忧" — a cultural statement about calming the heart, not a fragrance claim.

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The cultural symbolism is well documented; the materia-medica record is **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the Fabaceae placement, and the cultural record.
