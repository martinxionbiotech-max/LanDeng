> **Direct answer:** Lemon (柠檬, níngméng) is the fruit and rind of *Citrus × limon*, a cultivated hybrid citrus in the rue family (Rutaceae). Its peel carries a bright, zesty, sweet-sour citrus scent from its essential oil, and it is a modern essential-oil fragrance material, not a classical Chinese incense ingredient. It must be kept distinct from the classical fragrance fruits citron, finger citron, and pomelo peel.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** 柠檬 (lemon) is bright, zesty, and sweet-sour, with the sharp, clean, instantly recognizable scent of fresh lemon peel. Its character comes from the oil in the rind, chiefly the compound limonene with citral.

## Botanical source and origin

> **Direct answer:** *Citrus × limon* is a small evergreen tree of the rue family (Rutaceae), a cultivated hybrid of the citron (*Citrus medica*) and the bitter orange (*Citrus × aurantium*). The fragrant part is the rind, whose oil glands hold the volatile essential oil, and it is widely cultivated but not a native Chinese wild species.

*Citrus × limon* is a small evergreen tree of the rue family (Rutaceae), a cultivated hybrid of the citron (*Citrus medica*) and the bitter orange (*Citrus × aurantium*). This distinguishes it sharply from the citron and finger citron, which are ancient fragrance fruits with long Chinese records.

## The lemon / lemon-balm distinction (read this)

They share a lemony scent from overlapping chemistry but are not related.

The "lemon" scent family has two common members that are easily confused:

## Why is lemon used in modern fragrance?

> **Direct answer:** Lemon is used for its bright, clean, zesty top note — cold-pressed from the peel, it "opens" a fragrance with a sharp citrus sparkle. It is a modern essential-oil material, usually blended, because its top note is volatile and fades quickly.

Lemon's role in modern fragrance is the **bright top note**. Cold-pressed lemon oil, taken from the peel's oil glands, provides the clean, sharp, instantly recognizable citrus opening that lifts a perfume, candle, or diffuser blend. Because the note is volatile and fleeting, lemon is almost always blended — its job is to "open" the scent with sparkle, after which the heart and base notes carry the fragrance. This is a modern essential-oil logic, entirely distinct from the classical 清供 display-fruit logic of the citron and finger citron.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Lemon's fragrant material is its essential oil, cold-pressed from the rind. The oil is the product — a volatile concentrate — distinct from the dried peel or juice, and cold-pressing (rather than distillation) is the standard method for citrus oils.

The processing for fragrance is **oil extraction**:

1. **Harvest and separate** — the rind (flavedo) is the fragrant part, separated from the juice and pith.
2. **Cold-press** — the rind's oil glands are ruptured mechanically to release the essential oil; cold-pressing (expression) is standard for citrus, preserving the fresh character that heat would alter.
3. **Separate** — the oil is separated from the juice and water.

The product is **lemon essential oil**, a volatile concentrate; it is a distinct material from the juice (a food) and from the dried peel (a spice). This oil-extraction logic — the same for all citrus — is what makes lemon a modern essential-oil crop.

## What determines quality?

> **Direct answer:** Lemon oil quality is a matter of freshness and chemical character: the bright top note is volatile, and oxidation dulls it. The chief quality signals are freshness, the limonene/citral balance, and freedom from adulteration (which is common in citrus oils).

The quality factors for lemon oil:

1. **Freshness** — the bright top note is volatile and oxidizes; a fresh oil is markedly brighter than a stale one.
2. **Chemical character** — the limonene/citral balance shapes the zesty, sweet-sour character.
3. **Adulteration** — citrus oils are commonly extended or adjusted; a clean oil is the baseline.

There is no classical grading ladder; freshness, chemistry, and freedom from adulteration are the practical signals, and the adulteration risk is real for a commodity oil like lemon.
