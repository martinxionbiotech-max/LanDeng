**Technical answer:** 薄荷 — *bòhé* in Chinese — is the aerial herb of *Mentha haplocalyx*, a perennial of the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to East Asia; modern botanical databases treat this name as a synonym of the broader *Mentha canadensis*. It is a cooling, minty, fresh aromatic from its menthol-rich essential oil, used in Chinese incense as a **cooling top note** and as a culinary herb. It is the "mint" of the Chinese pharmacopoeia, distinct in source from European peppermint.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Mint is cooling and fresh, with the unmistakable menthol character that gives it a crisp, penetrating, slightly sweet-green scent. It is a true top note — bright, volatile, instantly recognizable — that adds lift and freshness to a blend rather than body.

Mint is cooling and fresh, with the unmistakable menthol character that gives it its crisp, penetrating, slightly sweet-green scent. It is a *top note* in the truest sense — bright, volatile, and instantly recognizable. In a blend it adds a cooling lift and freshness, cutting through heavier, warmer notes rather than adding body. Its cooling character comes chiefly from the compound **menthol**.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** 薄荷 is *Mentha haplocalyx*, treated by modern botanical databases as a synonym of *Mentha canadensis* — a rhizomatous perennial mint in the Lamiaceae. It is a different plant from peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*) and spearmint (*Mentha spicata*), which share the family but not the species.

The Chinese pharmacopoeial mint is *Mentha haplocalyx*, a perennial herb of the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to East Asia. Modern botanical databases treat *Mentha haplocalyx* as a synonym of *Mentha canadensis*, a widely distributed northern-hemisphere mint — so the scientific label may appear either way. Flora of China records the plant as a **rhizomatous perennial with erect stems 30–60 cm tall**, flowering July–September, growing in wet areas from sea level to 3,500 m and recorded "in all provinces." It is a source of mint oil, historically used in cigarettes, pharmaceuticals, and oral preparations. See [Mentha canadensis in Flora of China](http://www.efloras.org/florataxon.aspx?flora_id=2&taxon_id=210001261).

The part used is the aerial herb — leaves and flowering tops — fresh or dried. The mint family also includes 紫苏 (perilla, *Perilla frutescens*) and 藿香 (agastache, *Agastache rugosa*), which share a green-herbal family character.

## Chinese name and terminology

> **Direct answer:** 薄荷 (bòhé) is the Chinese name for the East Asian mint of the pharmacopoeia; it is distinct in Chinese terminology from 留兰香 (spearmint) and 胡椒薄荷 (peppermint). The three mints carry separate Chinese names precisely because they are different plants.

The *Mentha* genus supplies three distinct mints, each with its own Chinese name:

- **薄荷 (bòhé, mint)** — *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *M. canadensis*), the East Asian pharmacopoeial mint, the "mint" of Chinese tradition. This entry.
- **留兰香 (liúlánxiāng, spearmint)** — *Mentha spicata*, the sweet, carvone-dominant mint. See [spearmint](/ingredients/spearmint/).
- **胡椒薄荷 (hújiāo bòhé, peppermint)** — *Mentha × piperita*, the sharp, menthol-dominant hybrid mint. See [peppermint](/ingredients/peppermint/).

Where English tends to lump these under "mint," Chinese keeps them separate — a useful guard against a mislabeled product.

## Where does mint come from?

> **Direct answer:** *Mentha canadensis* is a widespread East Asian and northern-hemisphere mint, recorded by Flora of China "in all provinces" and across East Asia into North America. The Chinese pharmacopoeial 薄荷 is this broadly distributed plant, not a narrowly regional crop.

*Mentha canadensis* is a widely distributed mint: Flora of China records it "in all provinces" of China, in wet areas from sea level to 3,500 m, with a range extending through Cambodia, Japan, Korea, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Russia, Thailand, and Vietnam, and into North America. This breadth is part of why the name *Mentha haplocalyx* has been folded into the broader *M. canadensis*: the East Asian mint is one regional face of a wide-ranging species.

## Why is it used in incense?

> **Direct answer:** Mint is used in incense as a cooling top note — it adds lift, freshness, and a clean minty character that cuts through heavier, warmer notes. It is used sparingly and usually blended, because its bright top note can dominate a formula if over-dosed.

Mint's role in incense follows directly from its aroma: as a bright, volatile top note it **lifts and cools** a blend, cutting through the weight of woods and resins. This is why it is used **sparingly and usually blended** — a small amount freshens a formula, while too much overpowers it. It is a classical cooling element in 合香 (blended incense) rather than a material burned alone.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Mint is used as the dried aerial herb — leaves and flowering tops — or distilled for mint essential oil. The dried herb needs only harvesting and drying; the oil is a separate steam-distilled product whose menthol content sets its cooling character.

Mint reaches use in two paths:

- **Dried herb** — the aerial parts are harvested and dried; the dried leaves and flowering tops are the culinary and incense form. No extraction step is involved.
- **Essential oil** — mint oil is steam-distilled from the herb; the menthol content sets its cooling, penetrating character, and the oil is a distinct product from the dried herb.

For powder buyers, particle size and moisture are the practical variables, but the exact target depends on the formula, binder, and production line (see Our Industry View below). The grinding and forming mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process.

## What determines mint quality?

> **Direct answer:** Freshness is the decisive variable — mint's volatile top note fades quickly once dried, so a fragrant, recently dried lot beats a stale one. After freshness, freedom from stems and debris and (for powder) consistent particle size drive quality.

The quality factors, in rough order:

1. **Freshness and volatility** — the menthol-rich top note fades with age and exposure; a crisp, fragrant lot is the priority.
2. **Cleanliness** — freedom from stems, woody debris, and foreign matter.
3. **Leaf-to-stem ratio** — the leaves carry the aroma; a leafy lot beats a stemmy one.
4. **For powder** — consistent particle size and moisture, judged against the specific formula.

There is **no standardized grading system**; the buyer evaluates freshness and physical condition of the lot.

## Common forms

> **Direct answer:** Mint is sold as fresh herb, dried leaves, and mint essential oil. The dried leaf is the culinary and incense form; the oil is a separate distilled product. Powder is ground from the dried leaf for blending.

## In Chinese tradition

- **Culinary herb:** 薄荷 is a staple fresh and dried culinary herb, used in teas, soups, and as a garnish across Chinese cooking.
- **Incense:** 薄荷 is used as a cooling, fresh top note in incense, adding lift and a clean minty character.
- **Materia medica:** 薄荷 is recorded in traditional Chinese medicine (本草綱目, 草部) with described uses. This is **traditional materia-medica language, not modern clinical evidence.**

## Traditional use vs modern evidence

The culinary and aromatic use is well documented. Medicinal uses are **traditional**, not established clinical fact, and we make **no medical claims**. The documented, non-medical facts are botanical identity, the *Mentha* genus placement, and the cooling minty aromatic use.

## Mint vs the other mints

> **Direct answer:** 薄荷 (mint, *M. haplocalyx*/syn. *M. canadensis*) is the East Asian pharmacopoeial mint; spearmint (*M. spicata*) is the sweet, carvone-dominant mint; peppermint (*M. × piperita*) is the sharp, menthol-dominant hybrid. They are three different plants sharing a minty family character.

| Mint | Botanical source | Aroma character |
|---|---|---|
| 薄荷 (mint) | *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *M. canadensis*) | Cooling, fresh, sweet-green; the Chinese pharmacopoeial mint |
| 留兰香 (spearmint) | *Mentha spicata* | Sweet, soft, carvone-dominant |
| 胡椒薄荷 (peppermint) | *Mentha × piperita* | Sharp, camphoraceous, menthol-dominant |

All three share a minty, menthol-adjacent character from the common family and chemistry, but they are distinct plants with distinct aromas. See [spearmint](/ingredients/spearmint/) and [peppermint](/ingredients/peppermint/).

## Adulteration & misidentification

> **Direct answer:** The main risk is misidentification under a shared "mint" name: 薄荷 (*M. haplocalyx*) is sometimes conflated with spearmint or peppermint. All are legitimate mints, but they differ in aroma and intended use, so the label should state the species.

Mint is not a heavily faked aromatic; its risk is **species confusion**. Because English "mint" covers several species while Chinese separates them (薄荷 / 留兰香 / 胡椒薄荷), a buyer who wants the pharmacopoeial 薄荷 should not unknowingly receive spearmint or peppermint — or vice versa. The species — not the word "mint" — sets the actual aroma and intended use, so the label should name the plant. See the [incense substitution cross-reference](/blog/incense-substitution-cross-reference/) for the general screening logic.

## Sustainability & sourcing

> **Direct answer:** Mint is a widely cultivated, fast-growing herb with no equivalent scarcity concern to the wild-harvested roots and woods; the sourcing question is freshness and species rather than conservation. Confirm the species and the drying date, since the top note fades.

Because *Mentha canadensis* is widespread and mint is easily cultivated, there is no CITES-style or wild-harvest scarcity concern of the kind attached to spikenard or agarwood. The sourcing question is therefore **freshness and identity**, not conservation: confirm which species is meant and how recently the material was dried, since the volatile top note is what the buyer is paying for. ## Modern commercial applications

> **Direct answer:** Mint is used as a culinary herb, in confectionery and oral products (as mint oil), and as a cooling top note in incense and fragrance. The oil channel (menthol) and the dried-herb channel are separate products with different price structures.

Mint reaches the market through distinct channels:

- **Culinary herb** — fresh and dried mint is a staple across cuisines.
- **Mint oil** — the distilled oil is a workhorse of confectionery, toothpaste, and oral-care products, carried by its menthol content.
- **Incense and fragrance** — the dried herb and oil are used as a cooling, fresh top note.

The oil and dried-herb markets are separate; a sourcing buyer should be clear which one they are buying.

## What buyers should look for

> **Direct answer:** Buyers should pin down the species (薄荷 vs spearmint vs peppermint), then judge freshness — a crisp menthol scent and a recent drying date — and, for powder, confirm particle size and moisture against the formula. The top note is volatile, so freshness is the decisive variable.

For an incense maker or sourcing buyer:

- **Species** — confirm *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *M. canadensis*) rather than spearmint or peppermint; a real seller names the species.
- **Freshness** — the menthol top note fades with age; ask about the harvest/drying date and check the scent on arrival.
- **Form and cleanliness** — leafy, stem-free dried herb, or powder; each behaves differently in a blend.
- **Powder consistency** — confirm particle size and moisture, and test a sample against your formula.

## Safety

Incense combustion produces smoke and particulate matter. Burn in a well-ventilated space, avoid inhaling concentrated smoke, and keep away from children and pets. Mint's menthol can be intensely cooling; use sparingly. This is editorial knowledge content, not medical advice. See our [safety guide](/safety/).

## Our Industry View

> **Direct answer:** In manufacturing, mint's value and its constraint are the same thing: volatility. A bright top note lifts a blend but fades in storage and can dominate if over-dosed, so the practical discipline is freshness control, species verification, and careful dosing — not the nominal "mint" name.

From a manufacturing perspective, mint is a **top-note problem more than a material problem**. Its entire value is a volatile, cooling character that is exactly the part most vulnerable to age, heat, and grinding — so a formula that depends on mint's lift must control freshness and dosage more tightly than a formula built on a stable wood or resin.

Two points follow:

- **Freshness is the specification.** Because menthol and the other volatiles fade, a "mint" that smelled right on the supplier's shelf may not smell right after storage. The practical specification is the drying date and a freshness check on arrival, not a grade name.
- **Dosage is the discipline.** As a bright top note, mint can dominate a blend if over-dosed and vanish if over-diluted. The right proportion **depends on formulation** — the other ingredients, the intended strength, and the production line — so a fixed percentage does not transfer between recipes.

In our view, the commercial sweet spot is a **named species, recently dried, used sparingly** — a precise top-note accent, not a bulk filler.

## Summary

Mint (薄荷, *bòhé*) is the aerial herb of *Mentha haplocalyx* (syn. *Mentha canadensis*), a rhizomatous perennial mint of the Lamiaceae and the "mint" of the Chinese pharmacopoeia. It is valued for a cooling, fresh, menthol-driven top note used as a culinary herb and as a sparing, bright accent in blended incense. Its quality rests on freshness — the volatile top note fades with age — and its naming risk is confusion with spearmint (留兰香) and peppermint (胡椒薄荷), two different mints. In incense it is a precision top-note modifier; commercially, species verification, freshness, and careful dosing outweigh the nominal "mint" name.

Peppermint is *Mentha × piperita* and spearmint is *Mentha spicata* — different mints of the same family. They share a minty, menthol character, but 薄荷 is the source used in the Chinese tradition. The shared character reflects the common family and chemistry, not the same plant.

**What does mint smell like?** Cooling and fresh, with the unmistakable menthol character that gives it a crisp, penetrating, slightly sweet-green scent. It is a true top note — bright, volatile, and instantly recognizable. In a blend it adds a cooling lift and freshness, cutting through heavier, warmer notes rather than adding body.

**Why is mint described as "cooling"?** Because of menthol, the dominant compound in its essential oil, which triggers the sensation of coolness on skin and mucous membranes. This cooling character is sensory — a perception, not a temperature change — and it is what gives mint its crisp, fresh, penetrating quality in both flavor and fragrance.

**How is 薄荷 used in Chinese tradition?** Primarily as a staple culinary herb — fresh and dried in teas, soups, and as a garnish — and as a cooling, fresh top note in incense. **Why does dried mint fade in scent?** Because mint's aroma is carried by volatile compounds — chiefly menthol and related constituents — that evaporate and oxidize over time once the herb is dried and exposed to air. Freshness is therefore the decisive quality variable for mint: a recently dried lot keeps its crisp top note, while an old one loses exactly the character the buyer is paying for.
