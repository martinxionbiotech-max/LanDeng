> **Direct answer:** Rosemary (迷迭香, mídiéxiāng) is the aromatic herb *Salvia rosmarinus* (long known as *Rosmarinus officinalis*), an evergreen shrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae) native to the Mediterranean. It is a herbal, camphoraceous, fresh aromatic used in cooking and fragrance, and in Chinese incense as a herbal accent. The 香乘 records it as an exotic Western import introduced to the court in the third century.

## Aroma profile

> **Direct answer:** Rosemary is herbal and camphoraceous, with a fresh, piney, slightly woody sharpness and a clean resinous lift. In a blend it reads as a fresh herbal top note.

## Botanical identity

> **Direct answer:** Rosemary is an evergreen shrub of the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to the Mediterranean. It is not a Chinese native and has no Flora of China treatment.

## The 迷迭香 classical record (read this)

> **Direct answer:** Rosemary has a genuine but narrow classical Chinese footprint. The 香乘 records 迷迭香, quoting 广志 (出西域) and 魏略 (出大秦国), noting it was transplanted to the 魏文帝 (Cao Pi) court — an imported aromatic worn to scent clothing, not a native incense-canon material.

Rosemary has a genuine — but narrow — classical Chinese footprint:

- **香乘 entry:** 香乘 records 迷迭香, quoting 广志 (出西域) and 魏略 (出大秦国), and noting it was transplanted to the imperial court in the 魏文帝 (Cao Pi) period — described as an imported aromatic "可佩服令人衣香" (worn to scent clothing), not as a native incense-canon material.
- **Western import, not native:** the record frames rosemary as a curiosity from the Western Regions / 大秦 (the Roman sphere), introduced to China around the third century.
- **Modern crossover:** today rosemary's incense and fragrance use is a Western-herbal crossover, not a continuation of a core 香道 tradition.

The significance of this record is that rosemary is a **documented early import** — proof that a third-century Chinese court took note of a Mediterranean aromatic — but it never entered the recipe canon the way 甘松, 乳香, or 木香 did.

## How is it processed?

> **Direct answer:** Rosemary is used as the dried leaf, harvested and dried, then kept whole for slow release or ground to powder for blending. The leaves are also steam-distilled for rosemary essential oil, a separate, concentrated product with a stronger camphoraceous-piney character.

The part used is the leaf. Processing is simple:

- **Dried leaf** — whole or lightly crushed, for sachets and slow release.
- **Powder** — ground leaf for blending into sticks and cones; particle size and moisture affect how the powder packs and burns.
- **Essential oil** — steam-distilled rosemary oil, a distinct, concentrated product with a stronger camphoraceous-piney character.

The grinding and forming mechanics sit within the wider [how incense is made](/blog/how-incense-is-made/) process.

## What determines rosemary quality?

> **Direct answer:** Quality turns on freshness and oil content: a good rosemary is green, intact, and strongly camphoraceous-piney when bruised or warmed. Old, faded, or shattered leaf loses much of its essential oil and reads flat. The taxonomy shift (*Rosmarinus* → *Salvia*) is a naming point, not a quality signal.

The practical quality points, in rough order:

1. **Freshness and oil content** — a good leaf releases a strong camphoraceous-piney aroma when bruised; old leaf fades.
2. **Leaf integrity and color** — green, intact leaf beats brown, shattered material.
3. **Cleanliness** — freedom from stems, dust, and foreign leaf.
4. **Species note** — *Salvia rosmarinus* / *Rosmarinus officinalis* are one plant; the name change is taxonomy, not a grade.

Rosemary carries no standardized grading ladder — it is an abundant culinary herb — so quality means freshness, oil content, and integrity.
