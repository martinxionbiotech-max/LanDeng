# Plant Sources of Classic Incense Scents: A Botanical Map

**Direct answer:** Every classic incense scent traces to a specific plant part — a **wood** (sandalwood, agarwood), a **resin** (frankincense, myrrh), a **flower** (jasmine, rose), a **peel** (citrus), or a **bud/bark** (clove, cassia). Knowing the plant source tells you what kind of material — and therefore what kind of scent and burn — you are getting.

**Key facts:** The source part matters: wood and resin burn slowly and carry depth; flowers are delicate and are usually rendered as fragrance accords; peels and buds are bright and spicy.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Each classic scent maps to a **specific plant part** — wood, resin, flower, peel, or bud/bark.
- **Woods and resins** are the classical backbone (slow, deep).
- **Flowers** are usually **fragrance accords**, because raw flowers burn poorly.
- Knowing the source = knowing the likely scent character and burn.

## What the Part Tells You

- **Heartwood** (sandalwood, agarwood) — dense, slow-burning, deep and persistent.
- **Resin** (frankincense, myrrh, benzoin) — bright-to-balsamic, burned as tears or chips.
- **Flower** (jasmine, rose, lotus) — delicate, usually a fragrance accord in sticks (see the [lotus](/lotus-incense/) and [lavender](/lavender-incense/) guides).
- **Peel / bud / bark** (citrus, clove, cassia) — bright or spicy top notes, usually blend components.

---

## Why This Matters

The plant source is the single most reliable predictor of what you are buying:

- "Sandalwood" or "agarwood" points at a **material tradition**.
- "Jasmine," "rose," or "lotus" usually points at a **fragrance accord**.
- "Citrus" or "clove" points at a **blend component**.

Read the label with this map in mind, and a lot of the mystery in incense buying disappears.

## FAQ

### Q: Where does the sandalwood scent come from?
A: The heartwood of *Santalum album* (and related species), not the flowers or leaves.

### Q: Are flower scents real in incense?
A: Usually not — raw flowers burn poorly, so most floral incense is a fragrance accord. See the [lotus guide](/lotus-incense/).

### Q: What part of the plant is frankincense?
A: A gum-resin tapped from the bark of *Boswellia* trees — not a wood or flower.

### Q: Why does the plant part matter?
A: The part (wood vs resin vs flower vs peel) determines the scent character and how it burns — deep and slow for wood/resin, bright and delicate for flowers.

## People Also Ask / 长尾问答

### Q: Which plant parts burn best in incense?
Woods and resins burn (or heat) slowly and carry deep, persistent scent, so they are the classical backbone. Raw flowers burn poorly, which is why florals are usually fragrance accords. Peels, buds, and barks are bright or spicy top notes, usually blend components.

### Q: Is the lotus in lotus incense a real flower?
Usually not — lotus has a subtle, aquatic scent that does not survive burning, so most "lotus" incense is a fragrance accord. See the [lotus guide](/lotus-incense/).

### Q: Why is makko listed as a plant source?
Makko (*Machilus thunbergii* bark) is a functional ingredient — the combustible binder — not a scent. It is listed because it is the one plant material in incense whose job is structure, not fragrance. See [makko & natural binders](/makko-natural-binders/).
