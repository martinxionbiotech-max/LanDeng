# Agarwood Grading: 沉香, 沉水, and 奇楠 — the Definitive Reference

**Direct answer:** Agarwood (沉香 chénxiāng) is graded primarily by **resin density** — expressed in the 沉水 (sink-in-water) system — and by the **奇楠 (qínán) distinction**, which separates the top "oil-wraps-wood" grade from ordinary "wood-wraps-oil" agarwood. The five-color 奇楠 hierarchy (白/绿/紫/黄/黑) is a further, traditional subdivision.

**Key facts:** This is the deep grading reference; for the broader "what is agarwood" and aroma material, see the [agarwood scent guide](/agarwood-incense/).

---

## Key Takeaways

- **沉香 = "sinking fragrance"** — the name encodes the primary grading method.
- The **沉水 system** grades by whether the material sinks, half-sinks, or floats in water.
- **奇楠 (qínán / "kyara")** is the top grade, distinguished by **"oil wraps wood"** vs ordinary 沉香's **"wood wraps oil."**
- The **five-color 奇楠** hierarchy (white/green/purple/yellow/black) is traditional — and **sources disagree** on whether some colors are distinct grades or aging stages.
- The **etymology of 奇楠 is genuinely uncertain**; this page does not assert a single origin.

---

## The 沉水 (Sinking) System

The name 沉香 literally means "sinking fragrance," and the primary grading method is physical: does the piece sink in water?

| Grade | Behavior | Resin content |
|---|---|---|
| 沉水 (sinks) | Fully submerged | Highest, most valuable |
| 半沉半浮 (half-sinks) | Partially submerged | Medium |
| 浮 (floats) | Floats | Lower (historically 黄熟香) |

The logic is direct: resin is denser than wood, so the most resin-saturated pieces sink. This is the backbone of the Chinese grading system — and it is surprisingly rarely explained clearly in English.

---

## 奇楠 vs. Ordinary Agarwood (the Distinction English Misses)

The single most important grading distinction is between ordinary 沉香 and the top grade, **奇楠 (qínán**, Japanese "kyara"**)**:

- **Ordinary 沉香 = "wood wraps oil" (木包油).** Hard wood with resin veins running through it.
- **奇楠 = "oil wraps wood" (油包木).** The wood fiber is so thoroughly resin-transformed that the material becomes **soft and pliable** — it "shaves into curls and kneads into pellets" (削之成卷、捏之成丸) and is **aromatic even at room temperature**.

This is not a small difference; it is a different material state. The traditional description is worth learning because it is the one piece of the system English content almost always omits.

---

## The Five-Color 奇楠 Hierarchy (Traditional)

奇楠 is further divided by color, traditionally into five grades:

| Color | Chinese | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 白 (white) | 白奇楠 | Highest |
| 绿 (green) | 绿奇楠 / 莺歌绿 | Widely regarded top tier |
| 紫 (purple) | 紫奇楠 / 兰花结 | Disputed — distinct grade or aging stage? |
| 黄 (yellow) | 黄奇楠 / 金丝结 | Disputed — distinct grade or aging stage? |
| 黑 (black) | 黑奇楠 / 铁结 | Lower |

**Important caveat:** sources disagree on whether the purple and yellow grades are **distinct grades** or **aging stages of the green grade.** This page treats the hierarchy as traditional and possibly non-distinct, rather than settled fact.

## Formation Types

How the resin formed also factors into grading and naming:

- **熟结** — resin from a dead tree, buried or aged.
- **生结** — resin from a living tree.
- **虫漏** — resin formed around insect boring.
- **脱落** — resin from an external wound or break.

These describe *how* the agarwood came to be, and interact with the density and 奇楠 distinctions above.

## Etymology: An Honest "Uncertain"

The origin of the name 奇楠 is genuinely disputed. Theories include:

1. Sanskrit **Kāla** (काल, "black/time").
2. Sanskrit **tagara**, "a fragrant plant," shortened to 伽罗/伽楠.
3. A **"temple" (伽蓝)** reading.

More rigorous 考证 argues that **tagara is a mistranslation** — the Sanskrit word for agarwood is **agaru / aguru**, not tagara.

**The honest position: "etymology uncertain; multiple theories."** This page does not pick one.

## The Author's Take

I'll give you my honest view, not just the reference facts: the 沉水 sink test is simultaneously the most useful and the most overrated grading signal in agarwood. It is useful because it is objective — either the piece sinks or it does not, no argument. It is overrated because oil-injection fakes are *engineered specifically to sink*. If you grade by the sink test alone, you are not grading the wood; you are grading the counterfeiter's technique.

In my experience, the better first question is not "does it sink?" but "how does it smell when gently heated, and who is selling it to me?" A half-sinking piece whose warm aroma I have actually enjoyed is worth more to me than a "sinking" piece I cannot verify. The sink test is a useful tiebreaker — never the whole story.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the difference between 沉香 and 奇楠?
A: Ordinary 沉香 is "wood wraps oil" (hard wood with resin veins); 奇楠 is "oil wraps wood" (soft, pliable, aromatic at room temperature). 奇楠 is the top grade.

### Q: How is agarwood graded?
A: Primarily by resin density via the 沉水 (sink-in-water) system, then by the 奇楠 distinction, then (traditionally) by the five-color hierarchy.

### Q: What does "sinking fragrance" mean?
A: 沉香 literally means "sinking fragrance" — the finest resin-dense pieces sink in water, which is the primary grading method.

### Q: Is the five-color 奇楠 hierarchy settled?
A: No — sources disagree on whether purple and yellow are distinct grades or aging stages of green. Treat it as traditional.

### Q: Where does the word 奇楠 come from?
A: It is genuinely uncertain — competing theories include Sanskrit "Kāla," "tagara" (possibly a mistranslation), and a "temple" reading.
